Refractory perirenal effusion in a pediatric patient with antithrombin III deficiency and KILT syndrome: a case report

[b00001] Abstract
Congenital antithrombin III (ATIII) deficiency is a rare hereditary thrombophilia that is caused by SERPINC1 variants. Reports on KILT syndrome, which is defined by the triad of kidney anomaly, inferior vena cava (IVC) anomaly, and leg thrombosis, have been sporadic. We described the case of a 10-year-old boy with a solitary left kidney and IVC agenesis who carried compound heterozygous variants in SERPINC1. He presented with flank pain due to renal infarction and subsequently developed massive perirenal effusion. Despite anticoagulation and prolonged percutaneous drainage, the effusion persisted and was confirmed to be of lymphatic origin. Imaging revealed abnormal lymphatic flow related to venous hypertension in the renal hilum. Complete resolution of the effusion was achieved following four sessions of ethanol sclerotherapy. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first reported case of ATIII deficiency complicated by refractory perirenal lymphatic leakage. The case suggested a potential link between thrombosis and secondary lymphatic dysfunction and demonstrated the effectiveness of repeated sclerotherapy for refractory perirenal effusion. Early recognition of hypercoagulable states in children with IVC and renal anomalies is crucial for the prevention of thromboembolic events.

[b00002] Introduction
Congenital antithrombin III (ATIII) deficiency is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by reduced ATIII levels (type I) or impaired function (type II), leading to inadequate inhibition of key clotting factors, such as thrombin and factor X. Pathogenic variants in the SERPINC1 gene result in decreased synthesis or diminished activity of ATIII, predisposing affected individuals to venous or, less commonly, arterial thrombosis [].

[b00003] Introduction
KILT syndrome is a rare triad of kidney abnormalities, inferior vena cava (IVC) anomalies, and leg thrombosis. After its initial recognition by Van Veen et al. in 2002, little is known regarding its epidemiology, natural history, and underlying mechanism []. Here, we report a pediatric patient who manifested with features of KILT (kidney and IVC abnormalities with leg thrombosis) syndrome in association with ATIII deficiency and developed refractory perirenal effusion, a previously unreported complication in the context of KILT syndrome or ATIII deficiency. We reviewed available literature on KILT syndrome and discussed the implications of our case on pathophysiology of KILT syndrome.

[b00004] Case report
A 10-year-old boy presented to the emergency room with severe left flank pain. He had been diagnosed with a solitary left kidney and IVC agenesis at 4 months of age and has had superficial abdominal varices since birth. At 9 years of age, clinical findings and imaging studies led to the suspicion of KILT syndrome. At that time, laboratory evaluation showed reduced ATIII activity at 56% to 60% (normal range, 82% to 126%). Sanger sequencing confirmed the diagnosis of ATIII deficiency, based on the identification of the following compound heterozygous variants in the SERPINC1 gene: c.235C>T, p.Arg79Cys from his mother, pathogenic [] and c.1277C>T, p.Ser426Leu from his father, pathogenic []. At the emergency department, abdominal computed tomography revealed a left perirenal hematoma (Fig. 1) with renal infarction. His kidney function was within normal range, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 92.26 mL/min/1.73 m2 (calculated using the creatinine-based bedside Schwartz equation) without proteinuria (urine protein/creatinine ratio 0.08 mg/mg) nor hematuria. The patient was admitted to the hospital. He underwent percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD), which initially obtained approximately 3 L of fluid daily, in combination with anticoagulation therapy with warfarin and enoxaparin and ATIII supplementation. Contrary to our expectation, the drainage volume did not decrease, and its character changed from bloody to straw colored with slight turbidity. The drained fluid was predominantly composed of lymphocytes (76%) with 3% polymorphonuclear cells, and biochemical analysis revealed urea nitrogen of 13 mg/dL, creatinine of 0.27 mg/dL, triglyceride <7 mg/dL, glucose 99 mg/dL, protein 0.1 g/dL, and lactate dehydrogenase 33 U/L. These findings were consistent with non-chylous lymphatic leakage. Magnetic resonance lymphangiography demonstrated abnormal venous pressure in the renal hilum, which led to lymphatic fluid accumulation between the renal fascia and renal capsule (Fig. 2). Over time, the initial hemorrhage was absorbed, creating an abnormal space that allowed lymphatic fluid accumulation and persistent perirenal lymphatic effusion. Ethanol sclerotherapy was performed. The drainage volume remained unchanged after the first session but significantly decreased after a total of four sessions over a month. Follow-up imaging clearly showed a decrease in fluid collection, and his symptoms improved. The percutaneous drain was subsequently removed, and the patient was discharged. On outpatient follow-up at 6 months after discharge, the boy remained stable on warfarin without evidence of thromboembolism or hemorrhage. His kidney function was stable, with an eGFR of 81.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 without proteinuria (urine protein/creatinine ratio 0.09 mg/mg). Comparison of kidney ultrasound 18 months before showed less prominent left kidney enlargement from 14.3 cm to 13.1 cm, without evidence of perirenal fluid.

[b00005] Discussion
Congenital ATIII deficiency is a rare genetic disorder characterized by recurrent venous or arterial thromboembolism, most often involving the deep veins of the legs and arms. Pulmonary embolism is also a recognized complication, and in rare cases, thrombosis may involve the renal veins []. ATIII deficiency is typically inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, because homozygous pathogenic variants, especially type I deficiency, which lead to markedly reduced ATIII levels, are believed to be fatal in utero []. However, some have reported individuals with compound heterozygous variants in SERPINC1 [], which was also seen in our patient, affecting the heparin-binding (c.235C>T, p.Arg79Cys) and reactive (c.1277C>T, p.Ser426Leu) sites [].

[b00006] Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this was the first reported case of KILT syndrome associated with ATIII deficiency. A previous report described a familial case with ATIII deficiency and IVC agenesis but without kidney abnormality; in that patient, a single pathogenic variant in SERPINC1 was identified within the heparin-binding domain []. A recent systematic review summarized 29 reported cases of KILT syndrome []. Of 20 patients who underwent thrombophilia testing, 20% showed abnormalities, such as factor V Leiden or MTHFR polymorphism. These findings supported the hypothesis that inherited or acquired prothrombotic conditions may contribute to the development of KILT syndrome. Our patient has not yet developed leg thrombosis, although among individuals with ATIII deficiency, venous thromboembolism is uncommon in the first two decades of life, after which the risk increases significantly []. Therefore, given his young age, our patient was considered to have manifestations that were consistent with KILT syndrome. This diagnosis was supported by a previous systematic review, which categorized patients with kidney and IVC abnormalities but no lower limb thrombosis as compatible with KILT syndrome or pseudo-KILT [].

[b00007] Discussion
IVC agenesis is believed to result from intrauterine or perinatal venous thrombosis []. Absence of IVC impairs venous drainage from the right metanephros and potentially disrupts normal development of the right kidney, resulting in agenesis. This proposed mechanism is less applicable to left kidney agenesis, because the left metanephros has an alternative drainage system that can compensate for an absent IVC []. Interestingly, in the aforementioned systematic review on KILT syndrome, kidney anomalies were observed to have nearly equal distribution between the right and left sides, with slightly higher prevalence on the left (44.8% vs. 51.7%) []. Therefore, although impaired venous drainage may explain right kidney agenesis in some patients, other mechanisms are likely involved in the pathogenesis of kidney anomalies in KILT syndrome.

[b00008] Discussion
Taken together, considering that agenesis of both IVC and kidney may result from perinatal thrombosis, the possibility of an underlying hypercoagulable state and, consequently, KILT syndrome should be considered in infants and children with both IVC and unilateral kidney agenesis. Early recognition is important, because timely initiation of long-term anticoagulation can reduce the risk of thromboembolic complications.

[b00009] Discussion
Although a direct link between ATIII deficiency and lymphatic leakage has not been described in literature, plausible explanations can be proposed. First, various stimuli, including necrotic cells, infection, and lymphatic endothelial damage, can activate the coagulation cascade and cause lymphatic thrombosis. In a hypercoagulable state, recurrent lymphatic thromboses and subsequent lymphatic flow disturbance may contribute to lymphatic leakage []. Second, venous thrombosis in the kidney may increase hydrostatic pressure in the renal venous system, which, in turn, increases lymphatic pressure and may ultimately result in persistent lymphatic leakage into the perirenal space. This hypothesis is supported by previous reports on renal lymphangiectasia following renal vein thrombosis [,].

[b00010] Discussion
Although no report has described an identical case, a previous report on renal lymphangiomatosis with perirenal cystic masses suggested that PCD combined with repeated sclerotherapy may be effective []. In the context of lymphocele after kidney transplantation, simple aspiration alone carried a recurrence rate as high as 90%, whereas repeated sclerotherapy achieved a success rate of up to 92% without recurrence []. This evidence supports the use of repeated sclerotherapy as a safe and effective treatment approach for persistent perirenal lymphatic leakage unresponsive to drainage.

[b00011] Discussion
This case illustrates a unique presentation of refractory perirenal effusion in the context of KILT syndrome associated with ATIII deficiency, successfully managed with repeated sclerotherapy. Our case sheds light on the still obscure pathophysiology of KILT syndrome and suggests an effective treatment option for this rare complication. Further accumulation of similar cases may help establish appropriate therapeutic strategies.
