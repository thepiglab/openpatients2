Successful Pneumatic Retinopexy with Strict Positioning for Persistent Retinal Detachment after Scleral Encircling Buckle: A Case Report

[b00001] None
Dear Editor,

[b00002] None
Pneumatic retinopexy (PR) is sutureless retinal reattachment technique that involves intravitreal gas injection combined with retinal tear sealing by retinopexy. There have been few studies on PR for persistent or recurrent retinal detachment (RD) after scleral buckling [–]. Anatomical success rates varied, ranging from 53% to 100%. We report a case of successful PR with strict positioning for persistent RD after scleral encircling. Written informed consent for publication of the research details and clinical images was obtained from the patient.

[b00003] None
A 27-year-old male patient presented with visual field defect in his right eye that had started 2 days ago. He had history of bilateral barrier laser photocoagulation and implantable collamer lens (ICLs) insertion. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was counting fingers at 50 cm. Anterior segment was unremarkable. Fundus showed macular-off rhegmatogenous RD extending from 8 to 1 o’clock (Fig. 1A, 1B). There was a horseshoe tear at 8.5 o’clock periphery, lattice degeneration (LD) with multiple atrophic holes at 10.5–12 o’clock periphery. Previous laser scars were found at the 12.5–1.5 and 5–7.5 o’clock periphery. On the same day, encircling was performed. Cryotherapy was applied to 8.5 o’clock tear, 13 mm from the limbus. One week after surgery, subretinal fluid (SRF) significantly decreased, with minimal SRF remaining at the macula and some residual SRF around the tear (Fig. 1C–1E). The tear at the 8.5 o’clock position was partially supported by the encircling band, whereas superior LD with multiple holes was well supported over the band. Three weeks after surgery, SRF around the tear increased again (Fig. 1F–1H). On the same day, intravitreal injection of 100% perfluoropropane gas (C_(3)F_(8), 0.35 mL) was performed. To prevent SRF from shifting toward fovea, patient was instructed to maintain prone position during the day and left side down position while sleeping. Four days after gas injection (postoperative day 25), SRF around the tear decreased (Fig. 1I–1K), and barrier laser photocoagulation was performed around the tear on the same day. Five weeks after surgery, temporal SRF increased (Fig. 1L–1N). A second injection of pure C_(3)F_(8) gas (0.4 mL) was performed, and patient was instructed to strictly maintain left side down position. One week later, at 6 weeks postoperatively, temporal SRF resolved (Fig. 1O–1Q), and additional laser photocoagulation was applied around the tear on the same day. Two months after surgery, retina remained attached. SRF bleb remained at the macula, but none was connected to the tear (Fig. 1R–1T). At 3 months postoperatively, there was no detachment, and no new breaks were found (Fig. 1U–1W). BCVA was 20 / 30.

[b00004] None
Reports on PR for persistent RD following scleral encircling are rare. In previous studies, success rate of PR following encircling varied, ranging from 66.7% (2 of 3 eyes) [] to 88.9% (8 of 9) [] and 100% (15 of 15) []. Because 8.5 o’clock tear was positioned partially over encircling band in present case, residual traction was not considered significant. Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) was absent. Therefore, we anticipated successful reattachment with PR. Although superior LD with atrophic holes was well supported by encircling band, it might have shifted off the band during revision. Therefore, PR was preferred over revision. As the patient had ICL, vitrectomy was avoided due to concerns about cataract progression.

[b00005] None
After first PR, prone and left side down positioning were alternated to prevent shifting of SRF and retina was reattached. However recurrent RD developed around the tear after first PR. After second PR, strict left side down positioning was maintained, resulting in successful reattachment. A larger gas volume might have provided more consistent coverage of tear, even with alternate positioning, thereby preventing redetachment. However, considering that gas volume gradually decreases, strict positioning is more important to keep tear insulated from liquid currents and to reduce residual traction around tear for successful reattachment.

[b00006] None
Reported success rate of PR following encircling varies from 66.7% to 100%. Therefore, it would be inappropriate to generalize successful outcome observed in the present case to all cases. Jung et al. [] demonstrated that PVR grade B and inferior tears are risk factors for PR failure. Thus, careful case selection is crucial when considering PR after encircling.

[b00007] None
In conclusion, PR can be a good alternative for persistent RD caused by incomplete tear coverage after encircling in selected cases. Strict positioning to keep tears insulated from SRF current is crucial for successful PR.
