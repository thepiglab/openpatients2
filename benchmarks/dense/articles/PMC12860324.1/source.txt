Postoperative meningitis due to carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae: A case report and literature review

[b00001] Abstract / Background:
Postoperative meningitis is a serious complication in neurosurgery. Early diagnosis and appropriate management are essential to reduce its significant morbidity and mortality.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Description:
We report the case of a 76-year-old patient who underwent evacuation of a posterior fossa hematoma. On postoperative day 19, he presented with fever and surgical wound necrosis. Surgical revision revealed a collection fistulized to the skin. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis and intraoperative samples confirmed postoperative meningitis caused by multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, susceptible only to colistin and fosfomycin. Despite treatment adjustment, the patient’s condition worsened, and he died 12 days after surgical revision due to massive pulmonary embolism.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion:
This case highlights the severity of postoperative meningitis due to extensively drug-resistant K. pneumoniae and underlines the importance of early diagnosis, bacteriological analysis of surgical samples, and rapid adaptation of antibiotic therapy in specialized settings.

[b00004] INTRODUCTION
Postoperative meningitis is a dreaded complication of neurosurgical procedures, associated with high morbidity and mortality. Its occurrence is favored by the presence of several risk factors, including a fragile medical condition (such as diabetes or immunosuppression), emergency surgery, a dural breach, the presence of foreign material, or surgical site infection. While coagulase-negative staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus are the most commonly implicated pathogens, the involvement of Gram-negative bacilli such as Klebsiella pneumoniae has become increasingly concerning due to the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains.[,]

[b00005] INTRODUCTION
We report here a case of postoperative meningitis caused by an extensively drug-resistant (XDR) K. pneumoniae following posterior fossa surgery, with an unfavorable outcome despite appropriate treatment, complicated by a fatal pulmonary thromboembolic event.

[b00006] CASE PRESENTATION
We report the case of a 76-year-old male patient with a medical history of high blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, and type 2 diabetes, who had undergone a suboccipital craniectomy for evacuation of a posterior fossa hematoma. The procedure was uneventful, with simple postoperative recovery and discharge to home.

[b00007] CASE PRESENTATION
The patient was readmitted 19 days later with a fever of 39°C persisting for 3 days, associated with meningeal signs including neck stiffness and photophobia and altered level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10: Eye response = 3, Verbal response = 3, Motor response = 4).

[b00008] CASE PRESENTATION
Physical examination revealed necrosis of the upper third of the surgical wound, with purulent discharge. Laboratory tests showed a marked inflammatory response with leukocytosis at 12,000/mm^(3) (90% neutrophils) (normal [Nl] under 10000/mm^(3)), C-reactive protein at 86 mg/L (Nl <2 mg/L), and procalcitonin at 2 ng/mL (Nl <0.5 ng/mL).

[b00009] CASE PRESENTATION
An urgent brain computed tomography (CT) scan was performed, revealing a fluid collection in the process of organization within the soft tissues adjacent to the suboccipital craniectomy site, with a fistulous tract to the skin [Figure 1].

[b00010] CASE PRESENTATION
A lumbar puncture revealed 28 cells/mm^(3) (80% neutrophils), a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to blood glucose ratio of 0.11, a protein level in the CSF of 6.98 g/L (Nl <1 g/L), and a lactate level in the CSF of 14 mmol/L (Nl <4 mmol/L), consistent with bacterial meningitis. Blood cultures obtained at the onset of fever were negative.

[b00011] CASE PRESENTATION
The patient was urgently taken to the operating room for wound debridement and skin closure. Empirical antibiotic therapy targeting meningitis was initiated, combining ceftazidime (150 mg/kg/day) and linezolid (600 mg every 12 h). He was transferred to the neurosurgical intensive care unit, intubated, mechanically ventilated, and sedated, with monitoring of sedation depth using bispectral index (BIS) and cerebral perfusion monitoring with transcranial Doppler.

[b00012] CASE PRESENTATION
Sedation was stopped 24 h later, with the patient being afebrile and showing good cerebral perfusion. However, no signs of awakening were observed after 48 h, with BIS values fluctuating between 40 and 50. Five days after surgery, we received cultures results: intraoperative samples were positive for K. pneumoniae producing carbapenemase (likely KPC), showing sensitivity only to colistin and fosfomycin [Table 1].

[b00014] CASE PRESENTATION
Note that the patient had not received any antibiotics prior to surgery and had no recent history of infections. However, several risk factors for MDR infections were present, including advanced age, recent neurosurgical intervention, and prolonged hospitalization in a neurosurgical ward. These factors likely contributed to the development of a nosocomial infection with an XDR organism. On admission, the patient was screened for MDR organisms as per hospital protocol. No colonization with carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae or other multidrug resistant bacteria was detected. This suggests that the subsequent infection was acquired during the hospital stay.

[b00015] CASE PRESENTATION
The treatment was modified with the introduction of colistin (9 million units loading dose, followed by 4.5 million units every 12 h) and fosfomycin (4 g every 6 h). Neurological improvement was observed 48 h after changing the treatment (BIS between 60 and 70, localized motor response to pain, eye opening to stimulation), corresponding to the 6^(th) postoperative day. The clinical course was marked by stagnation of neurological status, leading to a new lumbar puncture on postoperative day 8, which showed sterilization of the CSF. Follow-up CT scan confirmed the disappearance of the previously described collection, with no signs of intracranial complications or cerebral herniation.

[b00016] CASE PRESENTATION
On postoperative day 12, the patient’s condition suddenly worsened with acute respiratory distress and hemodynamic collapse. The diagnosis of massive pulmonary embolism was made based on clinical signs and cardiac ultrasound despite early initiated pharmacological prophylaxis for venous thromboembolism (postoperative day 1) with subcutaneous enoxaparin 40 mg daily, and the patient died shortly afterward despite resuscitation efforts.

[b00017] DISCUSSION
Postoperative meningitis is a serious complication of neurosurgical procedures, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.3% to 8.6%, depending on the type of surgical intervention, surgery duration, and the use of invasive devices.[,] Several risk factors have been identified, including repetitive surgeries, CSF leakage, and fistulized subcutaneous collections, as observed in the case of our patient.[,]

[b00018] DISCUSSION
The incidence of postoperative meningitis after posterior fossa surgery varies in the literature, ranging from 2% to nearly 10%, depending on surgical technique, patient comorbidities, and infection control measures.[,]

[b00019] DISCUSSION
Among the causative pathogens, K. pneumoniae remains an uncommon but increasingly reported agent, particularly in its MDR forms.[,]. Carbapenemase-producing strains (oxacillin 48 (OXA-48)) or extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producers are responsible for invasive nosocomial infections, which are often diagnosed late and associated with poor prognosis.[,,] In our case, the isolated strain exhibited an (XDR) profile, with susceptibility retained only to colistin and fosfomycin, significantly limiting therapeutic options.[,]

[b00020] DISCUSSION
The management of XDR K. pneumoniae meningitis relies on early, targeted antimicrobial therapy, which remains challenging. Colistin is a commonly used option in this setting, despite its modest meningeal penetration and well-known toxicity. High-dose intravenous administration (typically a 9 million unit loading dose, followed by 4.5 million units every 12 h) may be combined, in selected cases, with intrathecal or intraventricular administration to enhance central nervous system (CNS) penetration.[,]

[b00021] DISCUSSION
Intravenous fosfomycin, administered at high doses (up to 16–24 g/day), provides good CSF diffusion and can be used in combination therapy, particularly in infections caused by KPC-producing K. pneumoniae strains.[,]

[b00022] DISCUSSION
The efficacy of colistin-fosfomycin combination therapy has been reported in several case series, especially in the neurosurgical postoperative setting, and appears to be associated with improved clinical outcomes in severe forms.[,,] Other combinations, such as colistin-tigecycline or colistin-amikacin, have also been described, although the CNS penetration of these agents remains uncertain.[,]

[b00023] DISCUSSION
The use of novel-generation antimicrobials represents a promising therapeutic avenue. Meropenem-vaborbactam has demonstrated both clinical and microbiological efficacy against KPC-producing strains, including in cases of postoperative meningitis, as reported by Rezzonico et al., in a similar case successfully treated with this agent in combination with fosfomycin.[] Cefiderocol and aztreonamavibactam are also potential options under evaluation, although their availability remains variable across healthcare settings.[,] In our case, although susceptibility to agents such as meropenem-vaborbactam or cefiderocol was likely, these antimicrobials were not available in our institution at the time of treatment. Furthermore, due to the patient’s clinical instability and the need for urgent therapy, we opted for rapid initiation of the only available antibiotics with confirmed in vitro activity – colistin and fosfomycin.

[b00024] DISCUSSION
Microbiological diagnosis was based on intraoperative tissue and CSF cultures, which were crucial for confirming the presence of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae. Blood cultures remained negative, underscoring the importance of direct sampling from the infection site. While conventional culture methods provided definitive identification and antibiotic susceptibility results, rapid molecular diagnostic tools such as the BioFire FilmArray^(®) meningitis/encephalitis panel have demonstrated significant value in accelerating pathogen detection, often delivering results within hours.[] These rapid tests facilitate early targeted antimicrobial therapy, especially critical in infections caused by MDR organisms where empirical treatments may be ineffective.[] Although not employed in this case, incorporation of such rapid diagnostics in clinical practice could potentially improve patient outcomes through prompt treatment adaptation.[]

[b00025] DISCUSSION
In the absence of standardized guidelines, treatment duration is based on clinical and microbiological criteria. A 14–21-day antibiotic course is generally recommended, with extension in cases of intracranial complications or parenchymal involvement.[,] Surgical management remains a corner stone of care, including drainage of collections, closure of CSF leaks, and removal of contaminated devices, all essential for infection control.[,,]

[b00026] DISCUSSION
The unfavorable outcome of our patient, who died on postoperative day 12 due to massive pulmonary embolism, highlights the severity of MDR K. pneumoniae infections in the neurosurgical postoperative context.

[b00027] DISCUSSION
Although the pulmonary embolism was not a direct complication of the meningitis itself, it is likely to have been indirectly related to the overall clinical course. The patient had multiple risk factors for thromboembolism, including recent major neurosurgery, prolonged immobilization, systemic inflammation, and critical illness. Despite early pharmacological prophylaxis, these factors may have contributed to a prothrombotic state, ultimately resulting in a fatal thromboembolic event. Several studies report high mortality rates, sometimes exceeding 50%, particularly in cases of delayed diagnosis or limited therapeutic options.[,]

[b00028] DISCUSSION
This case underscores the need for heightened vigilance in the postoperative monitoring of high-risk patients, as well as the importance of prompt access to innovative antimicrobials and a multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists, neurosurgeons, and intensivists.

[b00029] CONCLUSION
Postoperative meningitis caused by K. pneumoniae remains rare but particularly alarming due to the severity of the clinical presentation and the high prevalence of MDR strains. The present case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with an (XDR) strain, which retained susceptibility only to agents such as colistin and fosfomycin, whose availability is sometimes limited.

[b00030] CONCLUSION
In retrospect, several aspects of the patient’s management could have been optimized. Earlier suspicion of MDR pathogens and the use of rapid molecular diagnostics might have enabled faster initiation of targeted therapy. Limited access to novel antimicrobials such as meropenem-vaborbactam constrained therapeutic options. A multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists could have supported earlier therapeutic adjustments. In addition, enhanced monitoring for thromboembolic events, given the patient’s high-risk profile, might have contributed to prevention or earlier detection of the fatal pulmonary embolism. These considerations highlight the complexity of managing XDR infections in neurosurgical patients and the importance of timely, coordinated care.

[b00031] CONCLUSION
The unfavorable outcome observed despite prompt management highlights the crucial importance of a multidisciplinary approach combining surgical intervention, infectious disease expertise, and critical care. This case also underscores the need for the early microbiological diagnosis and access to novel therapeutic options in the fight against MDR Enterobacterales in the neurosurgical setting.
