De novo Inflammatory Bowel Disease following Liver and Heart Transplantation: A Case Series Exploring the Role of IL-23 Pathway-Targeted Therapy

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
De novo inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a rare but clinically significant complication after solid organ transplantation (SOT), with incidence higher than that in the general population. Its pathogenesis, diagnostic challenges, and optimal management remain poorly defined. We describe 6 cases of de novo IBD following liver or heart transplantation and evaluate therapeutic outcomes with selective biologics targeting the IL-23 pathway.

[b00002] Abstract / Methods
Six SOT recipients (3 liver, 3 heart) developed new-onset ulcerative colitis-type (UC-type, n = 3) or Crohn’s disease-like (CD-like, n = 3) IBD after a median of 47.5 months post-transplantation. None had pre-transplant intestinal symptoms; colonoscopy was negative before transplantation in 2 UC-type cases. Alternative etiologies – including cytomegalovirus colitis, drug-induced colitis, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder – were rigorously excluded. Treatments included systemic corticosteroids, mirikizumab, ustekinumab, or risankizumab. Median follow-up was 15.6 months.

[b00003] Abstract / Results
Corticosteroids induced remission in 2 UC-type cases, while one steroid-refractory UC-type case and all CD-like cases achieved clinical improvement and endoscopic response or remission with IL-23-targeted biologics. No opportunistic infections, severe biologic-related adverse events, or graft rejection were observed.

[b00004] Abstract / Conclusions
De novo IBD emerged years after SOT, with variable phenotypes despite baseline immunosuppression. IL-23 pathway inhibitors demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in this vulnerable population, representing a promising strategy warranting further prospective evaluation.

[b00005] Introduction
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), comprising ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. While IBD typically arises in immunocompetent individuals, it is now increasingly recognized as a rare but clinically significant complication among solid organ transplantation (SOT) recipients []. In this context, de novo IBD is defined as newly developed, chronic intestinal inflammation meeting established diagnostic criteria for UC or CD – based on compatible clinical, endoscopic, and histopathologic findings – occurring after transplantation in patients with no prior history of IBD, and only after rigorous exclusion of alternative etiologies such as cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis, drug-induced colitis, ischemic colitis, or post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) []. The incidence of de novo IBD in SOT recipients has been estimated to be approximately tenfold higher than in the general population, at around 206 cases per 100,000 person-years [, ]. In Western countries, the majority of reported de novo IBD cases after SOT have been associated with liver transplantation for primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), particularly in patients with UC []. In Asian countries, while a recent case series has documented de novo IBD after kidney transplantation, with an estimated incidence of 355.8 per 100,000 person-years [], reports of de novo IBD after transplantation of other solid organs remain rare and largely limited to isolated case reports [, ].

[b00006] Introduction
Diagnosing de novo IBD in SOT recipients is challenging because clinical presentations may overlap with post-transplant colitides caused by opportunistic infections (e.g., CMV, Clostridioides difficile), drug-induced colitis, ischemia, or PTLD []. Long-term immunosuppressive therapy may mask or modify the typical endoscopic and histological features of IBD, complicating differentiation from these alternative etiologies. Therapeutic decision-making is also complex as baseline immunosuppression raises concerns regarding additive therapy, increased infection risk, and potential malignancy when introducing additional immunomodulators or biologic agents.

[b00007] Introduction
Although biologic therapies targeting distinct inflammatory pathways have transformed the management of IBD in the general population, evidence regarding their safety and efficacy in SOT recipients remains scarce. Here, we describe a case series of 6 patients who developed de novo IBD following liver or heart transplantation at our institution, aiming to delineate their clinical characteristics, diagnostic approach, and therapeutic outcomes, and to explore the potential role of selective biologic therapies targeting the IL-23 pathway in this unique patient population.

[b00008] Methods / Study Design and Patients
We conducted a single-center, retrospective observational study of SOT recipients who developed de novo IBD at the University of Tokyo Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. Eligible patients had undergone liver or heart transplantation with no prior history or symptoms of IBD, subsequently developed new-onset chronic intestinal inflammation after transplantation that fulfilled established diagnostic criteria for UC or CD, and had alternative etiologies rigorously excluded, including CMV colitis, drug-induced colitis (e.g., tacrolimus [Tac], mycophenolate mofetil [MMF]), ischemic colitis, Clostridioides difficile infection, and PTLD. For clarity, we refer to the phenotypes as “UC-type” and “CD-like” throughout; all cases fulfilled established diagnostic criteria for UC or CD. A total of 6 patients met these criteria: 3 diagnosed with UC after liver transplantation (all transplanted for PSC) and 3 diagnosed with CD after heart transplantation (2 for dilated cardiomyopathy and one for ischemic heart disease).

[b00009] Methods / Data Collection
Clinical data were retrieved from electronic medical records, including demographics, indication for transplantation, immunosuppressive regimens and Tac trough levels at IBD onset, interval from transplantation to IBD onset, presenting symptoms, laboratory parameters (serum albumin, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein), immunosuppressive agents such as Tac, MMF, and methylprednisolone, CMV serostatus of donor and recipient, use of valganciclovir (VGCV) prophylaxis, endoscopic and histopathologic findings, treatment course, and follow-up outcomes.

[b00010] Methods / Endoscopic and Histopathologic Evaluation
All patients underwent colonoscopy at the time of IBD diagnosis. Endoscopic disease activity was graded using the Mayo Endoscopic Subscore (MES) and the Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity (UCEIS) for UC-type cases, and the Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease (SES-CD) for CD-like cases. Histopathologic evaluation assessed features of chronic active inflammation (crypt architectural distortion, crypt abscesses, granulomas) and excluded infectious etiologies using CMV immunohistochemistry and Epstein-Barr virus-encoded RNA in situ hybridization (EBER-ISH).

[b00011] Methods / Treatment and Follow-Up
For UC-type cases, systemic corticosteroids were administered as initial therapy. Patients who were steroid-refractory or had CD-like IBD received biologic agents targeting the IL-23 pathway, including mirikizumab, ustekinumab, or risankizumab, in addition to their baseline transplant immunosuppression. Dose adjustments of calcineurin inhibitors or mycophenolate were made in collaboration with the transplant team. Patients were followed for clinical activity (full Mayo score for UC-type and Crohn’s Disease Activity Index [CDAI] for CD-like cases), endoscopic response or remission, graft function, infectious complications, and adverse events.

[b00012] Methods / Statistical Analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with ranges, and categorical variables as proportions (%). Given the descriptive nature and small sample size of this case series, no formal statistical comparisons were performed.

[b00013] Results
Six patients developed de novo IBD following liver or heart transplantation at our institution. Three patients had undergone liver transplantation for PSC and were subsequently diagnosed with UC. All 3 patients experienced intestinal symptoms for the first time after SOT and were, therefore, classified as having de novo IBD. Notably, in 2 patients (Cases 2 and 3), colonoscopy performed prior to transplantation confirmed the absence of colonic inflammation. The remaining 3 patients (Cases 4–6) developed CD-like de novo IBD following heart transplantation. None had experienced intestinal symptoms prior to transplantation, and given the rarity of IBD in heart transplant recipients, all 3 were considered to have new-onset disease. The median age at IBD onset was 46.5 years (range, 38–72), and the median interval from transplantation to IBD onset was 47.5 months (range, 9.7–162.2) (Table 1, 2). Two liver transplant recipients experienced a single episode of acute rejection during the early postoperative period, and both cases were well controlled with temporary high-dose corticosteroid therapy. All transplant recipients – particularly those who had undergone liver transplantation – were recommended routine post-transplant cancer surveillance consisting of annual contrast-enhanced abdominal CT, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, fecal occult blood testing, and colonoscopy as part of standard follow-up at our institution.

[b00028] Results
At the time of IBD onset, all 6 SOT recipients were receiving Tac-based immunosuppressive regimens, with concomitant agents including MMF (n = 5) and methylprednisolone, which was administered exclusively to liver transplant recipients. VGCV prophylaxis was administered in 2 patients, one of whom had a documented CMV donor-recipient mismatch (Table 1).

[b00029] Results
Clinically, all patients presented with diarrhea, and 5 experienced abdominal pain. Bloody stools occurred in 4 cases. The median time from transplantation to IBD onset was 47.5 months (range, 9.7–162.2). Laboratory evaluation showed hypoalbuminemia, anemia, and elevated inflammatory markers. The median serum albumin was 2.9 g/dL (range, 2.2–3.8), C-reactive protein was 4.87 mg/dL (range, 0.90–9.85), and hemoglobin was 10.5 g/dL (range, 8.9–11.1). Clinical disease activity scores indicated moderate-to-severe inflammation, with a median full Mayo score of 9 (range, 8–11) for UC-type cases and a median CDAI of 295 (range, 201–327) for CD-like cases (Table 2).

[b00030] Results
Endoscopic findings were consistent with the respective disease phenotypes. UC-type cases demonstrated diffuse mucosal friability and ulceration throughout the colon, with MES of 3 and UCEIS scores ranging from 6 to 8. In contrast, CD-like cases exhibited segmental inflammation with skip lesions; deep longitudinal ulcers were observed in Cases 4 and 6, whereas Case 5 showed localized ulcerations confined to the cecum. The SES-CD ranged from 8 to 37 (Table 3). Representative endoscopic images are shown in Figure 1, demonstrating marked mucosal improvement or remission in most patients following treatment.

[b00038] Results
Histopathological analysis revealed moderate-to-severe active inflammation in all patients. In UC-type cases (Cases 1–3), crypt architectural distortion and crypt abscesses were observed, consistent with chronic active colitis. In CD-like cases (Cases 4–6), no granulomas were detected. All cases were negative for CMV immunohistochemistry and EBER-ISH, and no histological evidence of CMV colitis or PTLD was identified (Table 3). In CD-like cases, CMV colitis could not be definitively excluded during the initial evaluation due to the presence of deep ulcerations. Therefore, repeat biopsies were performed during follow-up, but CMV remained consistently undetected.

[b00039] Results
Drug-induced colitis related to Tac or MMF was also carefully considered. Although MMF-related injury can present with a wide histological spectrum, none of the hallmark findings – such as increased epithelial apoptosis, GVHD-like crypt dropout, or eosinophil-rich inflammation – were observed in any case. When clinically feasible, adjustments to baseline immunosuppression were attempted to assess potential drug-related toxicity. In Cases 4 and 5, Tac trough levels were reduced and MMF was switched to everolimus (EVL), while in Case 6, Tac had already been minimized and further MMF modification was not possible due to severe inflammation with deep ulcers and multiple strictures. In contrast, the rapidly progressive UC-type disease in Cases 1–3 allowed no safe opportunity for immunosuppressant modification. Importantly, none of the attempted adjustments resulted in clinical, endoscopic, or histological improvement. Although drug-induced colitis could not be definitively excluded, the absence of characteristic histological features of MMF-induced colitis, together with the lack of response to immunosuppressant modification, supported the working diagnosis of de novo IBD and led to initiation of IBD-directed therapy.

[b00040] Results
In the UC-type cases (Cases 1–3), induction therapy was determined according to disease severity and clinical urgency. Cases 1 and 2 initially received 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) for safety considerations; however, the response was insufficient and corticosteroids (25–30 mg/day) were subsequently required, resulting in both clinical and endoscopic remission. Remission was maintained on their baseline immunosuppressive regimen without additional IBD-specific therapy. Case 3 presented with severe pancolitis with multiple deep ulcers and rapidly progressive disease, leaving insufficient time to trial 5-ASA. Corticosteroids were initiated but proved ineffective, and early escalation to mirikizumab (IL-23 p19 inhibitor) was required, leading to clinical and endoscopic remission. In the CD-like cases (Cases 4–6), biologic therapy was selected as first-line treatment because systemic corticosteroids were avoided. In these patients, the deep ulcerations made it difficult to exclude CMV colitis endoscopically, particularly under profound T-cell immunosuppression. In addition, the diagnostic sensitivity of CMV testing may be limited, especially in patients receiving VGCV prophylaxis, which further reduced our confidence in excluding CMV infection. For these reasons, corticosteroids were deferred and biologic therapy was used for induction therapy. Ustekinumab (IL-12/23 p40 inhibitor; Case 4) and risankizumab (IL-23 p19 inhibitor; Cases 5 and 6) achieved favorable outcomes, with clinical improvement and either endoscopic remission or partial response (Table 4).

[b00048] Results
During the follow-up period (median: 15.6 months; range: 5.1–38.2 months), all patients maintained stable graft function, and no IBD-related surgeries were required. One patient (Case 6) developed acute myeloid leukemia (AML), although its relationship to biologic therapy remains unclear. No other adverse events were observed (Table 4). Given the descriptive nature of this case series and limited sample size, no formal statistical analysis was performed.

[b00049] Discussion
This case series illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of de novo IBD in SOT recipients, highlighting the importance of rigorous exclusion of potential mimickers, including drug-induced colitis, CMV colitis, and PTLD []. Among our 6 cases, 3 patients developed UC-type disease following liver transplantation for PSC, and the other 3 developed CD-like disease following heart transplantation. All were classified as de novo IBD based on the absence of pre-transplant gastrointestinal symptoms. Notably, in the UC-type group, 2 patients (Cases 2 and 3) had undergone colonoscopy prior to transplantation, which confirmed the absence of colonic inflammation (MES 0 and UCEIS 0); however, mucosal biopsies were not obtained, and therefore, microscopic inflammation could not be completely excluded. In the remaining patient (Case 1), pre-transplant colonoscopy was not available as the liver transplantation had been performed more than 20 years earlier and detailed endoscopic records could not be retrieved; thus, the presence of subclinical UC cannot be definitively excluded, particularly given the well-established association between PSC and UC [, ]. In contrast, the 3 patients who developed CD-like disease had received heart transplants. None had experienced intestinal symptoms prior to transplantation. Although all liver transplant recipients in our series developed UC-type disease and all heart transplant recipients developed CD-like disease, previous reports have described both UC-like and CD-like phenotypes following either liver or heart transplantation [, ]. Accordingly, the phenotypic distribution observed in our cases should not be interpreted as organ-specific, but rather as reflecting the heterogeneity of post-transplant immune environments that may influence disease expression.

[b00050] Discussion
The pathogenesis of de novo IBD following SOT is multifactorial, involving host susceptibility, immune dysregulation, microbial alterations, and environmental triggers. Calcineurin inhibitors, which are core agents for preventing graft rejection, may disrupt the balance between regulatory T cells (Treg) and T helper 17 (Th17) cells [–]. Treg are generally classified into thymus-derived and peripherally induced subsets, both essential for maintaining immune homeostasis and tolerance []. Long-term exposure to calcineurin inhibitors may impair Treg differentiation and survival through IL-2 suppression, shifting mucosal immunity toward a pro-inflammatory state [, ]. Indeed, experimental models have shown that depletion of Treg leads to uncontrolled Th17-driven colitis []. In parallel, these agents may also alter the gut microbial ecosystem, reducing microbial diversity and promoting pro-inflammatory bacterial species, which can compromise epithelial barrier function and further amplify mucosal immune activation []. Interestingly, this immune imbalance appears to manifest predominantly in the intestinal tract, rather than triggering systemic autoimmune disorders, implying that gut-specific factors – such as CMV reactivation or calcineurin inhibitor-induced dysbiosis – may act synergistically with immune perturbation to promote the development of de novo IBD [, ]. In addition, a mismatch in CMV immune status, in which the organ donor is seropositive (previously infected with CMV) and the recipient is seronegative (never exposed to CMV), has been reported to increase the risk of developing de novo IBD, possibly by disrupting mucosal immune balance following post-transplant CMV infection []. Collectively, these findings suggest that de novo IBD in SOT recipients likely arises from a multifactorial interplay of immune dysregulation and microbial disturbances rather than being driven by a single immunosuppressive agent. Nonetheless, the precise pathogenic mechanisms remain undefined, and future molecular and immunological analyses will be essential to elucidate the mechanisms of de novo IBD.

[b00051] Discussion
Diagnosing de novo IBD after SOT remains challenging. Potential mimickers, including drug-related mucosal injury, CMV or C. difficile infections, ischemic colitis, and PTLD, must be carefully excluded []. Long-term immunosuppression may alter classic IBD features, complicating diagnosis. CMV colitis is particularly problematic as its clinical and endoscopic presentation may mimic IBD, and antiviral prophylaxis may reduce the sensitivity of diagnostic testing. In our series, repeated biopsies, expert pathological review, and multidisciplinary reassessment were essential to confidently exclude CMV infection. Drug-induced colitis related to Tac or MMF represents another important differential diagnosis in SOT recipients. MMF-induced colitis can closely resemble IBD and may present with a broad histopathological spectrum []. In principle, diagnostic evaluation may include reducing Tac trough levels or modifying MMF, such as temporary discontinuation or switching to EVL, to assess potential drug-related toxicity. However, such adjustments are frequently constrained by the competing risks of graft rejection and active intestinal inflammation. In our cases, immunosuppressant modification – including Tac reduction and switching from MMF to EVL – was attempted when clinically feasible but did not result in clinical, endoscopic, or histological improvement. Importantly, none of the cases demonstrated characteristic histological features of MMF-induced colitis, such as prominent epithelial apoptosis, GVHD-like crypt dropout, or eosinophil-rich inflammation. In addition, Tac was not completely discontinued in any case because of concerns regarding graft rejection. Taken together, although drug-induced colitis could not be completely excluded and de novo IBD remains a diagnosis of exclusion, the lack of response to immunosuppressant modification and the absence of typical histological findings favored de novo IBD as the most plausible working diagnosis in our cases.

[b00052] Discussion
From a therapeutic perspective, corticosteroids remain a mainstay in conventional IBD but may exacerbate viral replication and infectious complications in transplant recipients []. Accordingly, treatment strategies in our cases were individualized based on disease phenotype, severity, CMV risk, and background immunosuppression, in line with the clinical course described in the Results section. In UC-type cases, treatment was initially started with 5-ASA because of its favorable safety profile; however, the response was insufficient in Cases 1 and 2, necessitating escalation of therapy. In these patients, CMV infection had been repeatedly excluded histologically, antiviral prophylaxis was not being administered, and endoscopic findings demonstrated diffuse and continuous inflammation rather than focal deep ulcerations characteristic of CMV colitis. After multidisciplinary discussion with the transplant team, temporary corticosteroid induction was, therefore, considered acceptable despite background immunosuppression. Because these patients were already receiving potent baseline immunosuppression to prevent graft rejection, we aimed to avoid unnecessary addition of IBD-specific maintenance therapy. Cases 1 and 2 achieved remission with corticosteroid induction and were subsequently maintained on baseline transplant immunosuppression alone. Case 3 differed in that it presented with severe, rapidly progressive pancolitis with diffuse, continuous involvement and deep ulcers, leaving insufficient time to trial 5-ASA. Corticosteroids were initiated but proved ineffective, and early escalation to biologic therapy was required. Given the disease severity, biologic therapy was continued as maintenance therapy after successful induction. In contrast, CD-like cases were treated with biologic therapy as first-line induction. In these patients, deep ulcerations with a non-diffuse, non-continuous distribution under profound T-cell immunosuppression made CMV colitis difficult to exclude endoscopically, and 2 patients were receiving VGCV prophylaxis, raising concern regarding corticosteroid use. After careful discussion with the transplant team, systemic corticosteroids were, therefore, avoided. Moreover, because CD-like disease carries a substantial risk of stricturing or penetrating complications and thiopurines could not be added because of concomitant MMF therapy, biologic therapy was selected from the induction phase.

[b00053] Discussion
Historically, anti-tumor necrosis factor agents were commonly used to treat either exacerbation or de novo IBD following transplantation due to limited alternative options. While these agents are not invariably associated with severe complications, they may pose an increased risk of infectious complications in immunosuppressed patients [, ]. In recent years, the therapeutic landscape has expanded with the availability of biologic agents that may offer more favorable safety profiles. In UC-type disease, vedolizumab would ordinarily be considered in cases with a less urgent disease course because of its favorable safety profile. However, in Case 3, the disease course was rapidly progressive and severe, necessitating prompt induction of remission, and therefore, an IL-23 inhibitor with a faster onset of action was selected. Conversely, although anti-tumor necrosis factor agents are typically first-line therapy for severe CD in immunocompetent patients, IL-12/23 or IL-23 inhibitors were chosen in our CD-like cases to mitigate infection risk under profound baseline immunosuppression. Notably, IL-23 pathway inhibitors demonstrated clinical efficacy even under Tac- and MMF-based baseline immunosuppression, without opportunistic infections, PTLD, or graft dysfunction during follow-up. As demonstrated in immunocompetent populations [–], IL-23 pathway inhibitors may offer a favorable balance between safety and efficacy. However, clinical evidence regarding their use in post-transplant recipients remains limited. Although direct evidence implicating the IL-23 pathway in de novo IBD after SOT is currently lacking, these findings suggest that IL-23-mediated mechanisms may contribute to disease expression in a subset of cases and highlight the potential clinical utility of these agents in this high-risk population.

[b00054] Discussion
Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of mechanistic validation (e.g., immunophenotyping or microbiome analysis). Although IL-23 pathway inhibitors are approved for IBD in the general population, clinical data in SOT recipients remain scarce. Prospective studies are needed to evaluate efficacy, safety, and graft-related outcomes in this population.

[b00055] Discussion
In conclusion, de novo IBD after SOT appears to be a distinct clinical entity shaped by post-transplant immune dysregulation and gut-specific factors. IL-23 pathway inhibitors may represent a rational and potentially safer therapeutic option in selected SOT recipients, particularly in clinical scenarios, where corticosteroid use is undesirable or rapid and sustained disease control is required.
