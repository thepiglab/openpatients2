Omalizumab for Pediatric Cutaneous Mastocytosis: Case Report and Review

[b00001] ABSTRACT
We report an 11‐month‐old boy with diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis whose severe pruritus and steroid dependence resolved following off‐label treatment with omalizumab. A literature review identified five additional pediatric cases in which omalizumab led to complete symptom resolution in an average of 2 months and permitted discontinuation of systemic steroids. Although limited by the small sample size and lack of high‐quality data, omalizumab presents as a well‐tolerated and potentially effective steroid‐sparing agent for pediatric cutaneous mastocytosis.

[b00002] Introduction
Mastocytosis is a rare and heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by clonal mast cell proliferation. It can be classified into three types, each with further clinical subtypes: cutaneous mastocytosis (CM), systemic mastocytosis (SM), and localized mast cell tumors []. Mastocytosis is linked to c‐KIT gene mutations, specifically KIT D816V []. Childhood‐onset mastocytosis is typically cutaneous and self‐limited; however, it can be severe in the form of diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis (DCM), which typically presents within the first 6 months of life. DCM classically manifests as widespread vesiculobullous lesions, which progressively become more infiltrative within the skin. It is associated with high serum tryptase levels and mast cell mediator‐related symptoms (e.g., pruritus, flushing, hypotension, headache, diarrhea, and anaphylaxis) []. Diagnosis and management may be delayed as DCM can mimic more common blistering diseases of childhood [].

[b00003] Introduction
Treatment for cutaneous mastocytosis is symptom‐driven, and as pediatric mastocytosis may improve or resolve by puberty, benefits and risks must be carefully weighed. Cornerstones of management include education on potential triggers for mast cell degranulation and anaphylaxis, as well as multidisciplinary care with dermatology, allergy/immunology, and hematology when needed [, ]. Antihistamines are a mainstay of therapy, while topical and systemic corticosteroids are commonly used, particularly for more severe disease flares []. Antileukotriene medications may also be considered.

[b00004] Introduction
Omalizumab (Xolair) is a monoclonal anti‐IgE antibody that downregulates mast cells and their mediator release by reversibly binding to free serum IgE, preventing it from binding to its high‐affinity receptor on mast cell and basophil surfaces [, ]. In North America, omalizumab is approved for the treatment of allergic asthma, chronic spontaneous urticaria, and, in adults, nasal polyps. It has recently been approved in the United States for the management of food allergies in children. One randomized controlled trial showed improvement in adult mastocytosis with omalizumab, though the results were not statistically significant []. This case report and review of previously published cases show omalizumab to be a well‐tolerated steroid‐sparing agent for the treatment of severe treatment‐refractory pediatric CM.

[b00005] Case Report
A three‐month‐old boy presented with a three‐week history of progressively worsening generalized vesiculobullous rash, initially treated as impetigo. He was otherwise systemically well and, aside from gastroesophageal reflux, had no relevant past medical or family history.

[b00006] Case Report
Physical examination revealed diffuse, ill‐defined, leathery skin thickening, erythema, vesicles, and bullae with serous contents, and well‐circumscribed erosions (Figure 1). Darier's sign was positive. The patient appeared non‐toxic, with no lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly. Serum tryptase was elevated at 30.8 μg/L (reference range: < 11.4 μg/L). Abdominal ultrasound was unremarkable, and genetic testing for the KIT D816V mutation was negative. Hematology ruled out SM given the absence of systemic features such as gastrointestinal involvement, hepatomegaly, lymphadenopathy, or cytopenia. A bone marrow biopsy was later deferred as serum tryptase progressively decreased with omalizumab treatment. A skin biopsy revealed a dense, dermal mast cell infiltrate with strong, positive staining for tryptase and CD117, confirming the clinical diagnosis of DCM.

[b00007] Case Report
The child received multidisciplinary care through Dermatology and Allergy/Immunology. The family was educated on mast cell degranulation and anaphylaxis triggers, and epinephrine auto‐injectors were prescribed. He was started on betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointment and off‐label rupatadine at 10 mg daily, given as 5 mg orally twice per day, whereas the standard dose for infants weighing 10–25 kg is 2.5 mg orally once per day. Due to ongoing episodes of pruritus, flushing, and blistering, ketotifen was introduced at 0.1 mg/kg orally daily (standard dose: 0.05 mg/kg), along with montelukast 4 mg orally daily. In the first 12 months of life, despite the above medications, the patient required 8 prednisolone bursts, each lasting 4 weeks, to control symptoms, yet there continued to be significant impacts on quality of life. The regimen consisted of 1 mg/kg/day (10 mg orally daily) for 7 days, followed by tapering in decrements of 2.5 mg per week until discontinuation. Though multiple weans were attempted, maintenance prednisolone was unable to be decreased below 7 mg per day due to severe symptoms. At 11 months of age (9.5 kg), the patient was started on omalizumab 150 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks, with significant improvement in pruritus, blistering, and flushing noted after 2 doses (Figure 1). Over the next year, the dosing was escalated to 300 mg subcutaneously and the interval was later shortened to every 3 weeks for better symptom control with excellent response. After 1 month of omalizumab, systemic corticosteroids were successfully discontinued, as were montelukast and ketotifen. The patient has remained on omalizumab for over 2 years with improved ongoing control and no adverse events, with a serum tryptase that has decreased to 6.2 ug/L, though should the patient miss his q21‐day dose of omalizumab, symptoms of pruritus and flushing start to return.

[b00008] Discussion
This case adds to the limited but growing evidence supporting omalizumab as a potential therapeutic option for pediatric CM. Omalizumab downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells by binding circulating IgE, thereby reducing mast cell activation and mediator release [, ]. This provides a strong rationale for its use in pediatric CM, where disease burden is largely driven by mediator‐related symptoms.

[b00009] Discussion
To date (August 15, 2024), only five pediatric patients treated with omalizumab have been reported (ages 2–15 years; Table 1), spanning diffuse and maculopapular CM, with and without systemic involvement [, , , ]. Most had a history of anaphylaxis, and all had failed conventional therapies. Omalizumab (150–450 mg every 2–4 weeks) led to rapid improvement, often after a single injection, with complete resolution of mast cell mediator‐related symptoms and successful discontinuation of systemic corticosteroids. Tryptase levels generally remained unchanged, and recurrence has rarely been evaluated. In one patient, no relapse was observed after 9 months of discontinuation [].

[b00018] Discussion
Our case adds several unique insights: omalizumab was initiated at 11 months of age, among the youngest reported; the patient had severe steroid‐dependent CM, requiring eight prednisolone bursts in the first year, yet achieved complete steroid discontinuation after 1 month of omalizumab treatment; serum tryptase decreased substantially (30.8 ug/L vs. 6.2 ug/L); dose escalation (150 to 300 mg) and interval shortening (q4w to q3w) were required for sustained control; and over 2 years of follow‐up demonstrated durable efficacy, absence of adverse events, and symptom relapse when a dose was missed. Together, these findings strengthen the evidence that omalizumab is a promising, steroid‐sparing therapy for pediatric CM, with no reported long‐term adverse events. Nevertheless, data remain limited to case reports. Thus, larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm efficacy, establish optimal dosing, and evaluate outcomes following cessation. Investigation of omalizumab in adult mastocytosis also remains an important area for future research.
