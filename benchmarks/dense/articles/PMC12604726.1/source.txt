Managing subclinical rhegmatogenous retinal detachment with demarcation laser photocoagulation: A case series

[b00001] Abstract
Symptomatic retinal detachment threatening the macula is considered an emergency requiring surgical intervention. However, the management of subclinical rhegmatogenous retinal detachment remains controversial, with differing opinions among clinicians. Almost half of these cases are known to progress into symptomatic detachments; hence, management at the asymptomatic stage is advised rather than mere observation. In such cases, demarcation laser photocoagulation (DLP) can help to contain the detachment. It involves creating a laser barrage around the detachment by applying 3–5 rows of confluent laser burns with a laser indirect ophthalmoscopy delivery system. DLP, being an outpatient-based, inexpensive, and minimally invasive procedure, can prove beneficial in such patients, especially those who are not motivated enough to undergo an invasive procedure in an asymptomatic eye. Through this case series, the authors have described the clinical profile of the patients with subclinical retinal detachment, where DLP was helpful in the containment of the subretinal fluid.

[b00002] Introduction
The management of asymptomatic retinal detachment has always been an enigma for the clinicians. The condition is generally detected incidentally on routine ophthalmic examination, and counseling the asymptomatic patient harboring the vision-threatening disease becomes difficult. With the advent of widefield fundus imaging, a pictorial description of the present ocular condition to the patients has become easier and aids in the documentation.

[b00003] Introduction
Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment usually presents after the premonitory signs of flashes and floaters, and if the patient is vigilant enough, they may perceive field defects before macular involvement.[] Although there is no clear consensus regarding the definition of subclinical retinal detachment (SCRD), however, it is termed as the one that causes no change in visual acuity nor any field defects.[] By definition, it has been described as neurosensory detachment reaching more than one disc diameter from the edge of the retinal tear but no more than two disc diameters posterior to the equator.[]

[b00004] Introduction
Out of the various options available for the management of symptomatic retinal detachments that include scleral buckling (SB), pneumatic retinopexy, and microincision vitrectomy surgery (MIVS), demarcation laser photocoagulation (DLP) is the least invasive and nontraumatic method, which can be employed to delimit these asymptomatic detachments.[]

[b00005] Introduction
DLP is an outpatient-based procedure that involves creating a laser barrage of 3–5 rows of confluent laser burns of moderate intensity applied, delimiting the detachment. This creates chorioretinal adhesions, preventing the progression of subretinal fluid. This does not require anaesthesia, specific positioning, or special equipment as are needed for the abovementioned surgical procedures. This makes it a simple, inexpensive yet effective procedure to treat subclinical detachments.[] In this case series, the authors have described five similar cases with subclinical detachments who underwent DLP for the same and were followed for over a year.

[b00006] Case Series
In this case series, five patients who were identified with SCRD underwent DLP as management. All the patients underwent DLP similar to barrage laser photocoagulation, where 3–5 rows of confluent laser burns of moderate intensity were applied to contain the detachment using a laser indirect ophthalmoscope delivery system. All the patients were advised to instil topical eyedrops nepafenac (0.3%) once a day for 2 weeks. The patients were followed weekly for a month to detect any signs of progression and advised minimal physical activity for a minimum period of 2 weeks till the laser marks mature. Thereafter, the patients were followed monthly for another 3 months, followed by once in 3 months. No patient showed any sign of progression, nor did anyone require any laser reinforcement during their follow-up. None of the patients complained of any field defect postprocedure. [b00012] Case Series
RRD: Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, BCVA: Best-corrected visual acuity, RD: Recurring deposit

[b00013] Case Series / Case 1
A 54-year-old male presented with the complaint of recent onset of floaters and flashes in the left eye. He had a history of getting a laser barrage done for a retinal hole in the same eye and lasered retinal hole with transscleral cryopexy in the fellow eye 6 years back and was stable ever since. On examination, his best-corrected visual acuity was 20/30 in both eyes, with the refractive error correction of − 12 D Sph/−0.5 D Cyl @ 85° in the right eye and − 10 D Sph/−0.75 D Cyl @ 110° in the left eye. The anterior segment showed no signs of inflammation, and in both eyes, the crystalline lens was clear. On fundus examination, the right eye has a myopic tessellated fundus with the normal disc, but in the left eye, a large horseshoe tear along with an adjacent smaller tear was seen at the temporal periphery overlying the site of the previous barrage laser with subretinal fluid spanning over 2 clock hours [Figure 1a]. Diagnosis of subclinical rhegmatogenous retinal detachment was made, and the patient was counseled about his clinical condition. We also documented a stage 1 epiretinal membrane (ERM) with a normal foveal contour on macular optical coherence tomography in the same eye [Figure 1d and e]. The patient was not keen on getting an invasive procedure as his vision was not affected, so we gave him the option of DLP. He underwent DLP in the left eye and thereafter was regularly followed to detect possible progression of detachment and failure of the procedure [Figure 1b]. After 1 year of regular follow-up, the patient is stable; the laser barrage is well containing the detachment. However, the subretinal fluid is not yet resolved [Figure 1c].

[b00014] Case Series / Case 2
A 60-year-old male came for a regular retina follow-up. He had a history of undergoing MIVS in his left eye for a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment along with cataract extraction 1 year back. In his right eye, he had a history of a barrage laser done 5 years back for a retinal hole. His best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye with a refractive correction of − 6 D Sph and 20/40 in the left eye with a refractive correction of + 0.25 D Sph/−0.25 D Cyl @ 100°. His anterior segment examination was unremarkable except for an early cataract in the right eye. Right eye fundus examination revealed a normal disc and attached macula but also a temporal SCRD adjacent to the previous barraged hole anterior to the equator involving 2 clock hours. The left eye retina was attached post-MIVS. After discussing with the patient his present situation and the various treatment option available, DLP was done in his right eye for the subclinical detachment, and he was kept under close follow-up. Premonitory signs of progressing detachment in the form of flashes, floaters, or field defect were well explained to the patient. The patient has completed 1 year of follow-up, he is still asymptomatic, the subretinal fluid is persistent but no progression of the detachment is seen.

[b00015] Case Series / Case 3
A 48-year-old female came for a regular ophthalmic examination. She had no complaints in either of her eyes. She was a known myope and was using corrective glasses since her teenage. On examination, her best-corrected visual acuity in both eyes was 20/20 with a refractive correction of − 6 D Sph/−1.5 D Cyl @ 65° and − 4.5 D Sph/−0.5 D Cyl @ 30° in right and left eye, respectively. Her anterior segment examination was unremarkable. Fundus examination of the right eye showed an attached retina with a normal disc and macula. In the left eye, the disc and macula appeared normal but a superonasal retinal hole with SCRD was seen spanning over 2 clock hour and anterior to the equator. The patient was counselled about the clinical condition of the left eye, and after considering the various management option DLP was done. As the detachment was shallow, we could also apply some laser marks surrounding the break. The patient was followed up regularly and showed no signs of progression, and the detachment is well contained even after more than a year of follow-up.

[b00016] Case Series / Case 4
A 20-year-old male presented with the complaint of diminution of vision in both his eyes. he was experiencing blurred vision for a while, especially during outdoor sports activities. He had no history of using corrective glasses. On examination, his best-corrected visual acuity was 20/30 in both eyes with a refractive correction of − 1.0 D Sph/−0.75 D Cyl @ 175° in right eye and − 1.0 D Sph/−0.5 D Cyl @ 180° in left eye respectively. Anterior segment examination did not reveal any anomaly. On fundus examination, his right eye showed normal disc and an attached macula. A retinal hole with subclinical rhegmatogenous retinal detachment was noted in the supertemporal quadrant spanning 2 clock hours and within two-disc diameter posterior to the equator, which was judged by the position of vortex veins [Figure 2a]. The left eye showed attached retina, and the rest of the fundus examination was within normal limit [Figure 2d and e]. Upon refining history, he could not recollect any symptoms of flashes or floaters occurring in the past few days, which pointed this SCRD to be an incidental finding. The patient was explained the prognosis of his disease and discussed with him about the management options. Finally, DLP was done in his right eye, delimiting the detachment with some laser marks been applied surrounding the retinal break [Figure 2b]. The patient has completed over a year of follow-up with no signs of progression seen [Figure 2c].

[b00017] Case Series / Case 5
A 20-year-old female came for a regular retina checkup. She had a history of using corrective glasses for the past 5 years and had undergone laser barrage for a lattice with hole in the left eye 3 months before her initial presentation. On examination, her best corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes with the refractive correction of − 3.75 D Sph/−0.5 D Cyl @ 10° and − 4.25 D Sph/−0.75 D Cyl @ 175° in right and left eye, respectively. The anterior segment examination revealed no anomaly. The fundus of the right eye revealed attached macula with a normal disc along with multiple lattices in all the retinal quadrants peripherally. Left eye revealed an attached macula and a normal disc, but a SCRD in the inferotemporal quadrant. A lasered lattice can be seen with sub-retinal fluid originating from it, which appears to be the source. The patient had no complaints of floaters, flashes, or field defects. Considering the management option available, in the right eye, barrage laser photocoagulation for multiple lattices was advised. For the left eye, DLP was done in the single sitting, and thereafter the patient was routinely followed up. The patient has completed a year of follow-up, and the SCRD in stable with no evidence of progression noted.

[b00018] Discussion
Symptomatic clinical rhegmatogenous retinal detachment calls for urgent surgical intervention. However, there are no established guidelines for the management of asymptomatic ones, and practice patterns and opinions may vary among ophthalmologists.

[b00019] Discussion
The incidence and natural history of SCRDs are not clearly defined in the literature, as most of these patients remain asymptomatic and do not seek medical attention.[] Brod and Flynn Jr., in 1995, in a retrospective analysis of 31 eyes with asymptomatic detachment, demonstrated progression only in 2 cases (6%) after a mean follow-up of 2.25 and 3.3 years after initial presentation. They suggested observation as a reasonable option for managing asymptomatic detachments.[] Byer, in 2001, demonstrated progression in 11% of total cases of asymptomatic detachment; however, the incidence of spontaneous regression was also 11%.[] A study by Cohen, in 2005, demonstrated that asymptomatic detachments did not progress in the majority of patients, and none of them developed any symptoms after an average follow-up of 46 months.[] However, the study also mentioned that all of them had inferior or inferotemporal detachments with some degree of demarcation line at the time of presentation, indicating chronicity. Inferior detachments are known to progress slowly owing to the anti-gravitation movement of subretinal fluid.[]

[b00020] Discussion
There is no long-term study that clearly defines the criteria for the management of asymptomatic detachments. The inferences by Cohen cannot be directly applied to all types of SCRD, as they did not address the course of fresh detachments present in quadrants other than inferior or temporal. Waiting for the progression of an asymptomatic detachment before any intervention can be regretful if the patient lands into complete detachment. Before advising observation, consider the patient’s profile and compliance, as not every patient is fortunate enough to have an immediate vitreo-retina facility.

[b00021] Discussion
With advanced high-end machinery and viewing systems, pars plana vitrectomy has surpassed SB for retinal detachment management. With advancements in high-end surgical equipment and visualization systems, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) has become the preferred method over scleral buckling (SB) for managing retinal detachments. However, this comes at a higher cost and increased risk of complications. In particular, patients treated for asymptomatic detachments may experience a decline in visual acuity due to rapid cataract progression and other surgical risks. Additionally, counseling asymptomatic patients to undergo major intraocular surgery with its potential complications can be challenging. Therefore, there is a need for a less invasive procedure that offers minimal risks while maximizing benefits, especially for these patients.

[b00022] Discussion
In this case series, we have described the clinical course of patients with SCRD who were considered for DLP for SCRD. All the patients were followed up for a minimum of 1 year. Patients underwent a single sitting of laser photocoagulation for SCRD through laser indirect ophthalmoscopy delivery system, which remained stable for over a year, pointing towards the relative efficacy of the procedure. Three out of five patients were already in our follow-up for the management of the preexisting retinal pathology in the fellow eye. One of the patients had a history of laser done in the fellow eye elsewhere, whereas one patient was diagnosed with SCRD on his first retina evaluation. The clinical profile of the cases was the point of similarity, i.e., all pointing towards early detachment, which was proven by the shallow configuration, lack of retinal corrugations, and absence of any demarcation line. Only one out of five patients had experienced sudden floaters and flashes of light, for which he considered seeking medical attention. The patient who showed premonitory signs had a horseshoe tear as the causative lesion, in contrast with others who had holes or a lattice with holes. None of the eyes showed any signs of cystoid macular edema or visual field defects postprocedure. In Case 1, no progression in the stage of ERM was detected in subsequent visits.

[b00023] Discussion
Vrabec and Baumal, reported a series of macula-sparing rhegmatogenous retinal detachments (MSRRDs) that were managed by DLP, and they considered it an effective method to manage acute or chronic, primary or recurrent MSRRDs without associated Proliferative Vitreoretinopathy (PVR).[] In a similar series by Shukla et al., barrage laser photocoagulation was considered an alternative tool for managing asymptomatic clinical detachments.[] The study by Koçak et al. demonstrated the relative efficacy of DLP for managing SCRD. The patients who showed progression to clinical retinal detachment were at high risk and had multiple retinal breaks and superior detachment. Nonetheless, they concluded that DLP should be considered a primary treatment modality in managing patients with SCRD.[]

[b00024] Discussion
Similar to their study, we, through this case series, have demonstrated the relative efficacy and safety of the procedure in the described subset of patients. DLP, being a noninvasive and quick procedure, has better acceptance for patients with almost normal vision, in contrast to invasive vitreoretinal surgery; hence, it can be considered an alternative to invasive procedures. We consider DLP for patients with asymptomatic retinal detachment provided, the need for close monitoring and the clinical course of the disease have been well explained to the patients. Postprocedure, these patients should be reviewed to identify early signs of progression so that laser reinforcement or surgical intervention can be planned accordingly.
