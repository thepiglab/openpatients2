Paraquat Poisoning: A Case Series

[b00001] ABSTRACT
Paraquat (PQ) poisoning remains a critical public health concern in Nepal due to its high mortality rate and absence of a specific antidote. PQ, first introduced in 1955 as a herbicide, is extremely toxic when ingested. This report presents two incidents involving PQ poisoning managed at Chitwan Medical College Teaching Hospital. The first case involved a 27‐year‐old woman who ingested approximately 20 mL of 24% PQ in a suicide attempt. Despite prompt gastric decontamination and supportive care, she developed severe respiratory and renal failure, culminating in multi‐organ failure and death within 4 days. The second incident concerned a 35‐year‐old woman who ingested PQ and also administered it to her two sons. After intensive management and psychiatric evaluation, she recovered after 19 days of hospitalization. Her 7‐year‐old son ingested PQ mixed with milk and received supportive treatment for 8 days, after which he was discharged with improved renal and pulmonary function. The 1‐year‐old infant, however, presented with acute respiratory distress and neurological impairment and succumbed 7 h after admission despite continuous positive airway pressure and intubation. These cases underscore the severe systemic toxicity of PQ, particularly its pulmonary and renal effects, and the absence of a definitive antidote. Early diagnosis, prompt decontamination, and intensive supportive care remain crucial for survival. Strengthening preventive strategies, regulatory control, and nationwide poisoning surveillance are essential to mitigate the persistently high mortality associated with PQ ingestion in Nepal.

Paraquat poisoning causes rapid multi‐organ failure with high fatality. Early recognition, aggressive supportive care, and mental health intervention are vital. [b00003] Introduction
Poisoning is a leading cause of emergency hospital admissions in Nepal, posing a significant public health issue []. Paraquat (PQ), a potent herbicide widely used since 1955, is valued for its cost‐effectiveness and environmental benefits []. Initially employed to eliminate marijuana weeds in the U.S. and Mexico, it later gained global popularity []. However, ingestion, whether accidental or intentional, has a high fatality rate (60%–80%) due to the absence of a specific antidote [, ].

[b00004] Introduction
PQ toxicity occurs through redox cycling, where PQ accepts an electron to form a radical that reacts with oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). This continuous ROS production causes widespread oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular necrosis or apoptosis. The ROS also activate redox‐sensitive signaling pathways, including MAPK, NF‐κB, TLRs, TGF‐β/SMAD, and NLRP3 inflammasome, which trigger inflammation and fibrotic responses. These combined oxidative and inflammatory mechanisms contribute to multi‐organ damage in PQ poisoning [].

[b00005] Introduction
Management of PQ poisoning is primarily supportive, as there is no specific antidote. Early recognition and prompt decontamination are crucial. Activated charcoal or Fuller's earth should be administered within 2–4 h of ingestion to reduce absorption. Gastric lavage is avoided due to the risk of corrosive injury. Supportive care follows the ABC approach (airway, breathing, circulation), with close monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and biochemical parameters. Oxygen supplementation should be avoided unless oxygen saturation falls below 90%, since excess oxygen enhances ROS generation. Hemoperfusion or hemodialysis may be beneficial if started within 2 h of ingestion or in cases of renal failure without pneumonitis, though benefit decreases with delayed presentation. Immunosuppressive therapy using cyclophosphamide and corticosteroids may improve survival by reducing inflammation and fibrosis. Antioxidants like vitamins C and E, N‐acetylcysteine, and emerging agents such as rosiglitazone, doxycycline, or rapamycin show experimental promise in mitigating oxidative damage. Careful fluid balance, pain relief, and monitoring for respiratory or cardiovascular compromise remain essential for improving short‐term outcomes in PQ poisoning [, ].

[b00006] Introduction
Very few reports regarding PQ poisoning have been published in the literature. This report highlights two challenging cases of PQ poisoning treated at a Nepalese tertiary care centre.

[b00007] Case Report / Case I / Case History/Examination
A 27‐year‐old woman was admitted to the emergency room of CMTH at around 9 pm on July 29, 2023, 4 h after ingesting about 20 mL of 24% PQ (Gramoxone), with suicidal intent. She experienced nausea and vomiting and arrived at the hospital with a sore throat and epigastric pain. She reported no underlying medical problems, and had been working at a farm, where she had access to herbicides. She was fully conscious with blood pressure 100/50 mmHg, heart rate 110 beats/min, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min, and temperature 36.5°C. Her blood oxygen saturation at room air was 95%. Her oral mucosa was erythematous and edematous. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

[b00008] Case Report / Case I / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
Initial lab results, Electrocardiogram (ECG) and chest X‐rays were normal. Urine dithionite test was strongly positive, confirming the presence of PQ. Blood level for PQ at 6 h after ingestion was 1.98 μg/mL.

[b00009] Case Report / Case I / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
The patient was given 100 g of activated charcoal plus 100 mL of 70% sorbitol via nasogastric tube and intravenous fluid. The patient was admitted to ICU for close observation. After admission the patient was given a repeated dose of activated charcoal and sorbitol. Other supportive treatments included analgesics to control her epigastric pain. In the following 24 h, the patient experienced increasing epigastric pain, severe dysphagia, and shortness of breath. A subsequent chest radiograph revealed bilateral lower‐lobe and perihilar infiltration. Blood chemistries on the second day of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission revealed elevated blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and liver enzymes. Her arterial blood gas values on Day 2 were pH 7.48, PaCO_(2) 32 mmHg, and PaO2 56 mmHg. She required increasing supplemental oxygen to keep her oxygen saturation above 88%.

[b00010] Case Report / Case I / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
On Day 3, the chest radiograph revealed bilateral infiltrates, pneumomediastinum and pneumopericardium. She became increasingly hypoxemic and required intubation and mechanical ventilation. Her renal function deteriorated, with markedly increased blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels, and her urine output decreased. Hemodialysis was started. Progressive multiple‐organ failure ensued, and she died on Day 4 after admission.

[b00011] Case Report / Case II / Case History/Examination
A 35‐year‐old woman was admitted to Chitwan Medical College and Teaching Hospital's Emergency Room (CMCTH ER) on May 31, 2023, at around 7 pm, after ingesting 20 mL of PQ Dichloride with suicidal intent, approximately 3.5 h before presentation. She also poisoned her two sons aged 7 years and 1 year, by mixing the chemical with milk. Her youngest son's excessive crying alerted a relative, leading to suspicion of poisoning, and they were quickly taken to Hetauda Hospital before being referred to CMCTH. On arrival, the woman was stable, with intact consciousness, an open airway, a respiratory rate of 26 breaths/min, a pulse of 92 bpm, and a blood pressure of 100/60 mmHg.

[b00012] Case Report / Case II / Case History/Examination
The elder son was admitted to CMCTH on May 31, 2023, at around 7 pm after being exposed to PQ Dichloride 24% poisoning. 3 h prior to ER presentation. His mother had mixed the poison with milk in a homicidal attempt and given it to both of her sons, but the child spat it out due to the bitter taste. He vomited twice with greenish contents. The exact time since ingestion was unclear when the child was found by family members.

[b00013] Case Report / Case II / Case History/Examination
The younger son with global developmental delay, dystonia, and seizure disorder, was brought to CMCTH on May 31, 2023, at 7 pm, following suspected PQ Dichloride 24% poisoning, 3.5 h after ingestion. The mother had intentionally poisoned herself and her two sons, including this child, by mixing approximately 5 mL of PQ with milk, a dose of around 100 mg/kg (above the lethal threshold). After ingestion, the child experienced excessive crying and worsening of dystonic postures, arching backward which alerted family members. He had three episodes of vomiting with greenish, non‐bloody content. His developmental age was estimated to be between 3 and 9 months. There was no history of cyanosis, abnormal body movements, or external bleeding.

[b00014] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
Initial examination and lab tests, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, were within normal limits. She was treated with intravenous pantoprazole, ondansetron, and fluids, and then transferred to the Medical Intensive Care Unit (MICU) for further care.

[b00015] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
During her stay in the MICU, she complained of dry mouth on the first day, and was treated with intravenous dexamethasone, N‐acetylcysteine, and vitamin C. On the second day, she developed redness in her oral cavity including the tongue, which was managed with topical ointment Delta gel. A psychiatric consultation revealed persistent low mood, disturbed sleep, and anger, but no psychotic symptoms.

[b00016] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
On the third day, the patient developed polyuria with a creatinine level of 1.07 mg/dL. She was started on intravenous vasopressin at 1.8 mL/h. Her potassium level was 3.04 mmol/L for which intravenous potassium chloride supplementation was given. She also developed ulcers on her tongue and odynophagia managed with Chlorhexidine mouth wash.

[b00017] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
On the fourth day, urine output was maintained on vasopressor which was stopped subsequently. AKI was resolved but she started developing bradycardia (heart rate dropped to 28 bpm) and hypokalemia. N‐acetylcysteine and vasopressin therapy were continued.

[b00018] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
Along with his mother and brother, the elder child was taken to a nearby hospital, where he was treated with ceftriaxone and ranitidine before being referred to CMCTH after 3.5 h. On arrival at CMCTH, the child was ill‐looking and drowsy, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 14/15. He had a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/min, clear lung sounds, oxygen saturation of 98% on room air, and a heart rate of 105 bpm. After receiving intravenous ondansetron in the ER, he was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) for further observation.

[b00019] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
During his stay in the PICU, he had one more episode of vomiting, which was treated with Ondansetron. He remained hemodynamically stable with normal input and output and was soon transferred to the ward for continued monitoring. Renal function tests showed transient elevation of urea (143 mg/dL) and creatinine (2 mg/dL), which gradually improved with supportive treatment. Ultrasound revealed increased cortical echogenicity in both kidneys. He developed a mild cough with wheezing, which resolved after nebulization with salbutamol and budesonide.

[b00020] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
By the day of discharge, his urea (90 mg/dL) and creatinine (1.54 mg/dL) had returned to normal. He was stable, had normal urine output, and was discharged after 8 days with a prescription for Foracort via mask and spacer, along with follow‐up instructions.

[b00021] Case Report / Case II / Methods (Investigations and Treatment)
After the poisoning, the younger son was taken to a nearby hospital, where he was managed with oxygen, ceftriaxone, ranitidine, and hydrocortisone before being referred to CMCTH after 3.5 h. Upon arrival at CMCTH's ER, the child was in critical condition, with an unstable airway and audible stridor. His respiratory rate was 71 breaths/min, heart rate 195 bpm, temperature 101.9°F, and oxygen saturation (SpO2) was 91% on a facemask. Neurological examination showed hypertonia with dystonia, a GCS score of 14/15, and muscle power of 3/5. The child had crusted lips and erythematous oral mucosa.

[b00022] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
Her condition stabilized on the fifth day, and she was transferred to the high‐dependency unit (HDU) for continued care including oral potassium supplementation. On June 7, after psychiatric evaluation, she was diagnosed with a severe depressive episode (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale: 23 and Suicidal Intent scale: 17). Gastroenterology and ENT consultations were provided due to her throat pain, and she was treated with sucralfate syrup and antiseptic mouthwash. After 19 days of treatment and counseling, she was discharged on June 19, 2023.

[b00023] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
Note: The cases described occurred between May and July 2023, prior to the widespread clinical replacement of ranitidine following global withdrawal notices. Ranitidine was still available in some local hospitals at the time and was administered as part of initial emergency management before transfer.

[b00024] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
During the elder son's stay in the PICU, he had one more episode of vomiting, which was treated with IV Ondansetron. He remained hemodynamically stable with normal input and output and was soon transferred to the ward for continued monitoring. He was treated with IV Pantoprazole, IV PCM, and Ondansetron IV. Blood tests showed elevated creatinine (2 mg/dL) and urea (143 mg/dL), while an ultrasound revealed increased cortical echogenicity in both kidneys. His renal function tests after 2 days showed a slight improvement in urea but worsening creatinine levels (Ur/Cr = 141/2.14 mg/dL). He developed a dry cough with wheezing, which was resolved after nebulization with salbutamol and budesonide.

[b00025] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
By the day of discharge, his urea (90 mg/dL) and creatinine (1.54 mg/dL) had returned to normal. He was stable, had normal urine output, and was discharged after 8 days with a prescription for Foracort via mask and spacer, along with follow‐up instructions.

[b00026] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
The younger son was treated with intravenous dexamethasone, adrenaline nebulization, intravenous paracetamol, and resuscitated with Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) improving his oxygen saturation to 95%. Despite efforts, IV cannulation failed due to vein collapse, and no blood samples were collected. After counseling, the child was shifted to the PICU, where CPAP, dexamethasone, and adrenaline nebulization were continued.

[b00027] Case Report / Case II / Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)
The child developed one episode of generalized tonic–clonic seizure, which was aborted with intramuscular midazolam. The child had worsening of sensorium with central cyanosis. The child had respiratory desaturation. The child was resuscitated with bag and mask ventilation followed by endotracheal intubation. At around 6 h of admission in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), the child gradually developed bradycardia with heart rate reaching < 60 b/m. Unfortunately, despite resuscitation efforts, the child could not be revived and was declared dead 7 h after admission.

[b00028] Discussion
Both of our reported cases were of intentional ingestion, which underscores that although organophosphate compounds such as Methyl‐parathion remain the leading agents responsible for self‐poisoning in Nepal [], clinicians must also be vigilant for poisoning due to other easily accessible herbicides like PQ, which continue to cause significant morbidity and mortality in rural settings.

[b00029] Discussion
Following oral ingestion, PQ is rapidly distributed to multiple organs, with marked accumulation in the lungs, where it releases oxygen free radicals leading to pulmonary fibrosis, nephrotoxicity, and hepatocellular injury [].

[b00030] Discussion
The PQ concentration in lung parenchyma can reach 10–20 times that of plasma because of active uptake of PQ by alveolar epithelium through the polyamine transport system [].

[b00031] Discussion
Local effects typically include ulcerations and erosions of the lips, oral cavity, and esophagus, patients complaining of sore throat, odynophagia, or dysphagia [].

[b00032] Discussion
Depending on the plasma concentration and ingested amount, PQ poisoning is classified as follows: Mild toxicity (< 20 mg/L): Limited to erosions of the tongue, oral mucosa, and corrosive injury to the gastrointestinal tract. Moderate toxicity (20–50 mg/L): Leads to renal tubular necrosis, hepatic necrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Death within 2 and 3 weeks. Fulminant toxicity (> 50 mg/L): Results in multi organ dysfunction, shock, death within 3 days [].

[b00033] Discussion
All patients ingested more than 20 mL of 24% PQ, which corresponds to the acute fulminant category, leading to severe complications like acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis, toxic hepatitis, respiratory distress, and secondary infections. Among the four patients, two survived, emphasizing the lethality of the compound even with aggressive management. Diagnosis was primarily based on exposure history, clinical features, and oropharyngeal burns.

[b00034] Discussion
The Dithionite urine test, used in the first case, provides a rapid qualitative confirmation of exposure, though it has limited prognostic utility and may not influence immediate management decisions [].

[b00035] Discussion
Daily monitoring of renal and hepatic function, electrolytes, hematologic parameters, and serial chest radiographs is essential to detect evolving complications such as acute respiratory distress syndrome, alveolitis, pneumomediastinum, or pneumothorax. In patients with abdominal pain, serum amylase and lipase should be assessed to exclude toxin‐induced pancreatitis [].

[b00036] Discussion
Currently, no specific antidote exists for PQ poisoning. Treatment depends on the ingested amount and time since exposure. Standard resuscitation principles and management of airway, breathing, and circulation remain essential.

[b00037] Discussion
In our cases, adjunctive antioxidant therapy was administered, including high‐dose Vitamin C (ascorbic acid 1 g intravenously twice daily). Vitamin C acts as free radical scavengers, aiming to mitigate oxidative damage induced by PQ's redox cycling mechanism. The rationale for antioxidant therapy lies in its potential to limit pulmonary and systemic oxidative injury, which is central to PQ toxicity pathogenesis. However, its clinical efficacy remains uncertain. In our series, two out of four patients survived, suggesting partial benefit, possibly due to earlier hospital presentation, lower ingested dose, and timely initiation of antioxidants and corticosteroids, whereas the non‐survivors presented late with fulminant multi‐organ involvement, limiting therapeutic efficacy [, ].

[b00038] Discussion
Currently, no specific antidote exists for PQ poisoning. Management focuses on limiting absorption, enhancing elimination, and mitigating oxidative damage. Early gastrointestinal decontamination with activated charcoal or Fuller's earth (preferably within the first hour) is recommended, often combined with a cathartic such as sorbitol. Hemodialysis may be indicated for acute renal failure but does not significantly enhance PQ clearance. Supplemental oxygen is avoided unless hypoxia is severe, as it can exacerbate oxidative injury. Adjunctive therapy with high‐dose antioxidants (vitamins C and E), N‐acetylcysteine, corticosteroids, and cyclophosphamide has shown variable success, with some evidence supporting corticosteroid–cyclophosphamide combinations in reducing pulmonary fibrosis. Ultimately, treatment should be individualized according to the patient's condition, timing of ingestion, and available evidence [].

[b00039] Discussion
In PQ poisoning, survival is highly dependent on the time interval between ingestion and initiation of treatment, often referred to as the ‘golden time’ window. This critical period typically falls within the first 2–4 h after ingestion. During this window, gastrointestinal decontamination with activated charcoal or Fuller's earth can significantly reduce systemic absorption of PQ, thereby improving survival chances. Once PQ is absorbed and begins to undergo redox cycling, generating reactive oxygen species and causing irreversible cellular injury, the effectiveness of interventions such as hemoperfusion or antioxidant therapy markedly declines. Patients presenting after 6 h or with positive urine dithionite tests generally have a poor prognosis due to extensive oxidative damage and tissue accumulation of PQ. Therefore, early recognition, rapid transport to a medical facility, and immediate initiation of decontamination represent the only modifiable factors that can influence the outcome in both intentional and accidental PQ poisoning [, ].

[b00040] Conclusion
PQ poisoning remains a critical public health concern in Nepal due to its high mortality rate and lack of a specific antidote. The aforementioned cases highlight the diverse clinical presentations, rapid progression, and multisystem complications associated with acute PQ ingestion. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on clinical suspicion and available toxicological tests such as the urine dithionite test, though these are often limited in practicality. Timely and aggressive supportive care, including decontamination, organ support, and psychiatric evaluation, is essential to improve survival outcomes.

[b00041] Conclusion
Beyond individual patient management, these cases underscore the urgent need for stronger national pesticide regulation, stricter control over PQ distribution, and enforcement of safe handling policies. Integrating community‐based mental health screening, early intervention programs, and suicide prevention strategies—especially in agrarian and rural populations—should be prioritized within Nepal's public health framework.

[b00042] Conclusion
Given its lethal potential even in small doses, early recognition, public education, and a coordinated multisectoral approach involving healthcare providers, policymakers, and agricultural authorities are crucial to reduce the burden of PQ‐related morbidity and mortality.
