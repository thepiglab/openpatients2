Bacteroides fragilis as an unusual cause of neonatal pyogenic arthritis: A case report

[b00001] Abstract
We present a rare case of neonatal bone infection caused by Bacteroides fragilis without predisposing risk factors in an otherwise healthy newborn. The infant initially presented with symptoms of sepsis, including fever, irritability, and reduced limb movement, prompting an extensive workup that eventually revealed Bacteroides fragilis in joint fluid PCR analysis. Treatment involved multi-agent antibiotics targeting anaerobic organisms and a joint washout procedure. This case highlights the importance of molecular diagnostics in detecting uncommon pathogens in neonatal bone infections and guiding effective antibiotic therapy. This report contributes valuable knowledge to understanding and managing bone and joint infections in neonatal populations.

[b00002] Case summary
We report the case of a 4-week-old female neonate who initially presented at 3 weeks of age with fever (Tmax 39.5 °C), irritability, poor feeding, decreased urine output, and reduced movement of the left lower limb. A full septic workup was performed to evaluate for serious bacterial infection in the neonatal period, including lumbar puncture. Initial investigations (Table 1) revealed a WBC count of 14 × 10⁹/L (neutrophils 6.7 × 10⁹/L) and CRP of 24 mg/L. Blood and CSF cultures were negative. Urinalysis showed positive leukocytes (with negative urine culture), and respiratory PCR detected human rhinovirus/enterovirus. Hip ultrasound demonstrated a small left-sided effusion (Image 1). The patient was initially managed as a case of neonatal sepsis and completed 7 days of intravenous ampicillin and cefotaxime. One week later, she re-presented with irritability and limited movement of the left hip. On admission, her CBC was within normal limits and CRP was 5 mg/dL. MRI findings were consistent with left hip septic arthritis and periarticular pyomyositis (Image 2). Empirical intravenous ceftriaxone and vancomycin were initiated, and she underwent left hip joint washout and drainage. Both blood and joint fluid cultures were negative; however, PCR testing of the joint fluid was positive for Bacteroides fragilis. Antibiotic therapy was subsequently modified with the addition of metronidazole and later streamlined to piperacillin-tazobactam to complete a total of 4 weeks of antimicrobial therapy from the date of surgical drainage.

[b00013] Case summary
To the best of our knowledge, Bacteroides fragilis is an uncommon cause of bone and joint infections in neonates, particularly in the absence of identifiable risk factors predisposing to pyogenic septic arthritis or osteomyelitis.

[b00014] Discussion
Bacteroides species is a genus of gram-negative, obligate anaerobic, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped bacteria. The taxonomy of Bacteroides species has been revised over the last decade, and currently, over 30 species of Bacteroides have been recognized Bacteroides species can be transmitted from the mother to the newborn during delivery through the birth canal, establishing a relationship with the host early in life,around 10 days of life, Bacteroides species become part of the neonate's microbiota. In breast-fed infants, Bifidobacterium is often the predominant isolate until weaning. Bacterial interactions with host intestinal cells are supported by cellular and stromal components, blood, mucins, and neurons within the intestinal mucosa . Anaerobes are typically normal commensals, but infections can occur following skin or mucosal injuries,Infections have been reported in neonates, including cellulitis at the fetal monitoring site, scalp lacerations, neonatal aspiration pneumonia, bacteremia, meningitis, conjunctivitis, and omphalitis . Deep infections have also been reported in older individuals with underlying conditions like diabetes. Anaerobes can lead to various musculoskeletal conditions such as myonecrosis, necrotizing fasciitis, septic arthritis, infective spondylitis, and osteomyelitis , , .

[b00015] Discussion
B. fragilis is highly resistant to the antibiotics such as kanamycin, vancomycin, and colistin. The use of a simple disk diffusion assay for these antibiotics is often part of the identification process of the gram negative aneorbic organisms . Isolation of Bacteroides fragilis in the blood has been linked to a high mortality risk, with reported rates of bloodstream infections related mortality as high as 45 %, 16 % of which are attributed to resistant strains. Therefore, empirical antibiotic selection should be guided by knowledge of antimicrobial resistance patterns, including gram staining to identify commonly encountered mixed infections. A prudent approach involves using broad-spectrum antibiotics effective against both aerobic and anaerobic organisms. Bacteroides fragilis produces Beta-lactamase enzymes, rendering it resistant to penicillin. Other antibiotics like Cefoxitin, moxifloxacin, and clindamycin have limited effectiveness against Bacteroides fragilis. Metronidazole is the preferred antibiotic for anaerobic infections as it shows very low resistance rates in surveys, ranging from 0.5 % to 7.8 % . Given varying resistance patterns according to the geographic regions, establishing a robust surveillance system is critical. Antibiotics with good anaerobic coverage include carbapenems, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, metronidazole, and chloramphenicol. However, resistance has emerged in B. fragilis and related strains like P. distasonis, B. ovatus, B. thetaiotaomicron, B. uniformis, and P. vulgatus, against clindamycin, cephamycins, and moxifloxacin .

[b00016] Discussion
Pyogenic arthritis of non-prosthetic joints is a medical emergency with a high potential for rapid joint destruction, irreversible loss of function, and even mortality , Regarding our case report on neonatal bone infections, diagnosing and evaluating the condition can be complex due to the varied clinical presentations. Neonatal bone infections typically manifest in one of two clinical syndromes. The first is a subacute presentation characterized by mild fever, transient bacteremia, and restricted joint movement known as "pseudoparalysis." The second syndrome involves infants showing signs of systemic illness, such as severe symptoms, joint swelling, redness, tenderness, and elevated inflammatory markers, especially in cases of S. aureus musculoskeletal infections. The isolation of pathogens like Serratia. marcescens should prompt consideration of underlying immune deficiencies like chronic granulomatous disease. In a study focusing on neonatal osteomyelitis with 121 reviewed cases, only a fraction of infants presented with fever (34 %), with significant clinical symptoms including swelling (64 %), pseudoparalysis (55 %), tenderness (32 %), erythema (30 %), and irritability or lethargy (36 %) . Certain neonates face an increased risk of bone and joint infections, as noted in previous studies. Factors that elevate this risk include low birth weight (< 2500 g), gestational age of less than 37 weeks, emergency cesarean delivery, neonatal surgeries for congenital malformations, respiratory distress syndrome, hyperbilirubinemia, umbilical vein catheterization, perinatal asphyxia, skin lacerations on the scalp following vacuum-assisted delivery, and renal vein thrombosis. These factors have been associated with an elevated likelihood of bone infections in neonates. Identifying the causative pathogen in bone infections remains a clinical challenge,while blood cultures detects the pathogen in only 60 % of children with bone infections, while subperiosteal metaphyseal pus cultures identify the pathogen in about 70 % of cases . A Systemic review published by Neelam harsha et al. , that evaluated 16 articles and nearly 300 neonates with bone and joint infections identified Staphylococcus aureus (40 %) as the major causative agent followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (18 %) while the others had Candida, Streptococcus spp, haemophilus influenza, pseudomonas and proteus ssp and none was identified to have Bacteroides fragilis .

[b00017] Discussion
The Culture remains the gold standard for diagnosing infections. Utilizing blood culture bottles for joint fluid inoculation can increase pathogen detection compared to conventional agar plating methods, due to enhanced volume cultured and reduced potential growth inhibitors like pre-used antibiotics. Advanced diagnostic methods like 16S rRNA gene PCR with next-generation sequencing (NGS) and metagenomic shotgun sequencing directly from joint fluid specimens can improve sensitivity, especially in prosthetic joint infections. The FDA approved the BioFire Joint Infection Panel (JI panel) in 2022, which is a multiplex PCR assay targeting 39 pathogens, including bacteria (Gram-positive and Gram-negative), yeast, and common antimicrobial resistance genes linked to these organisms. This method requires minimal sample preparation and handling, needing only 200 µL of sample and offering a quick turnaround time of approximately 1 h. It is a convenient approach supporting early diagnosis and targeted therapy . A retrospective study assessed the efficacy of targeted molecular PCR in 78 children with community-acquired bone and joint infections. The study showed an enhanced detection rate of the causative agent, particularly with improved identification of Kingella kingae, especially in children under 5 years old. Kingella kingae is recognized as an emerging pathogen causing bone infections in younger children, posing challenges for isolation due to its fastidious nature. The study emphasizes that PCR techniques can enhance diagnostic accuracy, extending their utility beyond infancy .

[b00018] Discussion
In our case, the conventional method failed to isolate the pathogenic agent effectively, highlighting the superior sensitivity of PCR techniques in detecting bone and joint infections especially in cases that are pre-treated with antibiotics. The sensitivity of automated culture techniques in the conventional method typically ranges from 40 % to 80 %, which may decrease further in cases involving prior antibiotic use, biofilm-forming agents, or mixed infections. Conversely, PCR techniques exhibit a higher sensitivity of 70–90 %, proving its effectiveness, especially in pre-treated patients and infections caused by fastidious organisms. Previous reports suggest a substantial concordance of approximately 88.4 % between cultures and PCR techniques .

[b00019] Discussion
The optimal treatment duration for bone infections in younger patients remains a topic of debate. While some sources recommend prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy, international guidelines generally advocate for a shorter course of intravenous antibiotics followed by oral antibiotics in non-complex cases. This approach, lasting 4–6 weeks, is favored to reduce hospital stays, costs, and risks associated with prolonged intravenous treatment . The latest guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) suggest a more concise treatment approach for children with acute bacterial arthritis that does not involve bones. The recommendation advises a shortened duration of intravenous and oral therapy, totaling 10–14 days after achieving a prompt clinical improvement from the initial treatment . In our recent case, we opted to administer broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, such as piperacillin-tazobactam, for 4 weeks due to challenges in pathogen identification and susceptibility testing.

[b00020] Conclusion
Bacteroides fragilis is an uncommon cause of neonatal bone and joint infections, particularly in otherwise healthy infants without identifiable predisposing factors however it may also be due to under reported cases or even further diagnostic challenges as we demonstrated in our case report. Although uncommon, such infections can lead to considerable morbidity and potential mortality if not promptly recognized. Early detection through molecular and advanced metagenomic diagnostic techniques is essential for accurate identification and targeted antimicrobial therapy. The absence of prior reports of Bacteroides fragilis-associated bone infections in healthy neonates highlights the evolving role of advanced diagnostics in broadening our understanding of the microbiological spectrum of osteoarticular infections in early infancy.
