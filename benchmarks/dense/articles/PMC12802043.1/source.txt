Morganella morganii Urinary Tract Infection in Jordanian Children: Case Series from a Tertiary Hospital

[b00001] Abstract / Background
Urinary tract infections (UTIs) pose significant risks to children's health, with potential for kidney damage if untreated. Morganella morganii is an uncommon but important pathogen in pediatric UTIs. We aimed to describe the clinical characteristics, risk factors, and antibiotic susceptibility patterns of M. morganii UTIs in Jordanian children.

[b00002] Abstract / Materials and Methods
We conducted a retrospective review of pediatric patients (aged 0–18 years) diagnosed with M. morganii UTIs at King Abdullah University Hospital from 2017 to 2022. Data collected included demographics, clinical presentation, laboratory findings, imaging studies, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and treatment offered.

[b00003] Abstract / Results
Nine patients were identified, 77.8% of whom were female, with a median age of 3.35 years (interquartile range, 2-14.5). The most common symptoms were fever (55.6%), malodorous urine (44.4%), and vomiting (22.2%). A high prevalence of underlying urological abnormalities, particularly neurogenic bladder and myelomeningocele (44% each), was noted. Eight cases involved lower UTIs. M. morganii isolates were uniformly sensitive to amikacin, cefepime, and piperacillin-tazobactam but resistant to amoxicillin-clavulanate and nitrofurantoin.

[b00004] Abstract / Conclusion
M. morganii should be considered in pediatric UTIs, especially among children with urinary tract anomalies or catheter use. Awareness of its distinct resistance profile is critical for selecting appropriate empiric therapy.

[b00005] Introduction
Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a common and potentially serious condition in childhood that can result in permanent kidney damage []. The incidence of initial symptomatic UTIs is highest in both males and females during the first year of life, after which it gradually declines [], highlighting the importance of including UTI screening in the evaluation of febrile infants []. Clinical presentation varies by age, with neonates and infants often showing subtle or nonspecific signs requiring thorough evaluation [], whereas older children more commonly present with distinctive features that help distinguish upper from lower UTIs []. Risk factors include anatomical, neurological, and functional abnormalities, as well as urological anomalies such as vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) and genetic predispositions []; in older children, trauma, diabetes, and sexual activity—particularly in females—also contribute to increased risk [].

[b00006] Introduction
Escherichia coli remains the predominant cause of pediatric UTIs; accounting for 80–90% of cases across age groups []. Other pathogens, however, are increasingly implicated in complicated or healthcare-associated infections. These include Gram-negative bacteria such as Klebsiella, Proteus mirabilis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Morganella morganii, as well as Gram-positive organisms such as Enterococci and Staphylococcus saprophyticus [].

[b00007] Introduction
M. morganii is a Gram-negative, non-lactose-fermenting bacillus within the Proteae tribe and is part of the normal intestinal flora of humans and animals []. Although considered an uncommon uropathogen, it has been implicated in a wide spectrum of infections, including UTIs, surgical wound infections, peritonitis, and central nervous system infections, particularly among immunocompromised or hospitalized patients [].

[b00008] Introduction
Although rare in children, pediatric case series demonstrate its relevance. In a Turkish cohort of 2,866 culture-proven pediatric UTIs, M. morganii accounted for 11 cases (0.4%) []. A Chinese series similarly reported 11 pediatric cases, most occurring in children with nephrotic syndrome or immunosuppression []. In older adults, it has been identified as the sixth most common urinary pathogen among nursing home residents [], highlighting its role in vulnerable populations across the lifespan.

[b00009] Introduction
The importance of M. morganii lies in its distinctive resistance profile. It carries an inducible chromosomal AmpC β-lactamase that may be upregulated during therapy, leading to failure of third-generation cephalosporins []. It also exhibits intrinsic resistance to ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, nitrofurantoin, and many first- and second-generation cephalosporins []. Recent reports describe extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production and, rarely, carbapenemase-producing strains [], reflecting its potential as an opportunistic multidrug-resistant pathogen []. In addition to its potential to cause invasive disease in immunocompromised or comorbid children.

[b00010] Introduction
Accordingly, this study aims to describe the clinical features, risk factors, and antimicrobial susceptibility of M. morganii UTIs in children at a tertiary referral hospital, thereby providing insight into a rare but increasingly recognized uropathogen.

[b00011] Materials and Methods / 1. Study design and setting
This retrospective study was conducted at King Abdullah University Hospital, a tertiary referral center in northern Jordan. Medical records of pediatric patients (<18 years) diagnosed with UTI caused by M. morganii between October 2017 and January 2022 were reviewed.

[b00012] Materials and Methods / 2. Ethics statement
The study was approved by the scientific committee and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) committee at Jordan University of Science and Technology and King Abdullah University Hospital, with approval number [IRB no. 72/2023].

[b00013] Materials and Methods / 3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≤18 years; (2) symptomatic UTI with urine culture growing M. morganii; and (3) availability of clinical, laboratory, and treatment data.

[b00014] Materials and Methods / 3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Exclusion criteria included: (1) asymptomatic bacteriuria; (2) bag-only urine specimens (used for screening but not for diagnostic cultures); (3) polymicrobial cultures without a predominant M. morganii isolate; (4) cultures judged as contamination (e.g., mixed flora, low colony counts without pyuria); and (5) incomplete records that precluded outcome evaluation.

[b00015] Materials and Methods / 4. Case definition of UTI and recurrence
UTI was defined as the presence of compatible clinical symptoms together with a positive urine culture []. For midstream clean-catch samples, ≥10^(5) colony-forming units (CFU)/mL of M. morganii was considered significant, while ≥10^(4) CFU/mL was used for catheterized samples, and any growth from suprapubic aspiration was regarded as diagnostic []. Recurrence was defined as ≥2 episodes of UTI within 6 months or ≥3 episodes within 12 months, and relapse within 2 weeks with the same organism and similar susceptibility pattern [].

[b00016] Materials and Methods / 5. Urine collection and contamination control
To minimize contamination, toilet-trained children provided midstream clean-catch urine after perineal cleansing. In non–toilet-trained children, urine was obtained via catheterization if initial bag urinalysis suggested infection; bag specimens alone were not cultured. Mixed cultures or low-count bacteriuria without evidence of pyuria were considered contamination and excluded from analysis.

[b00017] Materials and Methods / 6. Identification of M. morganii UTIs and microbial analysis of urine samples
Urine samples were collected using standard techniques appropriate for the child’s age and clinical status. Initial evaluation included physical and chemical urinalysis, after which specimens were promptly processed, refrigerated when necessary, and re-cultured if delayed. Samples were inoculated on standard culture media and incubated for 24 hours, with extended incubation when clinically indicated. All cultures were assessed by a certified clinical microbiologist.

[b00018] Materials and Methods / 6. Identification of M. morganii UTIs and microbial analysis of urine samples
A semi-quantitative approach was used to interpret colony counts, with ≥10^(5) CFU/mL considered significant for clean-catch specimens, and lower thresholds applied for catheterized or suprapubic aspirates according to established pediatric criteria.

[b00019] Materials and Methods / 6. Identification of M. morganii UTIs and microbial analysis of urine samples
Identification of Morganella isolates involved recognition of characteristic off-white, opaque colonies on agar. Microscopic evaluation demonstrated Gram-negative, short, straight motile rods with peritrichous flagella. Biochemical testing confirmed Morganella species, and when necessary, the VITEK^(®) 2 (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) automated system was employed for species-level confirmation. All results were verified by the clinical microbiology team and uploaded into the electronic medical records.

[b00020] Materials and Methods / 7. Statistical analysis
Data collected were entered into a pre-prepared Excel sheet. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Given the nature of the case-series design, inferential statistical analysis was not applicable.

During the study period, a total of 834 episodes of pediatric UTI were diagnosed and managed at our hospital, of which 9 (1.1%) were attributed to Morganella infection. The study cohort included seven females and two males, with a median age of 3.35 years (interquartile range, 2.0–14.5). [b00032] Results
The most common comorbidities were myelomeningocele and neurogenic bladder, each present in 44% of patients. Recurrent UTIs were documented in five patients, four of whom were on prophylactic antibiotics and performed intermittent catheterization.

[b00033] Results
Clinical presentations varied, with fever being the most frequently reported symptom (5 patients, 55.6%), followed by malodorous urine (4 patients, 44.4%) and vomiting (2 patients, 22.2%). None of the patients were immunocompromised. Unique presentations included hypoactivity and respiratory distress in a 2-month-old infant (Case 9) and flank pain in a 16-year-old male (Case 2).

[b00034] Results
M. morganii was consistently isolated from urine cultures at the time of admission, with the exception of one outpatient case (Case 2), which did not require hospitalization. No isolates were detected from other body fluids, including blood, cerebrospinal fluid, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, pleural effusion, peritoneal effusion, secretions, or exudates.

[b00035] Results
Chronic medications included prophylactic antibiotics, antihypertensives, calcium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, and solifenacin. Interventions included intermittent catheterization and the use of double J catheters. One patient experienced two distinct M. morganii UTIs, the first at age of 13 and the second at age of 16. Because the episodes were separated by nearly three years, it did not meet the study definition of recurrence. The susceptibility profiles of both isolates were similar. However, given the long interval between episodes, these were most consistent with independent reinfections rather than relapse or biofilm-related persistence. On the second episode and due to persistent pyuria in a patient with a neurogenic bladder, an atrophied kidney, and a history of Morganella infection, treating physician opt to continue antibiotics for 20 days.

These patients exhibited normal or mildly elevated leukocyte counts, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were not consistently assessed. Blood cultures remained negative across all cases.

[b00077] Discussion
UTIs caused by M. morganii have been recognized since the late 1930s. Early reports from the 1950s and 1960s identified it primarily in nosocomial infections, and notably, a cluster outbreak in 1977 showed that 39% of M. morganii infections were urinary in origin, while 61% were wound-related. Although M. morganii is part of the normal intestinal flora of humans, animals, and reptiles, it remains an uncommon clinical pathogen, more frequently reported following surgical procedures or in nosocomial settings.

[b00078] Discussion
While most studies have focused on M. morganii infections in adults, a few have investigated its occurrence in the pediatric population. In the current study, M. morganii was identified as a cause of community acquired UTIs, aligning with similar findings in studies from Turkey and China in 2020 and 2022, respectively [].

[b00079] Discussion
Comparison with other pediatric studies reveals both differences and similarities in the clinical manifestations of UTIs caused by M. morganii. Manifestations vary with age, and in the Turkish population, irritability (45.5%) and dysuria (45.5%) were prominent symptoms in infants []. Conversely, Chinese children mostly lacked specific clinical symptoms, with fever being the only notable manifestation []. In the current study, urinary symptoms, including malodorous urine, were observed in 55.6% of cases. Malodorous urine in children is a clinical feature that may raise suspicion for UTI, though its presence alone is not diagnostic. Therefore, clinicians should consider malodorous urine as one of several factors when evaluating young children for UTI, particularly in the context of other symptoms like fever without an apparent source [].

[b00080] Discussion
Urinalysis results also displayed variation across studies. In Turkey, leukocyturia was present in 81.8% and hematuria in 45.5% of children with M. morganii UTI [], while in a Chinese cohort leukocyturia was observed in 100% and hematuria in 36.4% []. In our series, leukocyturia was documented in only 66.7% and hematuria in 44.4% of cases, a lower pyuria rate than previously reported. This discrepancy may reflect small sample sizes or differences in comorbidities among cohorts. More broadly, pyuria is not universal in pediatric UTIs: approximately 20% of febrile children with culture-proven UTI lack pyuria [], and non-E. coli uropathogens are consistently less likely to elicit pyuria compared with E. coli []. Taken together, these findings indicate that while pyuria is common, its absence does not exclude M. morganii infection. Clinicians should therefore interpret negative urinalysis with caution, especially in high-risk children.

[b00081] Discussion
Notably, the documentation of CRP levels in M. morganii UTIs exhibited inconsistencies across studies. The Turkish study noted that 45.5% of children had normal CRP and leukocyte levels, while the Chinese study reported normal or slightly elevated leukocyte counts and procalcitonin levels, along with normal CRP levels. In the current study, CRP levels were not conclusively documented [].

[b00082] Discussion
Previous investigations have identified risk factors for M. morganii infection, including advanced age, repeated hospitalizations, nursing home residence, urethral catheter use, and prematurity []. In our cohort, nearly half of the children required intermittent catheterization, and several had underlying urogenital abnormalities. This differs from the Turkish series, in which none of the children had hospital-acquired risk factors or conditions suggestive of bacteremia/sepsis; only two had chronic diseases, and all cases were considered community-acquired, likely arising from the natural environment or gastrointestinal flora []. In contrast, the Chinese series reported that 72.7% of children had nephrotic syndrome and 54.5% were receiving immunosuppressive therapy []. None of our patients were immunocompromised, reinforcing that in our setting, local urinary tract factors and catheter use appear to play a larger role than systemic disease. Together, these differences highlight how risk profiles vary across populations, with community-acquired infections predominating in Turkey, systemic disease driving cases in China, and urological abnormalities and catheterization contributing most in our cohort.

[b00083] Discussion
Regarding antibiotic susceptibility, our findings were consistent with international data, showing high sensitivity to aminoglycosides and broad-spectrum beta-lactams. Importantly, awareness of the bacterium's intrinsic resistance patterns is critical for appropriate empiric therapy, particularly as resistance to commonly used oral agents (e.g., nitrofurantoin, amoxicillin-clavulanate) was universal. Susceptibility rate for meropenem was not included in the current study due to unavailability.

[b00084] Discussion
Based on our sensitivity results, we recommend using an amikacin-based regimen in patients with normal renal function or ertapenem in patients with impaired kidney function as empirical treatment for individuals at high risk of Morganella infection. High-risk groups include patients with congenital urogenital anomalies, those with urinary catheters, and patients with a history of previous Morganella infections.

[b00085] Discussion
A key limitation of our study is that ESBL and AmpC β-lactamase production were not tested in our Morganella isolates. In addition to the retrospective design, single-center setting, and small sample size. Nevertheless, it adds valuable insight into an underrecognized pediatric pathogen. Future multicenter prospective studies are necessary to better define the epidemiology, risk factors, and outcomes associated with pediatric M. morganii infections.

[b00086] Discussion
Although M. morganii remains an uncommon pediatric uropathogen, our study adds value by drawing attention to the situations where it becomes clinically relevant. The children affected in our cohort were not random: most had identifiable vulnerabilities such as urogenital abnormalities or reliance on catheterization. These findings argue for a more nuanced approach to empiric therapy in pediatrics—one that takes into account not only the dominance of E. coli but also the possibility of less common, intrinsically resistant pathogens in high-risk groups.

[b00087] Discussion
This distinction has practical consequences. Agents often prescribed for presumed UTIs in children, including nitrofurantoin and amoxicillin-clavulanate, are unreliable against M. morganii due to intrinsic resistance. In children with recurrent infections, anatomic abnormalities, or catheter dependence, empiric regimens should therefore avoid these drugs and instead favor agents with demonstrated activity, such as third-generation cephalosporins or aminoglycosides, reserving carbapenems for severe or multidrug-resistant cases.

[b00088] Discussion
By contextualizing susceptibility data within recognizable clinical risk groups, this study provides clinicians with a clearer framework: when faced with a child whose UTI risk profile departs from the norm, M. morganii should be considered—not as a common pathogen, but as an important one.

[b00089] Discussion
M. morganii should be recognized as a potential uropathogen in pediatric patients, particularly those with underlying urinary tract anomalies, catheter dependence, or previous UTIs. This organism is notable for its intrinsic resistance to multiple commonly used antibiotics, including penicillins (such as amoxicillin/augmentin), nitrofurantoin, and many first- and second-generation cephalosporins, which can limit the effectiveness of standard empiric regimens. Awareness of these resistance patterns is essential when selecting empiric therapy, and clinicians are advised to review prior urine culture results and local susceptibility data to guide antibiotic choice. Importantly, the absence of pyuria should not lead to the exclusion of UTI in pediatric patients, particularly for M. morganii infections, which may present with subtle or atypical laboratory findings. Early recognition and targeted therapy are therefore critical to prevent treatment failure, recurrence, or complications in this vulnerable population.

[b00090] Discussion
Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to better characterize the epidemiology, risk factors, clinical outcomes, and management of M. morganii UTIs in children.
