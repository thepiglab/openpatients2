A Giant Traumatic Pneumatocele Manifesting as Hemoptysis in an Adolescent: A Case Report

[b00001] Abstract
Traumatic pneumatocele (TP) is a rare, benign pulmonary lesion that primarily affects children and young adults following blunt chest trauma. We present the case of a 16-year-old male judo athlete who developed persistent hemoptysis. Imaging identified a large hydropneumothorax. During video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, a cystic lesion was discovered in the left lower lobe, and intraoperative bronchofibroscopy revealed a minor bronchopleural fistula. A wedge resection was performed, resulting in an uncomplicated recovery; the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. This case underscores the rarity of complicated TP necessitating surgical intervention and highlights the importance of considering TP in young patients with blunt chest trauma.

[b00002] Case report
A 16-year-old male (height: 172 cm; weight: 82 kg) with no significant medical history was referred to the Department of Thoracic Surgery due to persistent hemoptysis and abnormal findings on chest radiography and chest computed tomography (CT). He had recently begun training as a judo athlete—approximately 2 weeks prior—and reported several episodes of hemoptysis that began a week earlier. There was no prior history of pulmonary disease or symptoms suggestive of bullous lung disease. Both physical examination and laboratory investigations were unremarkable. A routine school health screening, including chest radiography performed 1 year prior upon entering high school, revealed no abnormalities.

[b00003] Case report
The initial chest X-ray demonstrated an air-fluid level in the left thoracic cavity (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced chest CT revealed a large, localized hydropneumothorax occupying the posterior pleural space of the left hemithorax (Fig. 1). Based on these findings, the preoperative differential diagnosis included extrapleural hydropneumothorax and empyema.

[b00004] Case report
Given the lesion’s size, mass effect, and infection risk, the patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for diagnostic exploration and resection. Intraoperatively, a large cystic structure was identified, extending from the posterior basal segment to the superior segment of the left lower lobe (Fig. 2). The lesion was excised using an endoscopic stapler.

[b00005] Case report
Intraoperative bronchofibroscopy, performed via a double-lumen endotracheal tube, revealed partial obstruction of the left bronchus by old blood clots originating from the cyst. Some clots had migrated to contralateral bronchial segments. These findings demonstrated communication between the cystic cavity and the bronchial tree, indicating a minor bronchopleural fistula.

[b00006] Case report
The surgery proceeded without complications. The resected specimen measured 21.5×10.5×3.7 cm and weighed 112 g. Histopathological analysis demonstrated intra-alveolar hemorrhage and emphysematous changes, without evidence of infection or malignancy.

[b00007] Case report
A chest drain was inserted during the procedure and removed on postoperative day 2. The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged on the same day, with no immediate postoperative complications.

[b00008] Case report
Written informed consent for publication of this case report and accompanying images was obtained from the patient’s legal caregiver.

[b00009] Discussion
Traumatic pneumatocele (TP), also known as a traumatic pulmonary pseudocyst, is an uncommon benign pulmonary lesion observed in patients following blunt chest trauma. It is characterized by a thin-walled, air-filled cavity within the lung parenchyma that lacks an epithelial lining and may contain air, fluid, or blood. TPs predominantly occur in males under 30 years of age, accounting for approximately 75%–85% of reported cases. This predominance is likely due to the increased compliance and flexibility of the thoracic wall in younger individuals [,]. Such anatomical characteristics facilitate the transmission of external force to the lung parenchyma during trauma, thereby increasing the risk of injury [].

[b00010] Discussion
The pathophysiology of TP involves a 2-step mechanism. Initially, blunt chest trauma rapidly compresses the lung parenchyma, causing barotrauma and tissue disruption []. This is followed by a sudden decompression phase, during which elastic recoil creates increased negative intrathoracic pressure []. The combination of these forces leads to alveolar and interstitial tearing, forming cavities filled with air. Repetitive cycles of compression and decompression may further contribute to lesion development.

[b00011] Discussion
Chest radiographs have a limited sensitivity (approximately 24%) for detecting TP, largely due to its delayed appearance and subtle findings []. In contrast, CT provides significantly higher sensitivity, with reported values up to 96%. CT is thus the preferred modality for identifying pseudocysts, assessing lesion size and location, and detecting potential complications []. In the present case, both chest radiography and CT were performed; however, neither clearly demonstrated communication with the lung parenchyma. The radiologic impression favored empyema, underscoring the diagnostic challenge posed by the presence of an air-fluid level and pleural thickening.

[b00012] Discussion
The differential diagnosis for TP includes lung abscess, pulmonary sequestration, diaphragmatic herniation of abdominal viscera, and post-pneumonic pneumatocele. A detailed trauma history is essential for accurate diagnosis. In this case, although there was no history of acute trauma, the patient had recently begun judo training, likely exposing him to repeated, subclinical blunt chest trauma. Clinical symptoms of TP are often nonspecific and may include cough, chest pain, dyspnea, fever, or even asymptomatic presentation [,]. Notably, hemoptysis was the only presenting symptom in this case.

[b00013] Discussion
While most TPs resolve spontaneously and can be managed conservatively with observation and serial imaging, surgical intervention may be warranted in selected cases—especially in patients with persistent air leak, enlarging lesions, infection, or diagnostic uncertainty [,]. Some studies classify TPs as either complicated or uncomplicated based on the presence of blood within the cavity [,]. One study reported a significantly prolonged mean resolution time for complicated TPs (145.8 days) compared to uncomplicated TPs (25 days) []. Furthermore, lesions larger than 2 cm were associated with recovery periods exceeding 122 days. These findings suggest that although conservative management is standard, larger or complicated lesions may justify early surgical intervention.

[b00014] Discussion
In the present case, surgical resection was performed due to the lesion’s considerable size, infection risk, and persistent hemoptysis. Intraoperative findings revealed a large cyst in the left lower lobe with an associated bronchopleural fistula—a rare but serious complication—along with significant clot burden obstructing the bronchial tree. Although lobectomy was initially considered, a wedge resection was ultimately chosen due to the patient’s young age and the unclear margins of the pneumatocele. Resection was accomplished with multiple applications of endo-staplers, allowing intraoperative adjustment of the resection line to minimize damage to surrounding lung parenchyma. The patient achieved complete resolution of the pneumatocele without postoperative complications and was discharged uneventfully on postoperative day 2. Outpatient follow-up showed no radiologic or clinical evidence of recurrence after 1 month. At 1 month postoperatively, follow- up contrast chest CT demonstrated complete resolution of the lesion, with no evidence of air-fluid space, empyema, or residual cavity. This case supports previous reports suggesting that large and complicated TP may be more effectively resolved through surgical intervention than conservative management.

[b00015] Discussion
Although TP has been reported in pediatric and adolescent populations, large lesions complicated by bronchopleural fistula that require surgical resection remain uncommon. Most cases described in the literature resolve without surgical intervention. This case adds to the limited number of reports of TP requiring operative management and highlights the importance of individualized decision-making based on lesion size, clinical presentation, and associated complications.
