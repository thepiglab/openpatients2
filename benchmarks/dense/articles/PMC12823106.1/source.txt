Emergence of Vitiligo during Treatment with Risankizumab-Rzaa for Plaque Psoriasis: A Case Report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Risankizumab-rzaa (Skyrizi) is a humanized monoclonal antibody (IgG1) approved for the treatment of severe plaque psoriasis. It selectively blocks the p19 subunit of the IL-23 pathway, leading to reduced keratinocyte proliferation reducing the inflammatory response.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
We present a case of de novo vitiligo in a patient being treated with risankizumab-rzaa (Skyrizi) for plaque psoriasis. Our patient had extensive plaque psoriasis affecting his trunk, upper extremities, and scalp, encompassing 30% of his total body surface area. He was treated with risankizumab-rzaa, an IL-23 inhibitor, with significant improvement of his psoriasis and residual hyperpigmented, post-inflammatory patches. In addition, he developed new-onset vitiligo, for which he declined treatment and was continued on risankizumab-rzaa. On subsequent follow-up over a year later, there were extensive areas of vitiligo now with a trichrome pattern suggestive of active vitiligo.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion
To our knowledge, no cases of de novo vitiligo associated with risankizumab-rzaa have been reported in the literature, and our patient may represent the first reported case. The role of IL-23 remains unclear, and our case represents the importance of further research to better elucidate IL-23’s role in vitiligo.

[b00004] Introduction
Risankizumab-rzaa (Skyrizi) is a humanized monoclonal antibody (IgG1) that selectively targets the p19 subunit of IL-23 and was FDA-approved for the treatment of severe plaque psoriasis in January 2022 []. IL-23 is a cytokine responsible for production and maintenance of pathogenic TH17 cells that secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL‐17 and IL‐22, key drivers of plaque psoriasis []. Blocking the IL-23 pathway leads to reduced keratinocyte proliferation and a decreased inflammatory response. We present a case of a 42-year-old male with severe plaque psoriasis treated with risankizumab-rzaa for over 2 years with resolution of active psoriasis. However, following treatment with risankizumab-rzaa, he developed several depigmented and hypopigmented patches on the back, left arm, fingers, and face consistent with active and unstable, trichrome vitiligo.

[b00005] Case Report
A 42-year-old man was diagnosed with moderate plaque psoriasis over 10 years ago which was initially treated with combination clobetasol 0.05% ointment mixed with calcipotriene 0.005% cream. His psoriasis was refractory to topicals, and he was unable to attend phototherapy sessions. Workup for consideration of systemic agents was performed at that time, but he was unable to follow-up due to loss of insurance.

[b00006] Case Report
He returned to clinic in November 2022 with extensive plaque psoriasis affecting his entire trunk, upper extremities, and scalp. On exam, there were well-demarcated, thick, scaly plaques on his entire back, trunk, dorsal upper extremities, and scalp, constituting over 30% of his total body surface area. He was also noted to have nail pitting and distal onycholysis. Treatment options were discussed with the patient, including phototherapy, and systemic agents like methotrexate, acitretin, and biologics. Phototherapy was not feasible due to his work schedule. He was not a candidate for methotrexate or acitretin due to his past history of elevated transaminases from alcohol use disorder. After shared decision making, he started on risankizumab-rzaa (Skyrizi), an IL-23 inhibitor, on November 16, 2022, with a loading dose of 150 mg subcutaneously at 0 and 4 weeks and a maintenance dose of 150 mg subcutaneously every 12 weeks thereafter.

[b00007] Case Report
He was seen for follow-up a year later in November 2023 and reported improvement in his psoriasis with no new lesions. Examination revealed multiple hyperpigmented patches in prior areas of psoriasis on the trunk and extremities consistent with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation with no evidence of active psoriasis, onycholysis, or nail pitting. However, he had new, depigmented patches on the back, left arm, nasolabial fold, left ear, left 3rd and 4th distal digits, and ankles. His plaque psoriasis on exam appeared well controlled and he was continued on risankizumab-rzaa every 12 weeks as well as topical triamcinolone 0.1% ointment and clobetasol topical 0.05% solution as needed. He was also offered treatment for the new-onset vitiligo, which he declined as it was not bothersome to him. Of note, the patient had no personal or family history of vitiligo. Patient also did not have any history of autoimmune thyroid disorders, diabetes, or viral hepatitis B.

[b00008] Case Report
He returned for his next follow-up in May 2025, reporting no active psoriasis and was tolerating risankizumab-rzaa well. On exam, he was noted to have complete resolution of active psoriasis with 0% total body surface area and only had hyperpigmented, post-inflammatory patches (shown in Fig. 1a, b). However, there were scattered areas of depigmented and hypopigmented trichrome patches on the back, left arm, fingers, and face consistent with active, unstable vitiligo (shown in Fig. 2, 3). He was continued on risankizumab-rzaa for plaque psoriasis every 12 weeks and he opted for continued observation and monitoring for the vitiligo.

[b00009] Discussion
Vitiligo is an acquired skin disorder that presents with depigmented macules and patches due to melanocyte destruction. The exact pathogenetic mechanism remains unclear; however, various autoimmune, genetic, chemical, and oxidative stress theories or combination thereof have been postulated as potential etiologies. Our patient developed de novo vitiligo associated with risankizumab-rzaa treatment for plaque psoriasis. He had trichrome lesions characterized by zones of white, light brown, and normal skin color that is pathognomonic for active vitiligo. Upon review of the literature, we found a few reports of de novo vitiligo during treatment with biologics such as TNF-α inhibitors, ustekinumab (IL-17/IL-23 inhibitor), secukinumab (IL-17A inhibitor), and guselkumab (IL-23 inhibitor) [, ]. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports of de novo vitiligo in patients treated with risankizumab-rzaa. Our patient presented very similarly to the patient on guselkumab for severe, refractory psoriatic arthritis, who developed new-onset vitiligo 4.5 months after starting treatment []. This raises the question of whether emergence of de novo vitiligo in patients treated with IL-23 inhibitors represents a rare adverse event related to the medication or a coincidental, sporadic occurrence given that this is only the second case report. It is also possible that the vitiligo may have resulted from koebnerization due to our patient’s plaque psoriasis. In contrast, there is a reported case of a patient with acrofacial vitiligo treated with tildrakizumab, another IL-23 inhibitor, who achieved 90% repigmentation after 1 year of treatment []. This treatment response is paradoxical to the patients who developed vitiligo after initiation of treatment with IL-23 inhibitors. However, the precise role of IL-23 remains unclear, with conflicting reports in the literature.

[b00010] Discussion
The IL-23/TH17 pathway has been implicated in the pathogenesis of vitiligo through autoimmune melanocyte destruction. In previous papers relating biologic use with the development of vitiligo, the mechanism involved immune dysregulation favoring a TH1 response and cytotoxic T-cell-mediated melanocyte destruction. In the case of IL-23 inhibition, this pathway primarily modulates a TH17 pathway and there is no evidence that this leads to a TH1 dominant shift or increased cytotoxic T-cell activity against melanocytes [].

[b00011] Discussion
Interestingly, paradoxical reactions with IL-23 inhibitors are exceedingly rare. IL-23 primarily suppresses TH17-mediated inflammation, which is implicated in both psoriasis and vitiligo, and may even be beneficial in the treatment of vitiligo by reducing pathogenic TH17 activity. Paradoxical skin reactions, including new onset or worsening of vitiligo, are almost exclusively associated with anti-TNFs, and to a lesser extent, IL-17 therapies, with the mechanism of action being overproduction of type 1 interferon; this is a reaction independent of classical T-cell autoimmunity []. While it is possible, that our patient had both psoriasis and vitiligo, since both diseases share overlapping genetic susceptibility, more research is needed to determine if IL-23 inhibition could have played any role in his vitiligo development [, ].
