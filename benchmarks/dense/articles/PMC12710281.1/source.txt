Novel Approach to Managing Inadvertent Arterial Placement of Central Venous Catheters: Report of Two Cases and Review of the Literature

[b00001] Abstract
Recently, there has been an increase in the frequency of central venous catheter insertions in multiple medical specialties. This increase has been accompanied by a higher incidence of complications, such as arterial puncture or subsequent catheter insertion. Arterial injuries resulting from inadvertent central venous catheter insertion have traditionally been managed with manual compression or open surgery. When traditional treatment methods are limited, they are supplemented with endovascular therapies such as vascular closure devices or balloon catheters. However, there are instances in which the application of these endovascular therapies is not feasible. Therefore, we aimed to present two cases in which the combination of temporary balloon tamponade and plug-based tract embolization resulted in effective treatment outcomes when conventional endovascular therapies were limited.

[b00002] INTRODUCTION
As central venous catheter (CVC) insertion for the rapid management of critically ill patients has become more common across various medical specialties, the incidence of complications associated with these procedures has increased (). The most common complications include arterial puncture and subsequent cannulation.

[b00003] INTRODUCTION
Current guidelines recommend open surgery as the initial management option because of the potential inadequacy of hemostasis through manual compression (). However, alternatives must be considered for patients who are unable to undergo general anesthesia. These alternatives include vascular closure devices (VCDs), covered stents, and balloon tamponade, each with limitations. For example, VCDs are less effective in deep structures such as the mediastinum. Covered stents and long-standing balloon tamponade carry the risk of impairing blood flow to the brain, which could potentially lead to neurological injury. Considering these limitations, alternative methods are required. Herein, we present two cases of inadvertent arterial catheterization treated with a combination of temporary balloon tamponade and plug-based tract embolization. We aimed to evaluate the treatment methods in various situations and propose a novel approach using a vascular plug in cases where conventional endovascular therapies were limited.

[b00004] CASE REPORTS
This study obtained Institutional Review Board approval from the institution, and the need for informed consent was waived (IRB No. GNAH 2025-01-001).

[b00005] CASE REPORTS / CASE 1
An 84-year-old male was admitted to our hospital with a compression fracture. Because the patient’s condition deteriorated during admission, a CVC was required for critical care. It was initially planned for insertion into the right subclavian vein, but the clinician realized that it had inadvertently been inserted into an artery, and removal was consulted 21 hours after the insertion. Chest radiography revealed that the CVC, which was intended to follow the path of the subclavian vein, was crossing over the trachea (Fig. 1A).

[b00006] CASE REPORTS / CASE 1
Therefore, it was necessary to identify the specific artery into which the CVC was inserted. First, we replaced the inadvertently inserted 7-Fr CVC with a 0.035-inch guidewire and 5-Fr catheter (Impress, Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT, USA). Angiography and cone-beam CT (Fig. 1B) revealed its placement at the bifurcation of the right subclavian artery (SCA) and right common carotid artery (CCA). After adjusting the angle, the insertion site was found to be closer to the right CCA (Fig. 1C). Given the patient’s previous time of flight (TOF) image, there appeared to be a high risk of brain damage if CCA occlusion was performed. After consultation with the neurosurgeon, balloon tamponade was performed on the right CCA for a brief period.

[b00007] CASE REPORTS / CASE 1
The right common femoral artery was accessed under local anesthesia and ultrasound guidance using a micropuncture kit (Micro-stick, Medcomp, Harleyville, PA, USA). A 0.035-inch guidewire was advanced, and a 5-Fr vascular sheath (Radiofocus, Terumo, Shibuya, Tokyo, Japan) was placed over the guidewire. After selecting the right CCA with a 5-Fr catheter (Headhunter, Terumo), balloon inflation was performed using a 10 mm × 40 mm balloon catheter (Mustang, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). Simultaneously, a 4 mm type IV Amplatzer Vascular Plug was implanted through the pre-inserted Impress catheter to perform tract embolization. After 3 minutes of inflation, the angiogram showed no contrast leakage at the site of CVC removal, and the procedure was completed without additional inflation (Fig. 1D). After 10 days, brain CT and MRI revealed no evidence of a newly developed infarction related to the index interventional treatment.

[b00008] CASE REPORTS / CASE 2
A 60-year-old man diagnosed with hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state was admitted to our hospital through the emergency room. An attempt was made to insert a CVC via the right internal jugular vein at the bedside. A postprocedural chest radiograph revealed that the tip of the catheter crossed the midline and deviated toward the contralateral side (Fig. 2A). Catheter removal was requested 54 hours after the insertion, and the patient was transferred to an interventional radiology suite.

[b00009] CASE REPORTS / CASE 2
After puncturing the right femoral artery and selecting the right brachiocephalic artery using a 5-Fr Headhunter catheter, it was confirmed that the CVC had been inserted into the right SCA, with the vertebral artery and CCA appearing intact (Fig. 2B). Subsequent ultrasonographic imaging revealed vein-artery cannulation, with the CVC penetrating the right internal jugular vein and entering the proximal right SCA (Fig. 2C). A venogram was performed via the right internal jugular vein to estimate the distance between the venous exit and arterial entry points (Fig. 2D). The length was measured to be 45 mm, which is within the suitable range for plug use. Performing balloon tamponade on the proximal right SCA can temporarily occludes the right vertebral artery; therefore, the Headhunter catheter was repositioned for a left vertebral angiogram to evaluate the cerebral posterior circulation. Both vertebral arteries were normal. Thus, it was determined that the temporary occlusion of the right vertebral artery would not pose a risk of brain ischemia. After changing the Headhunter catheter to a 7-Fr sheath, a 12 mm × 40 mm balloon catheter was placed and inflated in the proximal right SCA. Simultaneously, the CVC was replaced with a 5-Fr Impress catheter. A 6 mm type IV Amplatzer Vascular Plug was deployed for tract embolization (Fig. 2E). After 3 minutes of inflation, angiography of the right brachiocephalic artery revealed no extravasation (Fig. 2F). After the procedure, ultrasonography was performed to assess the relationship between the vascular plug and vein (Fig. 2G). The procedure was completed without complications, and no neurological deficits were observed during the 7-day hospitalization period. A follow-up visit 3 weeks later revealed no abnormalities.

[b00010] DISCUSSION
Generally, CVCs are inserted through the internal jugular or subclavian vein. Arterial injury, including puncture or cannulation, occurs in 6.3%–9.4% of internal jugular vein insertions and 3.1%–4.9% of subclavian vein insertions (). Although less common than arterial puncture, arterial cannulation is associated with a significantly higher risk of morbidity and mortality. Life-threatening complications include pseudoaneurysm, hemothorax, thrombus formation, and subsequent neurological damage such as stroke, highlighting the need for prompt management to prevent these fatal outcomes.

[b00011] DISCUSSION
If accidental arterial catheter placement is confirmed, it is crucial not to remove the catheter. Doing so can lead to thrombus formation and subsequent embolic stroke. Instead, leaving the misplaced catheter in place and promptly consulting a vascular surgeon or interventional radiologist can significantly reduce the risk of severe complications ().

[b00012] DISCUSSION
The primary methods for addressing arterial injuries from CVC insertion include manual compression, endovascular techniques, and open repair. Manual compression may result in incomplete hemostasis, especially in cases caused by large-bore (7-Fr or larger) catheters, which increases the likelihood of complications such as airway compression from hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, and delayed hemorrhage (). As a result, the current American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) practice guidelines recommend open surgery over manual compression as the initial management (). However, alternatives are required for patients who cannot tolerate general anesthesia or have coagulation disorders ().

[b00013] DISCUSSION
Advances in technology and equipment have led to the development of various endovascular therapies, with promising results. These options include VCDs, covered stents, and balloon tamponade. However, each treatment has limitations depending on factors, such as the location of the injury or the patient’s condition, making individualized treatment crucial.

[b00014] DISCUSSION
Studies have shown that manual compression after catheter removal can increase the incidence of stroke, whereas VCDs have been found to reduce the risks of stroke and hematoma (). However, VCDs may be difficult to use when the distance from the skin to the arterial puncture site exceeds 8 cm (Perclose) or 10 cm (AngioSeal), and their use is contraindicated in cases with branch vessel involvement, pseudoaneurysm, or underlying arterial dissection (). In cases 1 and 2, in which the arterial puncture site was deep in the mediastinum, VCD insertion was not attempted. Additionally, in case 2, the proximity to the origin of the vertebral artery increased the risk, further contraindicating the use of VCDs.

[b00015] DISCUSSION
The use of covered stents is effective in treating injuries in deep structures where VCDs are not applicable (). However, in case 1, the injury was at the bifurcation of the CCA and SCA, and the placement of a covered stent could obstruct the CCA, making its use impractical. Similarly, in case 2, the catheter was positioned in the proximal right SCA, where the insertion of a covered stent could block the entrance to the vertebral artery, potentially causing neurological damage.

[b00016] DISCUSSION
Unlike covered stents, balloon tamponade does not permanently occlude the blood vessels. However, when applied for extended periods, such as in the CCA, it can impede blood flow to the brain, potentially leading to ischemic damage. Studies have shown that occlusion of the CCA for >5 minutes can cause cerebral ischemia, with the risk being significantly higher in cases of compromised cerebral circulation (). In case 1, because of CCA injury, a previous brain MRI and TOF image was reviewed to assess the Circle of Willis. The results revealed that the anterior communicating artery was in poor condition, and one of the posterior communicating arteries was absent. Considering the compromised cerebral circulation, balloon tamponade was performed for 3 minutes, the minimum time necessary for hemostasis, without exceeding 5 minutes (). In case 2, the injury involved the proximal SCA, which resulted in occlusion of the right vertebral artery. While the left vertebral angiogram indicated adequate posterior circulation, the length of the balloon made partial occlusion of the right CCA unavoidable. To avoid complications resulting from CCA occlusion, as in case 1, the duration of balloon tamponade was limited to 3 minutes. The endpoint of balloon tamponade was defined as the point at which no extravasation was observed. In both cases, the postprocedural angiogram showed no signs of extravasation, allowing the procedure to be completed without the need for further inflation.

[b00017] DISCUSSION
As balloon tamponade had to be performed for a short duration at the site where a relatively large-diameter CVC was removed, it was considered appropriate to perform balloon tamponade from the inside while simultaneously performing tract embolization from the outside to minimize the risk of inadequate hemostasis. This new approach was inspired by the Mynx device, a type of VCD that seals the arteriotomy site using an internal semicompliant balloon that prevents the polyethylene glycol (PEG) sealant from entering the vessel and provides temporary hemostasis. The deployed self-expanding PEG sealant from the outside creates an immediate seal. Considering that the use of glue or coils during tract embolization cannot exclude the potential risk of embolic stroke resulting from glue migration, vascular plug-assisted tract embolization is deemed the most appropriate method. The vascular plug does not provide an immediate seal like the PEG sealant; however, by inserting the plug, we aimed to increase the resistance at tract entry, thereby redirecting the blood flow toward the opposite side. As the blood flow decreases in the area of higher resistance, the tract is expected to undergo obliteration.

[b00018] DISCUSSION
In both cases, the procedure was performed within a confined space in the mediastinum, which likely contributed to the prevention of bleeding. This was achieved without the need for external compression, because balloon tamponade and vascular plugs alone were sufficient. However, if the procedure fails and bleeding continues, the intermittent balloon procedure should be repeated. If this approach is unsuccessful, stent insertion may be considered even at the risk of cerebral ischemia. If this also fails, the final course of action would involve performing bypass surgery through sternotomy to bypass the bleeding site where bleeding cannot be controlled.

[b00019] DISCUSSION
Before using a vascular plug, its suitability must be assessed. In cases of vein-artery cannulation, such as in case 2, if the distance between the venous exit point and arterial entry point is short, the risk of migration increases, which makes the use of a vascular plug challenging. Given the length of the vascular plug, it is necessary to confirm, using a venogram and angiogram as in case 2, whether the tract length provides sufficient space for implantation.

[b00020] DISCUSSION
However, because the diameter of the inadvertently inserted CVC in all the cases was 7-Fr, we cannot guarantee that similar outcomes would be achieved using a larger catheter. In cases involving a larger catheter, a vascular plug alone may not provide sufficient resistance and repeated inflation may be required. Additionally, the limited number of patients and absence of a control group (untreated individuals) present limitations in conducting a comparative analysis.

[b00021] DISCUSSION
In conclusion, endovascular treatment using vascular plugs can offer optimal therapy for individuals with the limitations of open surgery while reducing the risk of complications, such as neurological complications or delayed hemorrhage. Moreover, upon realizing a misplaced catheter, it is crucial not to remove it immediately but rather to contact the vascular surgeon or interventional radiologist promptly.
