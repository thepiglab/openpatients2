Unraveling complexity: A case report on ABO hemolytic disease of the fetus newborn in a preterm neonate and delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction in the beta thalassemic mother due to alloanti kidd a antibody

[b00001] Abstract
ABO hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) generally occurs due to transplacental passage of maternal IgG ABO antibodies causing fetal and newborn red cell hemolysis. Preterm, premature neonates born to O blood group mothers with a high titer of anti-A and/or anti-B antibodies are more prone to developing ABO HDFN. This is a case of ABO HDFN in a preterm, premature, and low birth weight B-positive baby of an O-positive, HbE-beta thalassemic mother. The mother had a very high titer of anti-B antibodies, leading to ABO HDFN. The mother was also allo-immunized with anti-Jk (a) antibodies from past packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusions she received for thalassemia. This anti-Jk (a) antibody was not detected in the antenatal period, probably due to the low titer of the antibody. The mother had an anamnestic response to the PRBCs provided in the antenatal period, and thus, the anti-Jk (a) became identifiable during the pretransfusion workup for the baby. Even if the baby was Jk (a) antigen positive, the baby developed HDFN mostly due to ABO incompatibility. The baby was managed with a double-volume exchange transfusion with reconstituted whole blood and phototherapy. During the postnatal period, the mother developed a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction (DHTR) due to the anti-Jk (a) antibodies, as she had received Jk (a) antigen-positive PRBC transfusions in the antenatal period. Later, the mother responded well to the Jk (a) antigen-negative PRBC transfusions and conservative management for the DHTR.

[b00002] Introduction
Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) occurs when maternal IgG antibodies destroy the fetus or neonate’s red blood cells (RBCs).[] HDFN primarily results from ABO and Rh antibodies. However, it is crucial to recognize minor blood group incompatibilities such as Kell, Kidd (Jk), MNS, and Duffy (Fy) which may also cause significant hemolysis in the baby’s red cells.[]

[b00003] Introduction
ABO incompatibility commonly arises when the mother has blood group O and the baby has blood group A/B/AB. This condition usually manifests as less severe HDFN cases.[] However, premature babies with impaired bilirubin conjugation capacity may experience varying degrees of hemolysis, potentially leading to anemia, jaundice, and in severe instances, kernicterus.[]

[b00004] Introduction
HbE-beta thalassemia ranges from a transfusion-dependent, severe beta-thalassemia phenotype to a beta-thalassemia intermedia phenotype producing a moderate-to-severe disorder.[] Multiple transfusion requirements in thalassemic females during pregnancy can lead to alloimmunization, resulting in the passive transfer of maternal antibodies to fetal circulation.[] If undetected during prenatal and antenatal antibody screening, these alloantibodies can trigger an anamnestic response upon exposure to corresponding antigens from the fetus or transfusions received during pregnancy.[]

[b00005] Introduction
Delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions (DHTRs) typically occur 5–7 days after the transfusion, specifically associated with antibodies of the Kell and Rh systems. Jk antibodies are also notorious for causing DHTR, as their concentration becomes low in the serum very soon and undetectable in routine antibody screens.[]

[b00006] Introduction
Here, we report a case of a preterm baby with ABO HDFN and the case of an HbE beta-thalassemic mother who developed DHTR in the postnatal period due to anti-Jk (a) alloantibody in her serum. The baby, being premature, was suspected to have significant hemolytic disease due to maternally derived anti-B and/or anti-Jk (a) antibodies. But further, HDFN was concluded only due to ABO incompatibility and not due to anti-Jk (a) antibody.

[b00007] Case Report
A 31-year-old O Rh (D)-positive HbE beta-thalassemia primigravida, experiencing preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), was admitted due to anemia with a plan for a cesarean section. The mother presented with multiple comorbidities, including hypothyroidism, splenomegaly, and portal venous hypertension. Following the correction of anemia through PRBC transfusions, she underwent a cesarean section at 24 weeks gestational age.

[b00008] Case Report
A premature female baby with a blood group of B-positive and a birth weight of 600 g was born. The baby developed hemolytic anemia with severe jaundice on the 1^(st) day, necessitating a double-volume exchange transfusion (DVET). All routine immunohematology (IH) workups for the mother and baby were conducted during antenatal and as part of the pre-DVET evaluation, following departmental standard operative procedures.

[b00009] Case Report
The comprehensive IH workup details for the mother and baby are elaborated on separately below. All tests were carried out using column agglutination technology (Tulip Diagnostics, Goa, India), and antibody screening and identification were performed using Panoscreen I, II, III, and Panocell®-16 from Immunocore.

[b00010] Case Report
Immunohematological workup of the mother before delivery as a part of routine pretransfusion testing in an HbE beta-thalassemic female.

[b00011] Case Report
The blood center received a PRBC transfusion request for the mother from the labor room, with a hemoglobin level of 4.7 g/dL. She was admitted with PPROM and needed an emergency cesarean section. As part of routine pretransfusion testing for a thalassemia patient, blood grouping, direct antiglobulin test (DAT), antibody screen, Rh (C, c, E, e), and Kell phenotyping were performed. The blood group was determined as O Rh (D) positive, and the direct Coombs test and antibody screen using three cell panels were negative. As the patient is a thalassemic female, the PRBCs provided by the blood center were phenotypically matched for C, c, E, e, and K antigens and AHG cross-match compatible. As the patient’s Rh and K phenotypes were not giving mixed field reactions in CAT (C4+, c negative, E negative, e 4+, and K negative), it was assumed to be her original phenotype in spite of being transfused within 3 months.[] She was transfused with two units of PRBC on the day of admission and two units on the next day. She was taken for a cesarean section on day 2, and one unit of PRBC was transfused intraoperatively. Her hemoglobin levels increased from a preoperative value of 4.7 g/dL to 10.3 g/dL postoperatively. All the transfusions were uneventful.

[b00012] Case Report
She delivered a preterm premature, low birth-weight female baby with blood group B Rh (D) positive, experiencing severe hemolytic jaundice with serum bilirubin of 18 mg/dl and hemoglobin of 13 g/dl that did not respond to phototherapy. The blood center received a request to provide reconstituted whole blood for a DVET in the baby on postpartum day 2.

[b00013] Case Report / Pre-double-volume exchange transfusion evaluation
Blood grouping, direct antiglobulin test (DAT), and antibody screen were performed on both the mother and baby. The mother’s antibody screen, negative during the antenatal period and before the cesarean section, was found to be positive. Antibody identification confirmed the presence of the anti-Jk (a) antibody in the mother’s serum [Figure 1]. The mother’s DAT was 3+ positive with IgG specificity. Jk (a) phenotyping of the mother gave a mixed field reaction in CAT. As she was recently transfused, it could not be commented upon. Acid elution of mothers’ DAT-positive red cells revealed anti-Jk (a) antibody coating the mother’s red cells.

[b00014] Case Report / Pre-double-volume exchange transfusion evaluation
The baby’s blood group was identified as B Rh (D) positive, Jk (a) positive, and the baby’s DAT was 3+ positive with specificity for IgG. Acid elution of the baby’s DAT-positive red cells revealed anti-B in the eluate. The antibody screen of the baby serum and the eluate were negative.

[b00015] Case Report / Pre-double-volume exchange transfusion evaluation
Since the baby was suspected of HDFN, titers of both the suspected antibodies, anti-B, and anti-Jk (a), were determined in maternal serum/plasma.[] The IgG anti-B titer with B-positive Jk (a)-negative red cells were significantly high, reaching up to 4096 in the conventional tube method and 16384 in the CAT. The anti-Jk (a) titer with O-positive Jk (a)-positive red cells showed a titer of 8.

[b00016] Case Report / Follow-up of mother and baby
The baby was treated with DVET using reconstituted whole blood and phototherapy. The whole blood provided for DVET was prepared with O-positive, Jk (a) antigen-negative PRBC reconstituted with AB-positive plasma with a final hematocrit of 54. The baby responded positively to the exchange transfusion, with an increase in hemoglobin levels from 13 to 16 g/dl and a decrease in total serum bilirubin levels from 18 mg/dL to 13 mg/dl with one DVET, and the phototherapy was continued. The clinician was informed and alerted about the mother’s allo anti-Jk (a) antibodies in her serum with a positive DAT and was advised for a vigilant follow-up. She was advised to receive Jk (a)-negative PRBC units whenever necessary. All the PRBC units provided to the mother during the antenatal period from our blood center were traced back to identify the source of Jk (a) antigen-positive red cells in the mother. Of the five units typed, two units issued on the day of the cesarean were positive for the Jk (a) antigen. Two weeks later, the mother developed symptoms suggestive of DHTR, and hemoglobin decreased to 6.6 g/dL. The mother was provided O Rh (D) positive, c, E, Kell, and Jk (a) phenotype negative PRBC units. By transfusing two units of PRBCs and other conservative management, the hemoglobin level increased from 6.6 to 9.8 g/dL without any transfusion-related adverse events. The baby was stabilized in the neonatal intensive care unit for 1 week and was referred to a higher center for the management of complications related to prematurity and low birth weight. [b00028] Discussion
HDN due to ABO incompatibility is usually less severe than Rh incompatibility. One reason is that fetal RBCs express less of the ABO blood group antigens compared with adult levels. In addition, in contrast to the Rh antigens, the ABO blood group antigens are expressed by a variety of fetal (and adult) tissues, reducing the chances of anti-A and anti-B binding their target antigens on the fetal RBCs.[] In this case, the severity of HDFN and jaundice is mainly due to two reasons – the very high titer of IgG anti-B in the mother and the prematurity of the baby.[] The anti-B being in IgG form can transplacentally reach the fetal circulation and cause hemolysis. The uridinediphosphoglucuronate glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1) enzyme needed for bilirubin conjugation is not developed adequately in the liver of preterm neonates. Hence, the conjugation and removal of indirect bilirubin formed by hemolysis are comparatively more difficult in a preterm baby than in a term baby. As the baby’s eluate showed only anti-B and not anti-Jk (a), the possibility of HDFN by anti-Jk (a) could be ruled out.[]

[b00029] Discussion
The neonatal jaundice in the baby was effectively managed through DVET using blood units compatible with both the mother and the baby and followed by phototherapy.

[b00030] Discussion
The mother had received multiple transfusions during the antenatal period due to pregnancy-induced anemia in thalassemia.[] She may have been alloimmunized with anti-Jk (a) at some point in her life. Kidd antibodies typically decrease to very low titers and become undetectable in routine screening, making them implicated in DHTR. In this instance, the mother may have experienced an anamnestic response to the Jk (a) antigen-positive PRBC transfusions during the prenatal period, leading to the detection of anti-Jk (a) in the serum during the postnatal antibody screening. After 2 weeks, she experienced symptoms related to hemolytic transfusion reaction against the Jk (a) antigen-positive red cells transfused preoperatively. Positive DAT with IgG specificity, transfusion <2 weeks back, the presence of anti-Jk (a) antibody in the eluate, and decreased hemoglobin to pretransfusion levels favor the possibility of DHTR in the mother. As the clinicians were already alerted about the alloimmunization by the transfusion physicians, the patient could be managed promptly with Jk (a) antigen-negative PRBC transfusion and conservative management for a hemolytic transfusion reaction.

[b00031] Conclusion
Routine antibody screening is recommended for antenatal females, as it can indicate the potential for minor blood group antigen incompatibility between the mother and baby. Considering anti-A and anti-B titers in O-positive mothers is valuable for predicting the likelihood of ABO HDFN. Identifying and distinguishing between ABO HDFN and HDFN due to minor red cell antigens is crucial for facilitating appropriate transfusions in newborns with severe hemolytic jaundice. Regular antibody screening, coupled with follow-up titers of ABO and other minor blood group antibodies in subsequent pregnancies, is essential to prevent further occurrences of HDFN.
