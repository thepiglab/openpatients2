Atypical presentation of immunoglobulin A vasculitis mimicking acute appendicitis in a pediatric patient: a case report

[b00001] Abstract
Acute appendicitis is a common pediatric surgical emergency, but its clinical presentation can overlap with other conditions, complicating diagnosis. We present a case of a 4-year-old female who initially presented with symptoms suggestive of appendicitis, including right iliac fossa pain, vomiting, and fever. Imaging studies were inconclusive, and a laparoscopic appendectomy was performed. No signs of appendicitis were found, but vasculitic changes were noted in a segment of the ileum. Postoperatively, the patient developed a mild purpuric rash, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels prompted further investigation. Subsequent laboratory tests and clinical findings led to a diagnosis of Immunoglobulin A (IgA) vasculitis, characterized by a small-vessel vasculitis affecting the gastrointestinal tract. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges when presenting symptoms mimic appendicitis but are ultimately attributed to an atypical presentation of IgA vasculitis. The importance of considering systemic inflammatory conditions like IgA vasculitis in pediatric abdominal pain is emphasized, especially when initial diagnoses do not align with imaging or surgical findings. A multidisciplinary approach, including rheumatological evaluation, was crucial in securing the correct diagnosis and managing the patient’s care.

[b00002] PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY
IgA vasculitis (IgAV) is a condition that causes inflammation of small blood vessels. It can often affect the skin, joints, kidneys, and digestive system. This condition usually involves the upper part of the small intestine, but in rare cases, it can affect the very end of the small intestine (terminal ileum). In our case, a patient came to the hospital with sudden, severe abdominal pain and imaging that suggested a localized bowel problem. Surgeons operated urgently, and only later (when a skin rash appeared) IgAV was diagnosed. The patient recovered quickly after starting steroid treatment, without needing further bowel procedures. This case shows that IgAV can sometimes look like a surgical emergency. By being aware of this possibility, we can prevent unnecessary surgery and guide treatment decisions.

[b00003] ARTICLE HIGHLIGHTS
IgA vasculitis (IgAV) can present with focal terminal ileal involvement which can closely mimic acute appendicitis. Imaging and intraoperative findings in IgAV may be indistinguishable from appendicitis or localized ileitis. Endoscopic findings in IgAV range from subtle mucosal erythema to ulceration and can involve both proximal and distal small bowel. In this case, acute presentation necessitated urgent surgery before IgAV was suspected. Postoperative steroid therapy led to rapid resolution of symptoms and inflammatory markers. Awareness of atypical IgAV presentations may help avoid unnecessary extensive resections.

[b00004] Introduction
Acute appendicitis is one of the most common surgical emergencies in children, typically presenting with right iliac fossa (RIF) pain, fever, and leukocytosis []. However, a variety of conditions, including gastrointestinal, infectious, and rheumatological disorders, can mimic appendicitis, leading to diagnostic and management challenges [].

[b00005] Introduction
Immunoglobulin A (IgA) vasculitis, formerly Henoch-Schönlein Purpura, is a small-vessel vasculitis with IgA1-dominant immune complex deposits, affecting small vessels. IgA vasculitis most commonly affects children and adolescents, with a reported annual incidence rate of 3–26.7/100,000, though this number is likely underestimated [,]. Adults are less commonly affected, with an annual incidence rate of 0.8-1.8/100,000 []. Both adolescent and adult males have a reported higher incidence of IgA vasculitis when compared to females [,]. IgA vasculitis is most prevalent in Southeast Asia and to a lesser extent in Europe and North America, but it is rare in Africa [,].

[b00006] Introduction
Multiple sets of criteria have been used for the diagnosis of IgA vasculitis. The European League Against Rheumatism, the Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organization, and the Pediatric Rheumatology European Society have proposed a validated classification criterion with high sensitivity and specificity that includes the presence of purpura or petechiae (mandatory) with lower limb predominance plus one of the following criteria: abdominal pain, histopathology positive for IgA, arthritis or arthralgia, or renal involvement [] (see Table 1).

[b00012] Introduction
Vasculitides, such as IgA vasculitis (formerly Henoch-Schönlein purpura), can present with abdominal pain due to gastrointestinal involvement, sometimes preceding the characteristic purpuric rash []. Misidentification of such cases as appendicitis may result in unnecessary surgical intervention [].

[b00013] Case presentation
A 4-year-1-month-old female with no remarkable medical or family history presented to the emergency department with a 24-hour history of abdominal pain, vomiting, and new-onset fever. On physical examination, tenderness was noted in the right iliac fossa. The patient was vitally stable.

[b00014] Case presentation
Laboratory tests (see Table 2) revealed an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 23.1 mg/L and mild leukocytosis (16.5 × 10^(3) cells/μL; reference range: 5–15 × 10^(3) cells/μL). Initial ultrasound findings were inconclusive showing no frank signs of appendiceal inflammation. A subsequent CT scan showed dilated small bowel loops in the right iliac fossa, with no definitive confirmation or exclusion of appendicitis. Additionally, the radiological report described numerous large mesenteric lymph nodes, and a mild amount of free fluid was noted within the right iliac fossa (see Figure 1).

[b00018] Case presentation
Given the patient’s high clinical suspicion for acute appendicitis, inconclusive but concerning CT findings, and the known limitations of imaging accuracy in young children, immediate diagnostic laparoscopy was indicated to avoid progression to perforation. During the procedure, no signs of appendicitis were noted macroscopically. However, erythema and seemingly vasculitic changes were observed in a segment of the ileum (see Figures 2 and 3). The appendix appeared normal, and an appendectomy was performed. Histopathology revealed no signs of appendicitis, with findings consistent with benign reactive follicular hyperplasia.

[b00019] Case presentation
The patient was discharged the next day following resolution of initial symptoms and a negative fecal calprotectin test. However, 4 days after discharge, she was readmitted due to persistent abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, and inability to tolerate food. Notably, she developed a new mild purpuric rash and petechiae on her legs (see Figures 4 and 5). Blood tests showed a significant increase in CRP to 48.1 mg/L. The emergence of a rash and the elevated CRP prompted a shift in our clinical approach toward a more rheumatological perspective. Multiple additional tests were performed to investigate this possibility (Table 3).

[b00029] Case presentation
Given the clinical picture and the laboratory findings, the patient was started on corticosteroids. Two days later, she was discharged with instructions for follow-up appointments in the coming weeks. Despite the inconclusive nature of the diagnosis, the patient was diagnosed with IgA vasculitis based on the clinical findings and laboratory results.

[b00030] Case presentation
Renal involvement in IgA vasculitis (IgAV), also known as Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis, occurs in approximately 20–50% of cases, typically presenting as IgA nephropathy-like glomerulonephritis. It manifests as hematuria, proteinuria, and, in severe cases, nephrotic or nephritic syndrome, potentially leading to chronic kidney disease [,].

[b00036] Discussion
This case highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in pediatric patients presenting with abdominal pain, where clinical presentations may overlap between common conditions such as appendicitis and rarer diseases like IgA vasculitis. Although appendicitis is a common diagnosis in pediatric abdominal pain, a variety of conditions, including gastrointestinal and rheumatological disorders, can present similarly, complicating the diagnostic process []. Ultimately, the presentation shifted toward a rheumatological condition after surgery, when vasculitic changes were observed in a segment of the ileum and histopathology excluded appendicitis.

[b00037] Discussion
A particularly notable feature of this case is the ambiguity of the laparoscopic findings, which revealed only one segment of the ileum showing signs of erythema and vasculitic changes, while the rest of the bowel appeared normal. This finding raises several important questions regarding the nature of the disease and why the inflammation was localized to just one segment of the ileum, rather than affecting multiple or more extensive areas, as might be expected in cases of IgA vasculitis. IgA vasculitis is a small-vessel vasculitis that can cause widespread gastrointestinal involvement, typically affecting multiple areas of the gastrointestinal tract, particularly the small bowel [,]. However, the presence of vasculitic changes confined to just one segment of the ileum in this case suggests a more focal inflammatory process, which is unusual in the context of IgA vasculitis.

[b00038] Discussion
One possible explanation for the localized involvement could be that IgA vasculitis may not always lead to uniform gastrointestinal involvement. The immune response in IgA vasculitis can vary in intensity, and immune complex deposition may result in patchy inflammation rather than affecting the entire gastrointestinal tract []. Furthermore, vascular compromise in specific areas may lead to more concentrated inflammation, possibly explaining why only a single segment of the ileum was affected. This localized presentation raises important questions about the pathophysiology of IgA vasculitis, and the findings suggest that the disease may manifest differently in individual patients. More research is needed to understand the heterogeneous nature of IgA vasculitis, particularly in terms of its gastrointestinal involvement [].

[b00039] Discussion
Another factor contributing to the clinical course is the perioperative corticosteroid administration. Corticosteroids are known for their potent anti-inflammatory properties, and their use during surgery, particularly in the perioperative period, may have contributed to the initial clinical improvement observed in this patient. Corticosteroids can effectively reduce the systemic inflammatory response, leading to short-term symptom relief, even in the absence of full disease resolution []. In particular, the administration of early perioperative corticosteroid, likely attenuated the severity of cutaneous manifestations. The mild rash does not exclude the diagnosis when other EULAR/PRINTO/PRES criteria are met. This is consistent with the idea that corticosteroids administered during anesthesia may have masked the symptoms, allowing for temporary improvement and the subsequent discharge of the patient. The potential for symptom masking by corticosteroids is well documented in the literature and should be considered in cases with ambiguous or evolving diagnoses [].

[b00040] Discussion
The delayed onset of the rash—purpura and petechiae—several days after surgery could also be attributed to the anti-inflammatory effects of corticosteroids. While corticosteroids may reduce acute inflammatory symptoms initially, their withdrawal or the gradual cessation of their effects may allow for the emergence of characteristic signs of IgA vasculitis, including purpura []. In fact, the appearance of the rash is often seen in the later stages of the disease, and its delayed onset in this patient may reflect the course of the underlying condition rather than a new, unrelated pathology. The phenomenon of delayed symptom manifestation following corticosteroid therapy is not uncommon in patients with inflammatory diseases and underscores the importance of long-term monitoring for such patients [].

[b00041] Discussion
Although IgA vasculitis is traditionally described as preferentially involving the proximal small bowel, recent multicenter and single-center series show that the duodenum and the ileum can be frequently involved. Indeed, a large multicenter retrospective study of adult abdominal IgAV found that, among patients who underwent both imaging and endoscopy, the duodenum and ileum were the most commonly affected sites. Additionally, endoscopic findings ranged from congestion and erythema to erosions and ulcers. In addition, it is also important to note that bowel-wall thickening on CT/MRI and mesenteric lymphadenopathy were common imaging correlates [].

[b00042] Discussion
In addition, other single-center and multicenter analyses of adult patients with abdominal IgAV corroborate these findings. Indeed, endoscopic abnormalities in the descending duodenum and terminal ileum (congestion, petechiae, erosion, ulceration) and imaging evidence of segmental wall thickening of the ileum/jejunum are repeatedly reported. These studies emphasize that terminal ileum involvement is not exceptional and that endoscopic appearances are heterogeneous, from subtle erythema to frank ulceration [,]. From this perspective, the absence of endoscopy does not exclude clinically meaningful ileal disease, especially when operative inspection demonstrates vasculitic changes.

[b00043] Discussion
In the context of our case, the decision not to pursue postoperative endoscopic evaluation was made for two main reasons. First, the patient’s acute presentation required urgent surgical exploration before a diagnosis of IgAV was suspected. Second, after the postoperative emergence of purpura and confirmation of a systemic inflammatory process, symptoms improved rapidly with corticosteroid therapy.

[b00044] Differential diagnosis
The clinical presentation of this patient required consideration of several important differential diagnoses. Given the initial abdominal pain, vomiting, and fever, acute appendicitis was the first and most immediate consideration. However, due to the nonspecific imaging findings and the absence of clear signs of appendiceal inflammation, further diagnostic exploration was necessary. This led to the eventual discovery of IgA vasculitis, which is characterized by small-vessel vasculitis and the deposition of IgA immune complexes in affected tissues, notably the kidneys, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. Gastrointestinal (GI) involvement occurs in 50-75% of pediatric IgA vasculitis cases, presenting as abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding, and diarrhea [,]. Severe complications like intussusception, appendicitis, and ileal perforation can occur in up to 18% of cases []. Indeed, it is worth noting that GI symptoms may precede the characteristic skin rash, complicating diagnosis []. While usually self-limiting, severe cases may require steroid treatment [,]. GI involvement, particularly the presence of occult blood in stool, is associated with a higher risk of renal complications []. However, the severity of GI symptoms does not necessarily correlate with renal involvement,. Older age (>5 years) is also identified as a risk factor for renal involvement in pediatric IgA vasculitis [].

[b00045] Differential diagnosis
Nonetheless, other conditions must be considered when faced with a similar clinical presentation to our patient.

[b00046] Differential diagnosis
One important differential is systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a multisystem autoimmune disorder that can present with a variety of gastrointestinal symptoms, including abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and even peritonitis in some cases. Lupus enteritis, a rare but serious manifestation of lupus, can cause inflammation of the small bowel and mimic other causes of abdominal pain, including appendicitis. The presence of serological markers such as antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-dsDNA antibodies, and complement levels may help differentiate SLE from other conditions []. While this patient’s lab results did not support a diagnosis of lupus, the overlap of abdominal symptoms should always prompt consideration of autoimmune diseases like SLE.

[b00047] Differential diagnosis
Additionally, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any pediatric patient presenting with recurrent abdominal pain and gastrointestinal symptoms. IBD can cause small bowel inflammation and extra-intestinal manifestations, including skin rashes and arthritis, which may overlap with features of IgA vasculitis. Fecal calprotectin and other gastrointestinal markers are helpful in distinguishing IBD from IgA vasculitis, as the latter often does not present with significant gastrointestinal inflammation []. In this patient, the normal fecal calprotectin level was an important negative finding, making IBD less likely.

[b00048] Differential diagnosis
Mesenteric adenitis should also be considered, particularly in younger children, as it can present with symptoms indistinguishable from appendicitis. However, the absence of a palpable mass, along with the patient’s eventual diagnosis of IgA vasculitis, helped rule out this condition.

[b00049] Conclusion
This case emphasizes the importance of considering systemic conditions like IgA vasculitis in pediatric abdominal pain, especially when typical diagnoses like appendicitis do not fully explain the clinical picture. A multidisciplinary approach and timely use of rheumatological investigations are crucial for accurate diagnosis and optimal patient care.
