Premature Ovarian Insufficiency in a Young Woman with Hypothyroidism, Chromosome 9 Inversion, and Cavernous Angioma: A Case Report of an Unusual Presentation and Literature Review

[b00001] Abstract
Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) in a young unmarried woman was detected in association with hypothyroidism, chromosome 9 (Inv 9, p12q13) inversion, and cavernous angioma. A 27-year-old unmarried female presented with complaints of infrequent cycles only after undergoing withdrawal treatment for menstruation for the past 11 years. She was diagnosed with hypothyroidism and was subsequently prescribed thyroid replacement therapy. On examination, bilateral galactorrhoea was identified. Oestradiol and anti-Mullerian hormone values were low, whereas her follicle-stimulating hormone value was elevated. Her brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed a solitary cavernous angioma, and her karyotype indicated 46 XX and Inv(9) p12q13. POI in a young woman is a devastating condition affecting both physical and emotional well-being. Chromosomal aberrations should be ruled out in such cases. Life-long oestrogen replacement therapy and alternate options for infertility issues such as ovum donation or adoption can be considered.

[b00002] Introduction
Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) is the loss of ovarian function before the age of 40 years, which is preceded by menstrual irregularity and diagnosis is based on the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology criteria, which states the presence of amenorrhoea or oligomenorrhea for at least 4 months and an elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) level >25 IU/L on two occasions at least 4 weeks apart.[]

[b00003] Introduction
The prevalence of POI in a cohort study conducted between 1928 and 1932 was 1%.[] The prevalence of POI is 10%–28% in cases of primary amenorrhoea and approximately 4%–8% in secondary amenorrhoea.[]

[b00004] Introduction
The aetiopathogenesis of premature ovarian failure is multifactorial. Most of the cases remain unexplained or idiopathic.[] Genetic causes due to chromosomal and gene abnormality are responsible for 10.8% of POI cases,[] of which Turner syndrome (45, X0), trisomy X (47, XXX), and tetrasomy X (48, XXXX) are well-documented.[] Fragile X mental retardation 1 (FMR1) premutation carries an incidence of 12.9%.[] The incidence of Inv(9) is reported to be 1%–3% in the general population and lowest among Asians around 0.25%.[]

[b00005] Introduction
Autoimmune diseases are estimated to be present in 4%–30% of POI cases.[] Clinical presentation of POI will be symptoms of hypoestrogenemia such as vasomotor (hot flushes and night sweats), urogenital (dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, and urinary symptoms), sleep disturbance, mood changes, and increased risk of fractures due to osteoporosis and cardiovascular diseases.[]

[b00006] Introduction
Intracerebral haemangiomas are found in 0.5%–1% of the population and occur as sporadic or familial.[]

[b00007] Case Report
A 27-year-old unmarried female presented with complaints of infrequent cycles only after taking withdrawal treatment for menstruation for the last 11 years. She was diagnosed with hypothyroidism then with thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) value of 12.81 µIU/mL, T4 value of 1.92 ng/dL, T3 value of 68.69 ng/dL, and started on thyroid replacement therapy. She had attained menarche at the age of 14 years with regular 28–30 days cycles and of average duration and flow for the next 2 years. Her family history was non-significant. On examination, the patient was of average build with a body mass index of 21 kg/m^(2), she was not pale, and breast examination revealed galactorrhoea from both sides, otherwise, secondary sex characteristics were well-developed. Her systemic examination did not reveal any abnormality. The per-abdominal finding was normal. Her external genitalia were normal, and the hymen was intact.

[b00008] Case Report
Ultrasonography of the whole abdomen and pelvis showed a uterus of size 59.3 mm × 15.3 mm × 10.9 mm with 5.0 mm of endometrial thickness. Right and left ovaries measured 25 mm × 11 mm and 26 mm × 12 mm, respectively, other organs were normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study revealed a small size of uterus in the pelvis and brain revealed an 11 mm × 8 mm lesion on the left side of the pons suggestive of cavernous angioma and mild pituitary hyperplasia [Figure 1A and B]. The hormonal profile showed a serum TSH value of 1.57 µIU/mL, serum FSH value of 115.70 mIU/mL, serum luteinizing hormone value of 27.22 mIU/mL, serum oestradiol value of 12.87 pg/mL, prolactin value of 12.01 ng/mL, serum anti-Mullerian hormone value of 0.01 ng/mL. FMR1 premutation study showed no abnormality. Her karyotype report showed 46 XX, Inv(9) p12 q13.

[b00009] Case Report
We have counselled the patient and her parents about the clinical condition, potential complications associated with hypoestrogenemia, and future fertility issues. We explained the treatment options, including hormone therapy until the age of natural menopause, and the options available for her future childbearing. A neurosurgery opinion was sought because of the MRI finding suggestive of cavernous angioma, for which she was advised to avoid any active intervention and follow-up.

[b00010] Discussion
The present case report of POI demonstrates multiple conditions including hypothyroidism, karyotype with 46 XX, Inv(9) p12 q13, and asymptomatic single intracerebral cavernous angioma.

[b00011] Discussion
Thyroid disorders such as hypothyroidism, Hashimoto thyroiditis, and Grave’s disease are firmly associated with this condition.[] Iatrogenic causes include Combination chemotherapy, especially alkylating agents, radiotherapy, and pelvic surgeries involving removal or damage to the blood supply of ovaries. Ovarian endometrioma excision when performed bilaterally leads to a 2.4% chance of developing POI.[,] Infections such as mumps oophoritis, human immunodeficiency virus, tuberculosis, varicella, cytomegalovirus, and malaria can cause ovarian dysfunction.[,]

[b00012] Discussion
Among chromosomal aberrations, various types of pericentric inversions 9 have been reported. The most common breakpoints are located in the 9p12 or 9q13-21.1 regions.[] One of the largest epidemiological studies conducted on Inv(9) showed a 1.6% prevalence and Inv(9) was demonstrated in 37.1% cases of idiopathic reproductive failure.[] One case report with a literature review reported a case with rapidly declining ovarian reserve over 18 months in a reproductive-age woman. Her karyotype showed Inv(9) (p11q13). This literature review found a prevalence of 0.5%–2.5% in women with infertility carrying Inv(9).[]

[b00013] Discussion
Cavernous angiomas occur sporadically as solitary lesions, which remain asymptomatic in the majority while familial inheritance presents as an autosomal dominant pattern, mostly as multiple lesions with symptoms due to recurrent bleeding episodes producing seizures and focal neurological deficits. Therefore, other family members should be screened. MRI is the diagnostic modality. A conservative approach is taken in asymptomatic cases and symptomatic, multiple lesions require surgical resection.[,]

[b00014] Discussion
POI is a pathologic condition requiring a specific approach to management, mainly hormone therapy to reduce the risk of osteoporosis, and cardiovascular disease and alleviate the symptoms of urogenital atrophy. First-line therapy is oestrogen replacement in the absence of contraindications till the age of natural menopause.[]

[b00015] Discussion
A pregnancy rate of 4.8% was found in POI patients who had received oestrogen replacement and 6.3% after ovulation induction. Oocyte donation remains a reasonable option.[]

[b00016] Discussion
A study has found that approximately 75% of POI patients may have a small cohort of dormant primordial follicles in the ovaries.[]

[b00017] Discussion
Some of the newer therapeutic options are in the experimental phase, such as in vitro activation, mitochondrial activation, stem cells and exosome therapy, and intra-ovarian infusion of platelet-rich plasma. The role of microribonucleic acids in POI therapy is also under investigation.[]

[b00018] Conclusion
This is the first case report of POI showing Inv(9) p12q13 in association with hypothyroidism and incidentally diagnosed cerebral cavernous angioma. POI in young females is a life-long morbidity affecting the physical and emotional well-being in terms of health risk and negative impact on future fertility. Chromosomal aberrations should be ruled out in such cases.
