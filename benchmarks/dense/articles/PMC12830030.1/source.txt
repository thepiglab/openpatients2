Postoperative Chylous Leakage in Breast Cancer Patients: A Multicentre Case Series

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Chylous leakage is a rare complication following breast cancer surgery, characterized by milky, lipid-rich fluid collection in surgical drains. While well documented in thoracic and abdominal surgeries, its occurrence after breast procedures remains poorly understood with limited literature. This study evaluates the incidence, characteristics, and management of this complication.

[b00002] Abstract / Methods
A retrospective analysis was conducted, and seven patients with confirmed chyle leakage were identified among 1,441 breast cancer patients who underwent breast cancer surgery at two Hong Kong surgical centres within a 5-year period. Patients with prolonged drainage or milky fluid underwent laboratory analysis to confirm chylous leakage. Demographics, tumour characteristics, surgical details, management strategies, and outcomes were analysed.

[b00003] Abstract / Results
Seven patients (0.49%) developed chylous leakage. The median age was 67 years, and the median BMI was 22.2 kg/m^(2). Four patients had right-sided, and three had left-sided breast cancer. Most tumours (85.7%) were located in the upper outer quadrant. All patients underwent mastectomy with various axillary procedures. Chylous drainage was identified between postoperative days 2 and 15 in 6 patients, with 1 late presentation at 6 months. Six patients (85.7%) were successfully managed with dietary modifications (medium-chain triglyceride or low-fat diet), with resolution within 3–11 days. One patient with late presentation required surgical intervention as definitive treatment. No recurrences or delays in adjuvant therapy occurred.

[b00004] Abstract / Conclusion
Chylous leakage after breast cancer surgery can occur on either side and can occur in patient with sentinel lymph node biopsy. Conservative management with dietary modification is highly effective as first-line treatment. This study highlights the need for increased awareness of this complication and provides foundation for further investigation into its incidence, pathophysiology, and optimal management.

[b00005] Introduction
Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide []. Surgery, often combined with adjuvant treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, remains a cornerstone in the management of breast cancer []. Mastectomy and breast-conserving surgery, coupled with sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) or axillary dissection (AD), are the most common surgical procedures performed for breast cancer [].

[b00006] Introduction
Following breast cancer surgery, the accumulation of fluid in the surgical site is a common occurrence, necessitating the use of drains to prevent seroma formation and wound complications []. In most cases, the drainage volume gradually decreases, and the drains are removed within a few days post-surgery. However, in some instances, patients may experience prolonged drainage or develop milky, lipid-rich fluid in the drains, and subsequently biochemical test will be required to check if chyle leakage occurs [].

[b00007] Introduction
Prolonged drainage can be attributed to various reasons, such as patient factors, i.e., obesity, existing comorbidities, and the extent of surgery []. The content of drain fluid is typically serous or serosanguinous, with a clear to slightly blood-tinged appearance []. However, the presence of milky, lipid-rich fluid, known as chyle, is an unusual finding in the context of breast cancer surgery [].

[b00008] Introduction
Chyle is normally transported by the lymphatic system, with the majority of chyle flowing through the thoracic duct, which drains into the central venous system. The presence of chyle in the peripheral lymphatics following breast cancer surgery is a rare and poorly understood phenomenon, with an incidence of around 0.7% according to the published literature []. However, not all cases of chyle leakage are directly related to the thoracic duct, which is a well-documented complication of thoracic and abdominal surgeries. And reports have demonstrated that chyle leakage can occur in area outside the normal anatomical drainage area of the thoracic duct [].

[b00009] Introduction
In this case series, we retrospectively analysed the records of breast cancer patients who underwent mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery with SLNB or AD over the past 5 years in two affiliated surgical centres in Hong Kong SAR, China. This case series aimed to explore the characteristics of patients presenting with chylous fluid following breast cancer surgery and served as a foundation for future studies to better understand this unusual complication and its implications for postoperative care in breast cancer patients.

[b00010] Methods
This multicentre case series descriptive analysis examined chyle leakage after breast cancer surgery between July 1, 2018, and June 30, 2023. Retrospective data were collected from a prospective database and patients’ records using Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System. A total of 1,441 patients with breast cancer underwent various breast and axillary surgical procedures in the two affiliated surgical centres in Hong Kong SAR, China. For breast surgery, mastectomy, breast-conserving surgery, and oncoplastic surgery were offered to suitable patients with breast cancer. For axillary surgery, SLNB was performed on patients with lymph node negative clinically, while AD was performed to patients with confirmed axillary lymph node involvement preoperatively or intraoperatively. Regarding SLNB, standard dual tracer technique with radioisotope and blue dye were used to identify the possible sentinel lymph node []. AD of level I and II lymph nodes was performed from lower border of axillary vein with preservation of thoracodorsal bundle and long thoracic nerve []. In both axillary surgical procedures, the dissection was performed following our standard protocol using monopolar diathermy. Haemostasis and control of lymphatics were performed with diathermy, surgical clips, and ligature until dry. A single drain was usually placed for all mastectomy and AD cases, as well as selectively in breast conservative surgery or SLNB.

[b00011] Methods
The patients were given a normal diet once they became alert and conscious. The drain was kept in place until the discharge decreased to <30 mL/24 h for two consecutive days. Patients with prolonged drainage or with milky drain fluid were identified post-operatively or during follow-up in our clinic. With clinical presentation being compatible with chyle leakage, centrifugation of the drain fluid was performed and the diagnosis of chyle was confirmed with positive Sudan fat stain [].

[b00012] Methods
Patients with proven chyle were identified, and their records in terms of clinical profile and management were analysed. A review of the literature was carried out, and a hypothesis about the possible cause of chylous leak was suggested.

[b00013] Results / Incidence
During a study period of 5 years, a total of 1,441 breast cancer surgeries were performed in the two centres. Seven patients experienced chyle leak. The incidence of chyle leak was 0.49%.

[b00014] Results / Demographics and Comorbidities
Seven patients who developed postoperative chyle leak were identified. The median age was 67 years (range 41–82). The median body weight was 22.2 kg/m^(2) (range 21.4–28.5) (Table 1). Two patients (28.6%) had hypertension, and one had coronary artery disease. One patient had lupus-like disease, while another one patient had pre-existing thoracic pathology (eventration of diaphragm with phrenic nerve palsy). Around half of them (42.9%) were classified into American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade 3. None of them had any previous operation related to the breast, axilla, or thorax before the index operation.

[b00056] Results / Management and Outcomes
All patients received dietary consultation and modification as the primary management strategy. The dietary interventions included medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diet (42.9%) and low-fat diet (57.1%). The duration of resolution of chyle leak ranged from 3 to 11 days in 85.7% (6/7) patients. One patient with delayed presentation experienced persistent leak despite dietary modification and required surgical intervention.

[b00057] Results / Treatment Course
Three patients (42.9%) required additional drainage for seroma. All patients proceeded with their planned adjuvant therapy. No recurrence of chyle leakage was identified throughout the study period.

[b00058] Results / Treatment Course
In the patient with delayed presentation (patient 4), leakage was persistent. Hence, lymphoscintigraphy was also done and lymphatic leakage was found to be located over the right axilla. Surgical exploration was performed with plication under the guidance of ICG. Patient remained well with no evidence of seroma recurrence after the surgery.

[b00059] Discussion / Summary of Findings and Incidence of Chyle Leak in Breast Cancer Surgery
In this case series, we presented seven patients who developed chyle leak following breast cancer surgery. Notably, four of these cases occurred after surgery for right-sided breast cancer, and five involved axillary lymph node dissection. A majority of patients were managed successfully with conservative measures, including dietary modifications such as low-fat or MCT diets. One patient, however, required surgical exploration and plication with ICG guidance. There is no reported recurrence, highlighting the efficacy of non-invasive management strategies for chyle leak in the context of breast surgery and the importance of surgical intervention in selective cases.

[b00060] Discussion / Summary of Findings and Incidence of Chyle Leak in Breast Cancer Surgery
The reported incidence of chyle leak is approximately 0.7% in previous literature [], and our local incidence is compatible at around 0.49% based on our existing data. It is believed that the actual incidence could be even higher, as many patients may be missed due to less noticeable fluid colour changes or smaller amounts of output recorded in the drain chart, making the diagnosis of chyle leak occurrence underrated. Moreover, compared to the recent systematic review, which reported a 7.8% incidence for right-sided chyle leak and only 9.8% of cases involving SLNB, our case series exemplified positive findings over right-sided procedures predominantly (4/7, 57.1%) and a more significant portion of patients undergoing SLNB only (2/7, 28.6%).

[b00061] Discussion / Definition of Chyle Leak and Common Biochemical Tests
Chyle leak is defined as the extravasation of chyle, a milky fluid that is rich in triglycerides, lymphocytes, and proteins, from the lymphatic system into the surgical field or surrounding tissues []. The diagnosis is typically suspected when there is the presence of milky fluid from surgical drains or wounds, particularly after the initiation of oral intake. However, a chyle leak cannot be excluded solely based on the clear fluid appearance, as the colour can be affected by diet and fasting status []. The diagnosis of a chyle leakage is confirmed if the effusion exhibits a positive Sudan fat stain, a triglyceride level of 110 mg/dL (1.24 mmol/L), a cholesterol-to-triglyceride ratio of less than 1, or the presence of chylomicrons on lipoprotein electrophoresis [, ]. These biochemical markers help differentiate chyle from other fluids, ensuring accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

[b00062] Discussion / Chyle and Lymphatic Anatomy
Chyle leak is a rare but well-documented complication of breast cancer surgery, with an estimated incidence of around 0.7% []. While traditionally associated with left-sided procedures due to the anatomical dominance of the thoracic duct, significant anatomical variations do exist. Studies have shown that only 50% of individuals possess typical thoracic duct anatomy, with 2–3% having right-sided emptying and 1.5% showing bilateral drainage patterns [, ].

[b00063] Discussion / Pathophysiology and Mechanism
The pathophysiology primarily involves direct injury to the lymphatic system during surgical dissection. While the thoracic duct is commonly implicated in left-sided cases, the right lymphatic duct and its tributaries, which drain the right upper quadrant, can be injured during right-sided procedures. Systematic reviews indicate that lymphatic dissection, particularly axillary clearance, is strongly associated with chyle leak [].

[b00064] Discussion / Pathophysiology and Mechanism
However, clinical evidence points to additional mechanisms. In our case series, all patients with chyle leakage have undergone mastectomy. This indicates that chyle leakage can originate from the transection of smaller, peripheral lymphatic channels within the breast parenchyma and the overlying cutaneous system, which could be more extensively disrupted during mastectomy than in BCS []. The predominant findings in our series are consistent with the existing literature, in which only a single case of chyle leakage post-BCS relating to an injured ductule in the surgical bed has been reported []. Furthermore, chyle leak has been documented at distant sites, such as the donor site during autologous abdominal flap reconstruction, implying that lymphatic networks could be far removed from the central thoracic system [].

[b00065] Discussion / Pathophysiology and Mechanism
Notably, our series also demonstrated a significant proportion of right-sided chyle leak (57.1%, 4/7). Collectively, these cases imply that the mechanism is not always as simple as direct duct transection. Alternative explanations may include injury to variant or accessory lymphatic pathways, lymphatic backflow due to altered pressures following dissection, or drainage through aberrant peripheral connections to the central thoracic system [].

[b00066] Discussion / Evidence about Dietary Intervention for Chyle Leak
Conservative management with dietary modification, particularly through a low-fat or MCT diet, is the primary treatment for chyle leak and can be successful in most patients []. Dietary lipid intake leads to the absorption and packaging of triglycerides and cholesterol into chylomicrons, which enter the intestinal lymphatic vessels (lacteals) and are transported via the thoracic duct to the bloodstream []. This will increase the production of chyle and hence the leakage amount. The rationale behind dietary intervention is to reduce the production of chyle, thereby decreasing the flow through the injured lymphatic vessels and promoting spontaneous closure. Two main dietary strategies, namely, low-fat diet and MCT diet, are commonly employed to reduce chyle production. In particular, MCTs are absorbed directly into the portal venous system, thereby bypassing the lymphatic system and reducing chyle production [].

[b00067] Discussion / Evidence about Dietary Intervention for Chyle Leak
In some cases, parenteral nutrition (PN) may be necessary when a patient is unable to tolerate such a diet or if chyle output persists despite diet modifications []. PN involves an intravenous administration of nutrients, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and significantly reducing chyle production by eliminating the need for fat absorption through the lymphatic system. This approach is particularly beneficial in cases of high-output chyle leak or when oral intake exacerbates the leakage. Studies have demonstrated that PN can reduce chyle flow by up to 90%, providing a therapeutic window for the injured lymphatic vessels to heal []. However, PN is not without risks, including infectious complications, metabolic disturbances, and the need for hospitalization. Therefore, its use is generally limited to short-term management, with a transition back to enteral nutrition as soon as the leakage is controlled []. Hence, the decision to initiate PN should be individualized, weighing the potential benefits against the risks, and should involve a multidisciplinary team including surgeons, dietitians, and nutrition specialists.

[b00068] Discussion / Other Interventions during Chyle Leak
Somatostatin, a peptide hormone that inhibits the secretion of several other hormones, has been explored as a therapeutic agent in the management of chyle leak []. Its mechanism of action involves reducing lymphatic flow and promoting lymphatic vessel constriction, which can help mitigate the volume of chyle leak. Clinical studies have demonstrated that somatostatin analogues, such as octreotide, can effectively reduce the chyle output in patients with postoperative chyle leak []. For instance, a review of multiple studies has indicated that subcutaneous administration of octreotide at doses of 100 µg three times daily leads to the cessation of chyle leak in a significant number of cases, typically within 4 to 10 days of treatment.

[b00069] Discussion / Other Interventions during Chyle Leak
Intervention is warranted in refractory cases, which are typically defined by persistent drainage, nutritional compromise, or failure to resolve after 2–4 weeks of non-operative management []. Imaging involving lymphoscintigraphy or lymphangiogram might be required for anatomical delineation before surgery []. These imaging techniques not only aid diagnosis but also guide targeted interventions, such as percutaneous embolization or surgical ligation, by locating the anatomical origin of the leak []. Studies have reported successful outcomes with embolization techniques, achieving cessation of chyle leak within 24 h post-procedure []. Interestingly, a case report also suggested that lymphangiography could be a therapeutic tool due to regional inflammation triggered by lipiodol accumulated in the leakage site [].

[b00070] Discussion / Other Interventions during Chyle Leak
In axillary chyle leak, re-exploration often involves direct ligation of the injured lymphatic vessel (used in one of our patients) [], or applying fibrin glue []. Intraoperative techniques used to visualize the leak, such as administering a nasogastric bolus of heavy cream or methylene blue, may enhance surgical precision in less obvious cases []. However, as mentioned, surgery is always the last resort and most patients can actually be managed with a conservative method.

[b00071] Conclusion
Although the majority of cases occur on the left side due to the anatomical dominance of the thoracic duct, chyle leak can also occur on the right side, as demonstrated in this case series, possibly due to injury to the right lymphatic duct or its tributaries. It can also occur after SLNB. Conservative management with dietary modification, including low-fat or MCT diets, is highly effective and should remain as the first-line treatment. Surgical intervention is rarely required but may be appropriate in select cases. Future research should aim to thoroughly document the incidence of chyle leak in breast cancer patients, to understand the relationship between the right breast, axilla, and the thoracic duct, and to optimize treatment protocols for this uncommon but challenging complication.
