Leaflet fracture and escape from an On-X mechanical mitral valve in a child: a case report

[b00001] Abstract / Background
Leaflet escape is a rare complication of mechanical heart valves. The following case is the first report of leaflet escape from an On-X mechanical mitral valve in a child.

[b00002] Abstract / Case summary
A 12-year-old boy presented with acute pulmonary oedema one year after mitral valve replacement with an On-X valve for rheumatic heart disease. Transoesophageal echocardiogram diagnosed an absent mechanical mitral valve leaflet and severe mitral regurgitation for which the patient successfully underwent urgent mitral valve replacement. Computed tomography located fragments of the embolised mitral valve leaflet at the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta and within the right common iliac artery. In the absence of impaired lower limb perfusion, the leaflet fragments were left in situ. He was discharged without complication and remains asymptomatic three months post-discharge.

[b00003] Abstract / Discussion
Leaflet fracture and escape is a known but rare complication of mechanical valve prostheses. The first-line investigation for suspected mechanical valve dysfunction is transoesophageal echocardiogram which elicited the diagnosis on this occasion.

[b00004] None
Leaflet fracture and escape should be considered when a patient with a mechanical valve presents with sudden-onset haemodynamic or respiratory compromise.

[b00005] None
When available, transoesophageal echocardiography should be used as the first-line modality for diagnosis of leaflet escape from a mechanical valve.

[b00006] Introduction
Leaflet escape is a rare but recognized complication of mechanical heart valves. There have been four previous reports in the literature of leaflet escape from an On-X mechanical valve with two cases resulting in death. We report the first case involving a child who survived leaflet escape and is currently asymptomatic 3 months after the event.

[b00011] Case report
A 12-year-old boy weighing 75 kg (body mass index 31 kg/m^(2)) presented to a regional Emergency Department with acute-onset pulmonary oedema after diving into water from a height of ∼2 m. His background includes rheumatic heart disease for which he underwent mitral valve repair with a 28 mm Physio II ring, tricuspid valve repair and aortic valve repair at age 9 years. He subsequently developed haemolytic anaemia and underwent mitral valve replacement with a 25 mm On-X valve at 11 years of age. He was anticoagulated with warfarin targeting an international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.5–3.5, and there were no other regular medications. At the most recent clinic visit 3 months prior to presentation, there was normal prosthetic valve function with a mean mitral inflow gradient of 1 mmHg and no regurgitation. There was moderate aortic regurgitation.

[b00012] Case report
On presentation, he was normotensive (blood pressure 123/67) and tachycardic with a heart rate of 140 beats per minute. He was tachypnoeic (respiratory rate 60 per minute) and desaturated with oxygen saturations of 75% in room air which improved to 93% with 15 L of oxygen. Clinical examination revealed a mechanical first heart sound with loud systolic and diastolic murmurs at the apex. There were crepitations to the mid-zones bilaterally. The INR was therapeutic (2.9). He was established on high-flow nasal cannulae oxygen and transferred to the tertiary paediatric intensive care unit. On admission, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mean mitral inflow gradient of 20 mmHg with no obvious mitral regurgitation; however further assessment of the mechanical mitral valve was limited by image quality. There was moderate aortic regurgitation which was unchanged from the previous assessment and left ventricular systolic function was hyperdynamic. There was no echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. His respiratory status deteriorated with hypoxia secondary to pulmonary oedema prompting intubation and ventilation. Inotropes were commenced to support cardiac function. Transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrated an absent mechanical valve leaflet causing severe mitral regurgitation (Figures 1–3,see supplementary material online, Videos S1–S3). Over the following two hours, he deteriorated with an escalating requirement for respiratory and circulatory support. He was therefore urgently transferred to the operating room for mechanical mitral valve replacement.

[b00013] Case report
The intraoperative findings confirmed absence of the right-sided leaflet of the On-X mitral valve prosthesis. It was not seen on inspection of the left atrium, left atrial appendage or left ventricle. The valve ring and remaining leaflet was explanted and a 27 mm St. Jude mitral valve prosthesis was inserted. Three days following surgery, a computed tomography scan demonstrated a fractured mitral valve leaflet consisting of two segments; one located at the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta and the other within the right common iliac artery (Figure 4). In the absence of impaired lower limb perfusion, these fragments were left in situ. The child was discharged 14 days after the initial presentation and was well at 3-month follow-up.

[b00014] Discussion
The On-X valve is a mechanical bi-leaflet prosthesis which was first implanted in 1996. The valve uses leaflets which open to 90°, a flared inlet orifice and full annular support which offer advantages in haemodynamic performance, and prevention of thrombosis and pannus formation. Our paediatric institution has implanted 51 On-X valves in the mitral position in children (aged <16 years) over a 15-year period from 2007 to 2021, with the most common indication being rheumatic heart disease (37/51). This is the first time we have encountered leaflet escape from a mechanical valve. The incidence of leaflet escape from the On-X mechanical valve is unknown; however, the manufacturer has identified 24 similar events from 400 000 implants. This may underestimate the true incidence; however, as some cases may result in fatality without the diagnosis being identified.

[b00015] Discussion
Leaflet escape is an extremely rare but recognised complication of mechanical valve prostheses. This complication has been most commonly reported with the Bjork-Shiley convexo-concave tilting disc prosthesis; however it has also been reported with the Edwards-Duromedics and TEKNA bi-leaflet prostheses, the TRI Technologies mechanical valve and the Omnicarbon monoleaflet prosthesis. This is the fifth published case of leaflet escape from an On-X mechanical valve and the first case reported in a child. Of the four previous reports, three occurred in valves in the mitral position and one in the aortic position. The time from implantation to leaflet escape has varied from 1 year to 7 years. This case reported reinforces the fact that this complication can occur at any time after implantation, and highlights the importance of considering leaflet escape in anyone with a mechanical valve presenting with sudden-onset respiratory or circulatory compromise.

[b00016] Discussion
The diagnosis of leaflet escape in this case was not made with transthoracic echocardiography given the limited ability to visualise the prosthetic valve leaflets. This highlights the utility of transoesophageal echocardiography in determining the mechanism of mechanical valve dysfunction which accurately detected the absence of one of the mechanical valve leaflets. Considering this, we recommend that this modality be the first-line investigation when mechanical valve dysfunction is suspected, particularly for patients with suboptimal acoustic windows on transthoracic echocardiography. In settings where transoesophageal echocardiography is not readily available, previous reports have shown that fluoroscopy may support the diagnosis, whilst noting that it can be difficult to differentiate thrombosis from leaflet escape with this technique.^(,) Multi-detected computed tomography can also be a useful adjunct in the diagnosis of mechanical valve dysfunction, particularly if transthoracic and transoesophageal imaging is inadequate.

[b00017] Discussion
Of the previous reports of On-X valve leaflet escape, only one has demonstrated leaflet fracture. In the other cases, the embolised leaflet has been imaged or retrieved in its entirety. Whilst the mechanism of leaflet fracture and escape in this case is unknown, we propose that leaflet fracture allowed the segments to exit the hinge mechanism and embolise. The onset of symptoms after diving into water suggests rapid deceleration as a mechanism. Previous reports have documented symptom-onset with other physical activities including treadmill exercise and prolonged cycling. Whilst the embolised leaflet fragments have not been examined, the manufacturer examined the residual valve ring and leaflet extracted from the heart and reported no abnormalities. It is well known that mishandling at the time of implantation can damage the valve surface, predisposing the valve to structural dysfunction, leaflet escape or thrombus formation.

[b00018] Discussion
This case highlights a known but rare complication of On-X valve prostheses and the utility of transoesophageal echocardiography in establishing the diagnosis.
