Diagnostic Pitfalls in psychiatry: misdiagnosed psychosis in a patient with intellectual disability: a case report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction and importance:
Misdiagnosis is a substantial obstacle in psychiatry, often exposing patients to unwarranted risks.

[b00002] Abstract / Case presentation:
We present a 35-year-old male with longstanding developmental delays who was repeatedly diagnosed with psychosis and treated with antipsychotics. He developed catatonia during his course of treatment. A thorough re-evaluation, incorporating developmental history and neuropsychological assessment, confirmed intellectual disability rather than primary psychosis.

[b00003] Abstract / Clinical discussion:
This example illustrates how diagnostic overshadowing and neglecting developmental history can result in treatment errors, such as catatonia due to improper antipsychotic medication. The results were much better after the right diagnosis and a move to psychosocial and behavioral therapy.

[b00004] Abstract / Conclusion:
A comprehensive developmental history and periodic diagnostic reassessment are essential in psychiatry to prevent harmful misdiagnosis and optimize patient care.

[b00005] Introduction
Intellectual disability (ID) is defined by limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior with onset during the developmental period^([]). Behavioral disturbances are common in these patients, and misinterpretation often leads to misdiagnosis of psychotic or mood disorders^([,]). Misdiagnosis not only exposes patients to unnecessary medications but also risks complications such as catatonia^([]).

[b00006] Introduction
Diagnostic errors in psychiatry often arise from over-reliance on observable behaviors without sufficient developmental context, a phenomenon well-documented in recent literature^([,]). In individuals with ID, this leads to “diagnostic overshadowing,” where psychiatric symptoms are attributed incorrectly to psychosis rather than to cognitive limitations or environmental stressors.

[b00007] Introduction
This case describes a patient with ID misdiagnosed as psychosis and managed with antipsychotics, later complicated by catatonia. Timely recognition of ID corrected the diagnostic trajectory and improved outcomes which is in accordance with CARE case report guidelines^([]) and also complies with the TITAN Guidelines 2025 for declaration and use of AI^([]). HIGHLIGHTS A patient with long-standing developmental deficits was repeatedly misdiagnosed as psychosis, resulting in catatonia due to antipsychotic exposure. A comprehensive reevaluation verified the diagnosis of intellectual disability, which led to a change in care to include psychological and behavioral support. The case underscores the imperative for a thorough developmental history and ongoing diagnostic reevaluation to prevent adverse mismanagement in psychiatry.

[b00008] Introduction / Patient information
A 35-year-old male was brought to the emergency department for mutism, immobility, and refusal to eat. His past history revealed multiple hospitalizations over the last 2 years with a working diagnosis of schizophrenia, for which he was prescribed various antipsychotics including risperidone, olanzapine, and clozapine.

[b00009] Introduction / Patient information
Family members reported that since childhood he had delayed developmental milestones, limited schooling, poor social functioning, and lifelong dependence on family for basic activities. These features had been overlooked in earlier psychiatric assessments.

[b00010] Introduction / Clinical findings
Upon admission, the patient was discovered to be mute, immobile, and exhibiting negativism and posturing, which were consistent with catatonia. He lacked the ability to communicate, was unresponsive, and was poorly groomed. The physical examination was otherwise unremarkable. Table 1 below demonstrate the summarized timeline of the patient.

[b00019] Introduction / Diagnostic assessment
Routine investigations, such as neuroimaging, liver and renal function, and blood tests, were within normal limits. The electroencephalogram did not reveal any abnormalities. Moderate ID was confirmed through neuropsychological evaluation, which revealed deficiencies in the conceptual, social, and practical domains.

[b00020] Introduction / Diagnostic assessment
The revised diagnosis was intellectual disability with behavioral disturbance, as opposed to primary psychotic disorder.

[b00021] Introduction / Therapeutic intervention
A lorazepam challenge was administered to the patient during hospitalization, which partly alleviated catatonia. Antipsychotics were discontinued. The management plan was revised to include supportive interventions, family psychoeducation, and behavioral strategies. The family received instruction on structured caregiving strategies and realistic expectations.

[b00022] Introduction / Therapeutic intervention
Physicians played a pivotal role in recognizing the inappropriate continuation of antipsychotics and coordinating multidisciplinary reassessment emphasizing the importance of physician awareness in preventing misdiagnosis.

[b00023] Introduction / Follow-up and outcomes
The patient exhibited reduced aggression, enhanced mobility, and increased interaction at the 3-month follow-up. The catatonic symptoms had not recurred. Nevertheless, the family reported improved functioning and a reduced caregiver burden, despite the persistent status of intellectual disability.

[b00024] Discussion
This case illustrates the repercussions of misdiagnosing intellectual disability as psychosis. As a consequence of protracted exposure to antipsychotics that failed to offer any significant benefit, the patient developed catatonia, a potentially life-threatening complication^([,]). Physician-related diagnostic responsibility is central to avoiding such pitfalls. Early and comprehensive assessment, including developmental history, caregiver input, and neuropsychological testing, remains a physician’s ethical and clinical duty.

[b00025] Discussion
Family, social, and cultural contexts frequently contribute to diagnostic error. In this case, caregiver distress and sociocultural stigma toward intellectual disability likely influenced both reporting and clinical interpretation. Studies have shown that stigma and lack of awareness often bias physicians toward labeling behavioral changes as “psychosis,” especially in resource-limited settings^([,]). Recognizing these psychosocial dimensions can prevent future misclassification and promote holistic management.

[b00026] Discussion
Catatonia is becoming more widely acknowledged in ID, occasionally as a primary syndrome and occasionally as a secondary complication of antipsychotic use^([,]). Inadequate developmental history-taking and over-reliance on behavioral symptoms often lead to diagnostic errors^([]).

[b00027] Discussion
A careful developmental history, gathering collateral information from family members or caregivers, and, when possible, using neuropsychological assessments, is very crucial for accurate diagnosis^([]). Once intellectual disability is recognized, treatment should focus on practical supports such as psychosocial interventions, building daily living skills, and strengthening family involvement rather than relying mainly on medication^([]).

[b00028] Discussion
This case specifically demonstrates the importance of incorporating developmental aspects in psychiatric examinations and reassessing the diagnosis if a patient fails to react to therapy as expected.

[b00029] Discussion / Limitations
This report describes a single case with a short follow-up of 3 months. Broader generalization should be made cautiously, and further longitudinal studies are recommended.

[b00030] Discussion / Patient perspective
The family conveyed relief upon receiving the precise diagnosis. They said that knowing what the sickness really was helped them get better care and made them less angry.

[b00031] Conclusion
Misdiagnosis of intellectual disability as psychosis can lead to harmful treatments and complications such as catatonia. Comprehensive developmental assessment, physician vigilance, and attention to sociocultural context are essential to prevent such outcomes.
