Hypomagnesemia as a reversible complication of cyclosporine in pediatric nephrotic syndrome: a case report

[b00001] Abstract
Calcineurin inhibitors remain a cornerstone in the treatment of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) in pediatric patients. Hypomagnesemia, a clinically relevant yet frequently neglected complication of calcineurin inhibitors like cyclosporine, may exacerbate hypokalemia and induce neuromuscular symptoms such as tetany, challenging patient management. However, serum magnesium is not routinely monitored in many clinical settings. A 46-month-old girl with SRNS had been receiving high-dose cyclosporine for over 1 year. She was admitted with vomiting, diarrhea, generalized edema, and hypovolemic shock. After stabilization, she developed persistent hypokalemia refractory to supplementation, despite normal renal function and basic electrolyte panel. On hospital day 10, carpal-type tetany prompted the first measurement of serum magnesium, revealing severe hypomagnesemia (0.14 mmol/L). Cyclosporine was temporarily withheld, and intravenous then oral magnesium was administered. Tetany resolved within 48 hours, and both serum magnesium and potassium levels normalized. This case highlights the risk of delayed recognition of symptomatic hypomagnesemia during prolonged cyclosporine therapy in children. Tetany was the first neurological manifestation of a preventable complication. Current guidelines lack clear recommendations regarding magnesium surveillance in pediatric SRNS receiving cyclosporin. This case underscores a critical gap in practice and calls for the integration of magnesium monitoring into standard care for this vulnerable population.

[b00002] Introduction
Cyclosporine (CsA) is a widely used calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) for posttransplant immunosuppression and for various autoimmune diseases, including steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) in children []. While effective in immune modulation, CsA has a well-documented profile of adverse effects, particularly renal tubular dysfunction leading to electrolyte imbalances [-]. Among these, hypomagnesemia is an often overlooked but clinically significant disturbance, predominantly caused by increased urinary magnesium excretion.

[b00003] Introduction
Due to its often silent clinical course and nonspecific symptoms, serum magnesium levels are frequently not monitored routinely in clinical practice. However, severe magnesium depletion can present with neuromuscular irritability, including muscle twitching, tetany, seizures, or altered mental status, especially in vulnerable pediatric patients [,]. Moreover, hypomagnesemia is a recognized cause of refractory hypokalemia, further complicating the clinical picture if not promptly identified and corrected []. Although hypomagnesemia has been described in transplant recipients, reports of symptomatic hypomagnesemia, particularly those manifesting with tetany or seizures, in children receiving CsA for SRNS remain rare.

[b00004] Introduction
This report presents a pediatric case of symptomatic hypomagnesemia with neurological involvement, emerging during relatively high-dose CsA therapy for SRNS. The case highlights the importance of proactive electrolyte monitoring and the diagnostic value of considering magnesium deficiency in children presenting with unexplained hypokalemia or neurological symptoms while on CNIs.

[b00005] Case report
A 46-month-old girl diagnosed with SRNS was confirmed to have minimal change disease on renal biopsy. After failing to full-dose prednisolone, she was started on alternate-day prednisolone and CsA. Serial outpatient monitoring showed subtherapeutic CsA trough levels, prompting gradual dose escalation from 3.50 to 13.3 mg/kg/day over 1 year. This dose was maintained for several months before admission. At that time, the CsA trough level was 83 ng/mL–within the therapeutic range (80–120 ng/mL). The patient was adherent to treatment and had not received any diuretics, aminoglycosides, or other agents affecting electrolyte balance. She was admitted on the second day of illness with only one episode of vomiting and two loose stools with no signs of ongoing gastrointestinal losses, fatigue, and periorbital and lower limb edema. The patient's weight increased from 12 kg at baseline to 16 kg on admission. On arrival, she was in hypovolemic shock. Prompt fluid resuscitation led to stabilization of hemodynamic parameters. Initial laboratory investigations revealed normal complete blood count, coagulation profile, and liver function tests. Renal function and serum potassium levels are shown in Fig. 1. Serum sodium, chloride, total and ionized calcium remained within normal ranges. Hypoalbuminemia was significant (13.3 g/L), with total serum protein at 40.4 g/L. A 24-hour urine collection showed nephrotic-range proteinuria (1.10 g/m²/day), and the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio was 623 mg/mmol. Abdominal ultrasound revealed diffuse bowel wall edema and a large volume of simple ascites. Both kidneys were normal in size, with increased cortical echogenicity and preserved corticomedullary differentiation. Of note, the patient exhibited persistent and pronounced hypokalemia despite clinical improvement and resolution of vomiting and diarrhea. She was not taking any medications known to cause renal or gastrointestinal potassium loss. Potassium supplementation was initiated promptly, initially via intravenous infusion, followed by oral therapy, at doses appropriate for pediatric care. Nevertheless, serum potassium levels remained low, with only minimal improvement observed. This refractory hypokalemia persisted for nearly 10 days, suggesting a poor response to standard replacement therapy (Fig. 1). The unexpectedly persistent and treatment-refractory hypokalemia prompted further investigation for underlying causes (Fig. 2) []. Urinalysis showed a markedly elevated urinary potassium-to-creatinine ratio (72.9 mEq/g), indicating renal potassium wasting. The patient’s blood pressure exceeded the 95th percentile plus 12 mmHg for age, meeting criteria for stage 2 hypertension per the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines. This finding raised suspicion of renal or endocrine contributions to the electrolyte disturbance. Serum renin and aldosterone levels were within normal limits, ruling out hyperaldosteronism. Arterial blood gas revealed mild metabolic alkalosis (pH 7.48; HCO_(3)⁻ 24.7 mmol/L). A markedly elevated urinary chloride level (1,108 mmol/L) suggested renal salt loss. With no history of diuretic use and no clinical or laboratory evidence of Gitelman or Bartter syndrome, hypomagnesemia was considered the most likely underlying mechanism. Interestingly, during ongoing diagnostic evaluation, the patient suddenly developed clinical tetany, manifesting as carpal spasm coinciding with a spike in blood pressure during routine measurement (Fig. 3). This unexpected neurological event prompted immediate testing of serum magnesium, which had not been assessed previously. Results revealed severe hypomagnesemia (0.14 mmol/L) and persistent hypokalemia, while serum total and ionized calcium remained within normal limits. The patient was promptly treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate, resulting in resolution of tetany within hours. In the following days, repeat electrolyte testing showed marked improvement in both magnesium and potassium levels. Prior to the onset of tetany, the patient exhibited no overt neurological signs such as muscle weakness, tremor, or diminished reflexes. Subclinical symptoms may have gone unnoticed, but no clear neuromuscular dysfunction was observed. Electrocardiogram findings remained normal, without arrhythmias or QTc prolongation. Upon reviewing potential causes, CsA was identified as the likely etiology of hypomagnesemia. It was temporarily discontinued to prevent further electrolyte disturbances. The patient was transitioned to oral magnesium supplementation, with continued inpatient monitoring for four additional days. A sustained clinical and biochemical improvement was observed, and serum electrolyte levels remained stable throughout the remainder of the hospitalization. Oral magnesium with vitamin B_(6), consisting of magnesium lactate dihydrate plus vitamin (vitamin B_(6)), was continued for 1 week after discharge. Each tablet contained 50 mg of elemental magnesium and 5 mg of vitamin B_(6), and the pediatric patient received 25 mg/kg/day calculated as elemental magnesium. Notably, no potassium supplementation was required. The patient was scheduled for outpatient follow-up to reassess serum electrolyte levels and to re-evaluate the appropriateness of continuing CsA therapy.

[b00006] Discussion
As outlined above, CsA remains a cornerstone therapy for pediatric SRNS and is endorsed as first-line immunosuppression by international guidelines []. Nevertheless, its clinical benefit is tempered by under-recognized electrolyte disturbances–most notably hypomagnesemia, which can precipitate refractory hypokalemia.

[b00007] Discussion
CsA induces renal magnesium loss primarily by impairing reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule (DCT), which accounts for 5% to 15% of filtered magnesium reabsorption. This depends on transient receptor potential melastatin (TRPM)6 activity on the apical membrane of DCT epithelial cells, regulated by epidermal growth factor (EGF). EGF binds basolateral receptors, enhancing TRPM6-mediated transport. CsA downregulates both EGF production and TRPM6 surface expression, reducing magnesium reabsorption even in hypomagnesemia []. These findings align with in vivo rat data showing CsA suppression of TRPM6, TRPM7, and EGF in the DCT, causing sustained renal magnesium wasting. While intracellular magnesium shifts may occur early in CsA therapy, prolonged use results in persistent renal wasting as the dominant mechanism [,]. Supporting this, a retrospective study in pediatric transplant recipients on CNIs found 33% had hypomagnesemia, and 66% of those had increased fractional magnesium excretion, highlighting renal wasting as the key pathological process []. This effect is particularly pronounced in patients with underlying tubular injury, such as children with nephrotic syndrome, where CsA exacerbates the predisposition to magnesium loss []. Chronic magnesium deficiency further disinhibits the renal outer medullary potassium channels in the DCT, promoting urinary potassium wasting and contributing to refractory hypokalemia. Additionally, magnesium is an essential cofactor for Na^(+)/K^(+)-ATPase activity; thus, hypomagnesemia impairs the pump’s function, further compromising potassium reabsorption. Taken together, CsA-induced hypomagnesemia is not only sustained but also reinforces a vicious cycle of treatment-resistant electrolyte disturbances [].

[b00008] Discussion
Although mild hypomagnesemia often presents without overt symptoms and is easily overlooked, particularly in clinical settings where routine monitoring of serum magnesium is not standard practice, the neurological consequences of severe magnesium deficiency can be significant. Detection typically occurs only when patients exhibit persistent electrolyte disturbances or present with neuromuscular symptoms such as muscle spasms, fasciculations, tetany, or seizures. Mechanistically, magnesium deficiency increases neuronal excitability by lowering axonal stimulation thresholds and facilitating excessive presynaptic calcium influx, leading to heightened neurotransmitter release []. Recently, a systematic review published in 2023 contributed to raising awareness of this issue by demonstrating that hypomagnesemia may lead to a broad spectrum of neurological manifestations, extending beyond the traditionally recognized tetany or seizures. Among 60 cases reviewed, the most commonly observed symptoms included: ataxia (48.3%), postural tremor (23.3%), dysarthria (21.7%), seizures (36.7%), and downbeat nystagmus (26.7%). Importantly, nearly 30% of these cases exhibited persistent neurological sequelae after treatment, such as chronic ataxia, persistent dizziness, or mild cognitive impairment [].

[b00009] Discussion
In the current body of literature, neurological complications observed in patients treated with CsA are predominantly attributed to the drug’s intrinsic central neurotoxicity, such as posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, rather than to hypomagnesemia [,]. However, a landmark study reported that all 12 bone marrow transplant recipients who developed neurological symptoms during CsA therapy had profound hypomagnesemia. Notably, their symptoms, including seizures, ataxia, tremor, depression, and transient aphasia, significantly improved following magnesium supplementation, even as CsA treatment continued, suggesting that hypomagnesemia may independently contribute to the pathogenesis of CsA-induced neurotoxicity []. Despite these findings, similar evidence remains scarce in pediatric patients with SRNS, a group at high risk due to prolonged CsA therapy [].

[b00010] Discussion
Our case involved a pediatric SRNS patient receiving prolonged high-dose CsA therapy (up to 13.3 mg/kg/day to reach therapeutic levels), where the onset of tetany served as a critical turning point in the diagnostic process, leading to the identification of hypomagnesemia. Although CsA dosing in this patient was guided by therapeutic trough levels, the relatively high total daily dose may itself represent an independent risk factor for hypomagnesemia, one that was not initially recognized in this case due to the lack of early magnesium monitoring. This highlights the importance of routine electrolyte surveillance in patients receiving high-dose CNIs, even when trough levels remain within the therapeutic range. In reality, the failure to recognize CsA-induced hypomagnesemia can lead to persistent hypokalemia and complicate treatment, reflecting a common gap in clinical practice where serum magnesium monitoring is often omitted in patients receiving CsA. Importantly, in this case, serum magnesium was not measured until the 10th day of hospitalization, following the onset of tetany. Given the persistent, treatment-resistant hypokalemia and absence of other obvious causes, earlier consideration of magnesium deficiency may have enabled more timely intervention. This diagnostic delay reflects a common gap in routine electrolyte assessment and underscores the need to maintain a high index of suspicion for hypomagnesemia, particularly in children receiving prolonged or high-dose CsA. Early recognition is critical, as delayed detection may result in avoidable complications and prolonged hospitalization. In addition, the fractional excretion of magnesium (FEMg) could not be measured to investigate the underlying cause of hypomagnesemia due to the urgency of magnesium replacement and the patient’s inability to provide a urine specimen at the time of symptom onset. Furthermore, several clinical factors–such as recent fluid resuscitation, transient renal impairment, and concurrent hypokalemia–may have interfered with the accuracy or interpretation of FEMg, had it been obtained.

[b00011] Discussion
Although hypomagnesemia is a well-documented electrolyte disturbance associated with CsA therapy, standardized protocols for serum magnesium monitoring remain absent from major clinical guidelines. The 2020 recommendations from the International Pediatric Nephrology Association do not specify a routine monitoring interval, however, they advise oral magnesium supplementation in patients presenting with clinical signs of hypomagnesemia, and recommend serum magnesium assessment when risk factors are present, such as prolonged diarrhea, vomiting, loop diuretic use, or dose escalation of CsA []. Additional international guidance, including expert consensus from India, supports periodic monitoring every 3 to 6 months alongside serum creatinine and other electrolytes during CsA treatment []. Notably, the official prescribing information for Neoral (Food and Drug Administration-approved oral CsA formulation) offers more precise recommendations: serum magnesium, along with potassium and lipid profiles, should be assessed biweekly during the initial 3 months of therapy, and monthly thereafter if the clinical condition remains stable []. Based on our experience and existing literature, we recommend routine monitoring of serum magnesium during CsA therapy, particularly at moderate to high doses (>5 mg/kg/day), or those with additional risk factors for electrolyte disturbances (Table 1). A suggested monitoring protocol is provided to assist clinicians in early identification of electrolyte disturbances.

[b00012] Discussion
Taken together, current evidence underscores the necessity of integrating serum magnesium monitoring into standardized follow-up protocols, particularly during treatment initiation and dose titration of CsA. This strategy not only enables the early identification and management of hypomagnesemia but also improves outcomes in complex electrolyte disturbances such as refractory hypokalemia, while mitigating the risk of severe neurological complications. Drug-related problems can be identified through routine medication management by evaluating the appropriateness of indication, dosage form, dose, timing of administration, and potential drug interactions based on established guidelines []. This is especially critical in vulnerable pediatric populations, such as children with SRNS.

[b00013] Discussion
In conclusion, CsA-induced hypomagnesemia often presents insidiously and is easily overlooked in clinical practice, especially in children receiving treatment for SRNS. The presented case highlights the importance of recognizing this complication when persistent electrolyte disturbances or nonspecific neurological symptoms occur. Magnesium supplementation significantly improved refractory hypokalemia, underscoring the essential role of magnesium in maintaining internal homeostasis. Routine magnesium monitoring should be considered in pediatric patients receiving high-dose or prolonged CsA therapy, particularly those with additional risk factors for magnesium loss.

[b00014] Discussion
This report has several limitations. First, the patient received a higher-than-usual dose of CsA, which may have increased the risk of nephrotoxicity and renal magnesium wasting. Therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to patients receiving standard CsA doses. However, it is important to note that even within therapeutic trough levels, cumulative drug exposure and individual susceptibility can vary. As such, electrolyte monitoring remains advisable regardless of dose intensity. Second, due to the acute presentation of symptomatic hypomagnesemia, we were unable to obtain a urine sample for FEMg, limiting our ability to confirm renal magnesium loss. Additional limitations include the lack of baseline magnesium levels prior to CsA initiation and the single-case nature of the report, which precludes broad conclusions about incidence or causality.
