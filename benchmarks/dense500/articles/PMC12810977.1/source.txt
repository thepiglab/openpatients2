Plan of Management of Adrenal Incidentaloma Diagnosed with Carcinoma Colon: A Case Report and Review of Literature

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
During staging workup of colorectal cancer (CRC), adrenal incidentalomas (AIs) are frequently identified with an incidence of approximately 9–13%. Among AIs, adrenal adenomas are most commonly detected followed by adrenal myelolipomas (AMLs), presenting a diagnostic and management challenge in cancer staging.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
We present a case of well-differentiated carcinoma of the caecum in a male patient who presented with a right iliac fossa mass and severe anaemia without any comorbidities. Colonoscopic biopsy confirmed our diagnosis. During staging workup, CT imaging of abdomen revealed a coincidental left adrenal mass suggestive of myelolipoma. Endocrinal evaluation was performed to rule out functional status of the tumour. It was a normetanephrine secreting tumour though other parameters for pheochromocytoma were within normal limit creating diagnostic confusion. The patient underwent simultaneous right hemicolectomy and left adrenalectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of AML concurrent with carcinoma caecum. The patient’s post-operative course was uneventful. Patient received complete adjuvant treatment and under regular follow-up.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion
This case highlights the importance of detailed preoperative evaluation and management of AIs discovered during staging workup of colon carcinoma. While most AIs are benign, appropriate radiological and functional assessment is crucial for optimal surgical planning. Our case demonstrates the need for simultaneous resection of CRC and AML when the diagnosis is inconclusive.

[b00004] Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer type worldwide after lung and breast cancer. During diagnostic and staging workup of CRC, adrenal incidentalomas (AIs) are frequently detected on imaging studies of the abdomen specially on computed tomography (CT) []. An AI is defined as an adrenal mass without clinical symptoms, detected unexpectedly during radiologic examination that is done for indications not related to adrenal disease [].

[b00005] Introduction
The incidence of AIs in CRC patients appeared to be approximately 9–13% [, ]. The majority of AIs are lipid-ridge adrenocortical adenomas without any secretory activities. Adrenal myelolipomas (AML) are the second most common AIs after adrenal adenomas detected radiologically. Other less common causes for adrenal abnormalities are subclinical hypercortisolism (Cushing’s syndrome), pheochromocytomas, adrenal cysts, metastases, aldosteronomas, ganglioneuromas, and adrenocortical carcinomas, with incidences ranges between 0.2 and 6%. The detection of AI during staging workup for any malignancy pose a challenge for the treating surgeon regarding the diagnosis and management as coincidentally detected adrenal masses may be a functional tumour or metastasis.

[b00006] Introduction
AMLs are rare benign adrenal tumours, containing adipose and haematopoietic tissue, a result of reticuloendothelial cell metaplasia. It is sporadic with an overall incidence reported 0.08%–0.4% at autopsy previously, compared with increased incidence of 10%–15% recently due to the widespread use of imaging modalities like CT. It involves both the genders equally, mostly unilateral, and usually occurs in the fifth and seventh decades of life. Specific adrenal protocol has been followed to detect AML radiologically. The functional aspect of AI (including AML) should always be evaluated as radiological imaging may not be confirmatory [].

[b00007] Case Report
A 52-year-old male presented with lump in right iliac fossa (RIF) and weakness for 3 months. He was non-hypertensive, non-diabetic. On physical examination, severe pallor was present. A well circumscribed non-tender lump of size 5.5 × 4.5 cm was palpable in RIF on deep palpation with restricted mobility. Patient received few units of blood transfusion before presenting to us for severe anaemia.

[b00008] Case Report
Laboratory investigations revealed presence of severe anaemia (Haemoglobin 5.4 gm/dL) and hypoproteinaemia. CT scan of abdomen and pelvis with contrast revealed a circumferential neoplastic lesion in caecum with colo-colic intussusception into the ascending colon (Fig. 1, 2) with a heterogeneously enhancing round left adrenal lesion measuring 5.5 × 5.3 cm. CT scan with adrenal protocol was done for the adrenal lesion suggestive of AML (Fig. 3).

[b00009] Case Report
Colonoscopy was performed and biopsy was taken from the RIF mass. Histopathology report came to be a well-differentiated carcinoma of caecum.

[b00010] Case Report
Further evaluation was done to rule out the secretary activity of the left sided adrenal lesion. Biochemical investigations showed plasma-free metanephrine 11.80 ng/L (normal range 7.90–88.70 ng/L) which was within normal limit but plasma normetanephrine was 218.00 ng/L (normal range 20.10–135.40) which was elevated. Plasma aldosterone renin ratio and serum cortisol levels were within normal limits. Twenty-four hours urinary vanillylmandelic acid and urinary fractionated metanephrine levels were also within normal limit.

[b00011] Case Report
Considering the left adrenal lesion as normetanephrine secreting tumour and as per the size criteria of the AML the decision was made in multidisciplinary tumour board to proceed with left adrenalectomy along with right hemicolectomy. The patient underwent right hemicolectomy with left adrenalectomy (Fig. 4). A completely encapsulated adrenal tumour was removed intact and sent for histopathological examination. Post-operative period was uneventful.

[b00012] Case Report
Histopathological examination revealed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma of ascending colon with all margins free. Nine out of 31 lymph nodes were positive for invasive carcinoma. Lympho-vascular and perineural invasion was present (pT3N2b) (Fig. 5). On the other hand, the left adrenal lesion came to be adrenal lipoma with haemorrhagic infarction and fat necrosis (Fig. 6).

[b00013] Case Report
Patient received adjuvant treatment with oral Capecitabine 1,500 mg twice daily from day 1 to day 14 (repeated every 21 days) and Inj. Oxaliplatin 200 mg on day 1 (repeated every 21 days) for 8 cycles. Patient was doing well and under regular follow-up for last 1 year.

[b00014] Discussion with Review of Literature
The incidence of AIs in CRC patients appeared to be approximately 10%. The detection of AI during staging workup for any malignancy pose a challenge for the treating surgeon. First thing in this situation is to rule out adrenal metastasis though its incidence is low (0.4–1%). Adrenal metastases could be ruled out with proper radiological imaging following adrenal protocol. Therefore, the current accuracy of radiological imaging (CT scan and MRI) makes preoperative histopathological evaluation unnecessary in AI []. Adrenal metastases if found in colorectal carcinoma are mostly associated with systemic spread of disease and are considered surgically unresectable. However, aggressive surgical resection of adrenal metastases has been reported to improve overall survival [].

[b00015] Discussion with Review of Literature
Further we should evaluate all the adrenal masses for primary malignancy of adrenal gland. There is case report that shows that AML masquerading as an adrenal malignancy []. So in case of diagnostic dilemma, further evaluation like PET CT may be required.

[b00016] Discussion with Review of Literature
In our case, the diagnosis of AML was confirmed on typical radiological finding on CT scan and was planned for removal of tumour during right hemicolectomy because of its secretory activity and large size. AMLs are typically benign and predominantly composed of mature adipose tissue with intermixed myeloid component. AMLs are the second most common AIs after adrenal adenomas. Gierke [] initially described AML in 1905, and Oberling coined the term “myelolipomatoses” in 1929. Though these lesions are usually non-secreting in nature but can be functional on rare occasions like in our case.

[b00017] Discussion with Review of Literature
Rarely, AMLs are also found outside the adrenal glands. Extra-adrenal sites for AMLs include the retroperitoneum, thorax, and pelvis. Radiological imaging of AMLs shows presence of macroscopic fat which is pathognomonic. In CT scan with adrenal protocol, the lesions appear as well-circumscribed hypodense masses with an attenuation of −90 to −120 HU []. Similarly in MRI scan, the predominantly fatty areas in AML appear hyper intense on T1 MR images and intermediate to hyper intense on T2.

[b00018] Discussion with Review of Literature
Etiopathogenesis of these lesions is not definitively known. One hypothesis suggests that stimuli such as necrosis or inflammation could lead to the metaplasia of the reticuloendothelial cells, which could lead to the development of AMLs. Another hypothesis states that mesenchymal stem cells in the endothelium give rise to adipocytes which results in inflammation. Adrenal cortex secretes mediators in response to such inflammation and responsible for the recruitment of haematopoietic progenitors []. It is also hypothesized that excess adrenocorticotrophic hormone could be associated with the development of AMLs. This theory is supported by the increased incidence of AMLs in congenital adrenal hyperplasia, where the levels of adrenocorticotrophic hormone can be very high []. AML is often associated with conditions like Cushing disease, obesity, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, and diabetes, which can be considered as adrenal stimulants. AML was also reported with thalassaemia due to increased production of erythropoietin and usually these AMLs are giant []. In this case, we can hypothesize that long standing anaemia may lead to increased production of erythropoietin by kidney and ultimately responsible for the development of AML. Recent cytogenetic studies revealed that AML is a clonal tumour develops due to non-random X-chromosome inactivation [].

[b00019] Discussion with Review of Literature
The size of AMLs is variable and can range from a few millimetres to over 10 cm. In our case, the dimensions of AML were 7.6 × 6.5 × 4.6 cm. Giant AML is defined as diameter more than 10 cm. Akamatsu et al. [] described the largest AML without the endocrine disorder, largest diameter being measuring 31 cm and weighing 6 kg. Boudreaux et al. [] described the largest AML in a patient with congenital adrenal hyperplasia, size 34 × 24 × 10.5 cm, weight 5.9 kg.

[b00020] Discussion with Review of Literature
The previous guidelines published in 2009 by American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) did not suggest for metabolic or hormonal workup for AML. But recent AACE disease state clinical review suggested that incidentally discovered adrenal masses require dynamic and static hormonal measurements []. Endocrine dysfunction of AML is underappreciated. Measurement of serum catecholamines to diagnose of pheochromocytoma is not confirmatory and the sensitivity and specificity of plasma catecholamines are only 78.6% and 70.7%, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity are much more for plasma-free metanephrines or urinary fractionated metanephrines in pheochromocytoma. After thorough review of the literature, we found that 7% of AML and 11% of AIs were having secretary activities. That is why metabolic and functional workup should always be done in AMLs. Endocrine workup is beneficial in AML patient with hypertension, younger patients, diabetes or prediabetes, and those with bilateral AML [].

[b00021] Discussion with Review of Literature
Incidentalomas by definition are asymptomatic and are incidentally detected during investigations or workup for some other pathologies. Less commonly, patients present with symptoms such as abdominal, flank, or hypochondrial pain and more rarely dyspnoea, fever, virilization, and weight loss in case of secretary tumour. AMLs can be large enough to produce symptoms due to mass effect and complication like haemorrhage may occur occasionally. Isolated larger lesions (typically over 4 cm in size) and those predominantly composed of fat (>50%) have a greater propensity for a haemorrhagic event. Adrenal hormone excess might be detected in patients with AML in presence of concomitant adrenocortical adenoma or hyperplasia. Differential diagnoses based on imaging studies include retroperitoneal lipoma and liposarcoma, renal angiomyolipoma, retroperitoneal teratoma, adrenal cortical adenoma and adrenal carcinoma. The diagnosis of AML with low fat content may be difficult with cross sectional imaging as mentioned in various literature.

[b00022] Discussion with Review of Literature
Size of the tumour, its secretary potentiality and the presence of symptoms are the key deciding factors in management of AML. Small lesions measuring <5 cm and those who are asymptomatic are usually monitored via imaging over a period of 1–2 years. Even asymptomatic tumours need to be removed if there are suspicious for malignancy or grow in size during the follow-up period. According to various studies, it is suggested that symptomatic tumours or AMLs larger than 5 cm should undergo elective surgical excision though there are no uniform guidelines present [–]. This recommendation is based upon the fact that spontaneous rupture and haemorrhage within the large lesions may produce life-threatening emergencies []. The surgical access is totally dependent on the size of the lesion and the expertise of the surgeon. Minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques are best utilized for smaller-sized lesions. The midline laparotomy is always be preferred for masses larger than 10 cm or in cases of expected adhesions and infiltration to the surrounding viscera. Nevertheless, laparoscopic adrenalectomy has been successfully utilized in some centres even for large AMLs. Follow-up is absolutely mandatory in all cases managed conservatively to detect complications early. After thorough review of literature, we did not find any evidence of malignant transformation of AMLs.

[b00023] Discussion with Review of Literature
Due to the limitations of current literature, especially the lack of high-quality randomized trials, the exact indications for surgery for patients with adrenal incidentaloma have not been defined. Although European Society of Endocrinology clinical practice guidelines suggest operative intervention in case of secretory tumours, patient with comorbidities and if incidentaloma diagnosed coincidentally during staging of intra-abdominal malignancies as found in our case []. Size of the tumour is no more a criterion for surgical excision rather it is used to define the approach of surgery whether minimal invasive or laparotomy.

[b00024] Conclusion
Most of the patients of AML are asymptomatic although some present with pain or even endocrine dysfunction. The specific use of CT and MRI scans has led to an increased detection of AML accurately. Treatment varies from observation and routine follow-up to surgical excision of the lesion when indicated. Multidisciplinary team discussion is always recommended to decide the best possible treatment. The indications for surgery are symptomatic patients with comorbidities, secretory tumours or suspicious for malignancy. Although adrenal incidentaloma is rare, physicians should be familiar with the current recommendations to choose the most appropriate treatment.
