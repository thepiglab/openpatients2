Endoscopic Ultrasound-Guided Pelvic Abscess Drainage Especially for Pediatric Patients: A Report of Two Cases

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Endoscopic ultrasound-guided pelvic abscess drainage (EUS-PAD) was reported for the first time in 2003. However, there were few reports focused on EUS-PAD in pediatric patients beforehand. This procedure is one of the minimally invasive methods of treating pelvic abscess, which is close to the rectum. Here, we report the cases of two pediatric patients who underwent EUS-PAD.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
Two pediatric patients were referred to our hospital for the treatment of pelvic abscesses associated with appendicitis. Using endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) guidance, a pelvic abscess was identified adjacent to the rectal wall. After successive dilatation through a guidewire during fluoroscopic imaging, the deployment of one or two double pigtail stents was performed. Following successful stent deployment, both patients experienced good courses without any complications. After addressing the consequences of complicated appendicitis, both patients underwent laparoscopic appendectomy, which resulted in favorable postoperative courses.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusions
EUS-guided drainage can be considered a safe and effective method for primarily dealing with pelvic abscesses and as an efficacious bridge-to-surgery strategy for pediatric patients.

[b00004] Introduction
Endoscopic ultrasound-guided pelvic abscess drainage (EUS-PAD) was initially reported in 2003 []. Following that, its effectiveness, safety, and long-term outcomes have been documented, particularly in Western countries [–]. Additionally, in Japan, one retrospective report revealed the effectiveness of EUS-PAD []. Moreover, this procedure offers several advantages, including addressing cosmetic concerns associated with wounds of percutaneous drainage and shortening the hospitalization period. However, EUS-PAD has not yet become the primary treatment option for pelvic abscesses. Moreover, the procedure has been less frequently performed in pediatric patients than in adults. Here, we report two cases of successful pelvic abscess treatment achieved by transrectal puncture and placement of the internal drainage stent, with no complications.

[b00006] Case 1
The patient was a 6-year-and-9-month-old male with a history of cleft lip surgery, 125 cm in height/25 kg in weight. The patient presented with fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and lower abdominal pain persisting for 1 week. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) imaging at another hospital suggested a pelvic abscess associated with appendicitis, and the patient was referred to our hospital. The result of the blood test demonstrated an increased inflammatory reaction, with a leukocyte count of 19,500/μL (reference range, 3.5–9.0 × 10^(9)/L) and a C-reactive protein level of 23.56 mg/dL (reference range, 0.00–0.30 mg/dL). Abdominal CT scan revealed thickening of the appendix wall and a 40 mm × 30 mm low-density area, adjacent to the rectum in the vesicorectal fossa.

[b00007] Case 1
Emergent surgery was considered high risk due to severe inflammation in the deep pelvis surrounded by complicated anatomy, and the patient’s young age. Therefore, we decided to assess the pelvic abscess drainage on the second day of hospitalization in addition to antibiotic therapy with meropenem.

[b00008] Case 1
The low endoscopic ultrasound (EUS, scope was GF-UCT260; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was performed. Ultrasound imaging of the rectum wall revealed an abscess adjacent to the wall without visible septa. A 19G fine-needle aspiration (FNA) needle (EZ shot 3 plus; Olympus) was utilized to puncture the abscess, and two guidewires (VisiGlide 2 angle; Olympus) were placed in the abscess cavity. The puncture site was then dilated with an ES dilator (7 Fr, 2.45 mm; Zeon Medical Japan), followed by dilation with a Soehendra biliary dilation catheter (4 Fr–7 Fr; Cook Japan). A 7-Fr, 4-cm double pigtail catheter (Zimmon; Cook Japan) was deployed to establish internal drainage between the abscess cavity and the rectal lumen. The procedure was accomplished without any complications.

[b00009] Case 1
An abdominal CT scan was acquired 12 days after the drainage and demonstrated almost complete disappearance of the pelvic abscess. Therefore, antibiotic administration was discontinued on the same day, and the patient was discharged on the 14th post-procedure day. The internal fistula stent was scheduled to be removed 1 month after the procedure. However, on the 19th day, part of the stent had prolapsed from the anal verge due to a reduction of the abscess cavity and hence was removed endoscopically. Thereafter, no suggestive recurrence of the abscess was observed, and a laparoscopic appendectomy was performed 3 months after the procedure, resulting in a favorable postoperative course.

[b00010] Case 2
The patient was a 5-year-and-6-month-old male with no remarkable history, 110 cm in height/19 kg in weight. The patient was admitted to another hospital with fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain. He was diagnosed with infectious enteritis and administered symptomatic treatment. Since his symptoms did not improve after 10 days, he underwent abdominal CT, which revealed a suspected pelvic abscess associated with appendicitis. He was referred to our hospital.

[b00011] Case 2
Blood tests revealed an increased inflammatory response: leucocytes 16,600/μL (reference range, 3.5–9.0 × 10^(9)/L), C-reactive protein 13.1 mg/dL (reference range, 0.00–0.30 mg/dL). Abdominal ultrasonography and CT revealed a 50 mm × 30 mm abscess without any septum in the vesicorectal fossa. Additionally, the thickening of the appendiceal wall, connected to the pelvic abscess, was observed. Meropenem was prescribed as an antibiotic therapy, and the symptoms gradually improved. However, there was no reduction in the pelvic abscess’s size, and CT findings revealed a well-defined abscess cavity with a thick capsule and no internal septation, consistent with a mature abscess suitable for drainage; EUS-guided drainage was performed on the 7th day of hospitalization.

[b00012] Case 2
As described in the previous case, an EUS scope (GF-UCT260; Olympus) was employed, and an abscess adjacent to the rectal wall was identified. A 19G EUS-FNA needle (EZ shot 3 plus; Olympus) was used to puncture the abscess, and two guidewires (VisiGlide2; Olympus) with angles were inserted into the abscess cavity. After dilation with the REN biliary dilation catheter (8 mm, max 8 atm, Kaneka Medix, Japan) at 5 atm, two 7 Fr, 4 cm double pigtail catheters (Zimmon; Cook, Japan) were deployed to establish internal drainage. The procedure was completed without any complications.

[b00013] Case 2
An abdominal CT scan on the 14th day confirmed shrinkage of the pelvic abscess, and the patient was discharged. On the 16th post-procedure day, a physical examination revealed partial prolapse of the internal fistula stents from the anal verge due to the reduction of the abscess cavity. Consequently, stents were removed endoscopically. Laparoscopic appendectomy was performed 4 months after the EUS-drainage procedure. No evidence of abscess recurrence or complications was observed.

[b00014] Discussion
Pelvic abscesses occasionally occur as a consequence of intestinal and gynecological inflammatory diseases or complications of abdominal surgery. Appendicitis is the most common cause of pelvic abscesses in pediatric patients. The complex pelvic anatomy, surrounded by vital organs, bowel loops, and vascular structures, can make abscess treatment technically challenging.

[b00015] Discussion
Conservative treatment may potentially be the first choice depending on the situation. However, relatively large pelvic abscesses (>4 cm) often cannot be resolved with antimicrobial therapy alone. If necessary, abscess drainage should be considered for source control []. Several approaches to drainage are available, including surgical, percutaneous, and transluminal techniques.

[b00016] Discussion
Previous reports suggest that preoperative examination of percutaneous or transluminal drainage before surgery is safe []. Since the first article on EUS-PAD was reported in 2003 [], EUS has become a viable method for accessing deep pelvic abscesses. Notably, EUS enables real-time visualization of the detailed pelvic anatomy, reducing the risk of puncturing adjacent organs and vessels. The small distance between the rectum and abscess cavity further contributes to the safety and technical ease of the procedure.

[b00017] Discussion
A low recurrence rate of pelvic abscesses has been reported previously with EUS-PAD, and the reported technical success rate of EUS-PAD ranges from 90 to 100%, with clinical success in 85–95% of cases. Major complications such as bleeding or stent migration occur in less than 5% of cases, and no procedure-related mortality has been reported [, , ]. However, some evidence indicates that the success rate for drainage of diverticular abscesses is lower than that for other etiologies []. In contrast, pediatric patients seldom experience pelvic abscesses due to diverticulitis. Therefore, the possibility of drainage failure in children is lower than in adults.

[b00018] Discussion
According to retrospective research involving adult patients, certain limitations exist to this technique in terms of the size, location, and maturity of the abscess. Stent deployment is considered suitable for mature abscesses larger than 40 mm, within 2 cm of the colonic lumen [, ]. Aspiration alone can be considered in other cases, particularly in small abscesses that cannot be resolved by antibiotics alone or if urgent drainage is necessary for immature collections to attain clinical instability. Additionally, aspiration can be considered in cases where the distance between the rectum and the abscess is >2 cm. However, aspiration alone is associated with higher recurrence rates [, ].

[b00019] Discussion
The primary drainage methods used are internal and external fistulas. Additionally, lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS) may be deployed. While LAMS adequately drains abscesses comprising a large amount of debris, challenges such as bleeding risk, cost-effectiveness, and perforation have been reported []. More data are needed regarding the safety of LAMS. Additionally, due to their short length, they are not suitable for draining abscesses more than 1 cm away from the transducer [].

[b00020] Discussion
The advantages of external fistulas include the ability to manage cleaning and drainage effectively, as well as ease of removal []. However, some disadvantages such as strong discomfort in the anal area and restrictions on movement due to the tube have been observed. Pediatric patients, in particular, may have more difficulty tolerating restrictions and discomfort compared to adults. Additionally, since external fistula tubes are easily removable, a risk exists that patients may remove them on their own.

[b00021] Discussion
In contrast, the advantage of creating an internal fistula is that it eliminates discomfort and activity restrictions for the patient. However, disadvantages include the possibility of stent loss and the necessity of endoscopic examination when the stent is removed. Pediatric patients often exhibit strong resistance to endoscopic procedures, necessitating sedation.

[b00022] Discussion
Double pigtail plastic stents are usually used for drainage. No strict indication exists for the duration of drainage or the method of follow-up for the abscess. Previous reports indicate postoperative CT evaluation within 1–3 weeks to monitor for stent migration or dislodgement and to confirm the resolution of the abscess [, ]. In pediatric patients, deployed drainage stents tend to come off through the anus spontaneously following the shrinkage of the abscess. This phenomenon is attributed to the shorter distance between the rectum to the anal verge in pediatric patients compared to adults. Similar occurrences have been reported in other documented cases [, ], with no serious clinical complications observed due to stent protrusion. Nevertheless, some ingenuity may be needed.

[b00023] Discussion
Considering the opinions of the surgeons responsible for the surgery in these two cases, little tissue adhesion was noted. This result may be due to the immediate shrinkage of the abscess and a decrease in the inflammation. In addition, transrectal drainage had no significant adverse effect as well Figure 1.

[b00024] Conclusions
A few reports focusing on EUS-PAD in pediatric cases are available. Therefore, room for debate regarding the safety, efficacy, and other aspects of the procedure still exists. We must select the appropriate cases and obtain sufficient informed consent when performing EUS-PAD in pediatric patients. Even though an endoscopic procedure is necessary for each stent insertion and removal, internal stents offer significant merits, including the absence of strict physical activity restrictions and minimal discomfort for the patient.

[b00025] Conclusions
While this study reports only two pediatric cases, we consider EUS-PAD to be a safe and effective treatment for pediatric patients with pelvic abscesses. Further studies are warranted to establish its long-term safety and efficacy.
