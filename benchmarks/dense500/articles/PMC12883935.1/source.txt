Buried Balloon Syndrome After Percutaneous Radiologic Gastrostomy: Two Case Reports and a Literature Review

[b00001] Abstract
Buried balloon syndrome is a rare condition in which a balloon-retained gastrostomy catheter becomes embedded within the gastric or abdominal wall, potentially leading to catheter dysfunction, leakage, local infection, or even peritonitis. Despite its clinical significance, this complication remains under-recognized and has been reported only in anecdotal case studies. In Korea, the shift from pigtail-retained to balloon-retained catheters for percutaneous radiologic gastrostomy has increased awareness of this uncommon adverse event. This report presents two cases of buried balloon syndrome following percutaneous radiologic gastrostomy and outlines their clinical features, imaging findings, and management approaches. Given the potential for serious consequences if unrecognized, increased awareness of this condition among healthcare providers—particularly interventional radiologists—is essential.

[b00002] INTRODUCTION
Percutaneous gastrostomy, a widely adopted procedure for patients with impaired oral intake, offers a less invasive alternative to surgical methods for long-term enteral feeding (). Gastrostomy placement techniques can be classified into the pull technique, which employs a transoral route to position the tube, and the push technique, which delivers the tube directly through the abdominal wall via percutaneous access (). Depending on the technique and operator preference, various internal retention mechanisms may be used, including bumper-, pigtail-, and balloon-retained catheters ().

[b00003] INTRODUCTION
Traditionally, pigtail-retained catheters were predominantly used for percutaneous radiologic gastrostomy (PRG) in Korea. However, their discontinuation has led to a shift toward balloon-retained catheters. While balloon-retained catheters offer certain advantages—such as fewer tube-related complications compared to pigtail types—they also introduce new challenges (). Notably, we encountered a unique complication associated with balloon-retained catheters: buried balloon syndrome (). Although this condition resembles the well-known buried bumper syndrome associated with bumper-retained catheters, it exhibits distinct features.

[b00004] INTRODUCTION
Herein, we report two cases of buried balloon syndrome following PRG and discuss their clinical features and implications.

[b00005] CASE REPORTS / Case 1
A 90-year-old male with a history of aspiration pneumonia and Parkinson’s disease visited the outpatient clinic with concerns about a possible gastrostomy site infection. Three weeks earlier, he had undergone PRG for dysphagia. The position of the stomach and interposing abdominal organs was identified using ultrasound and fluoroscopy. After aseptic preparation, local anesthesia was administered with 2% lidocaine. Gastropexy was performed using a single T-fastener (Cope Anchor Set; Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA). The stomach was punctured 1 cm away from the gastropexy site. The tract was dilated, and a 14-Fr balloon-retained catheter (Entuit; Cook Medical) was inserted through a 16-Fr peel-away introducer (Enteral Access Dilation System; AVANOS Medical, Alpharetta, GA, USA). The balloon was inflated with 7 mL of sterile distilled water in accordance with the instructions for use. No additional catheter tagging sutures were applied. The anchor was removed after 7 days.

[b00006] CASE REPORTS / Case 1
At the current outpatient visit, physical examination revealed mild tenderness around the gastrostomy site. The retention balloon of the catheter was also palpable, with approximately half of it exposed outside the skin. An abdominal CT scan revealed that the retention balloon was embedded in the abdominal wall, with the catheter dislodged from the stomach (Fig. 1). However, there was no evidence of intra-abdominal abscess or localized peritonitis. After the catheter was removed in the interventional radiology suite, a large dead space remained in the abdominal wall. The skin defect was too extensive to be closed with sutures alone. In the operating room, an 18-Fr gastrostomy catheter was inserted through the existing tract, followed by surgical wound debridement and closure.

[b00007] CASE REPORTS / Case 1
Antibiotic therapy was administered for one month to manage inflammation. The gastrostomy site showed progressive healing without signs of recurrent infection or leakage. At the three-month follow-up, the wound had completely epithelialized, and enteral feeding continued uneventfully through the replacement gastrostomy tube.

[b00008] CASE REPORTS / Case 2
An 83-year-old male with a history of hypoxic brain damage presented to the outpatient clinic with peristomal leakage at the PRG site. Three months earlier, PRG had been performed to prevent aspiration. Under image guidance, the stomach was punctured using an 18-gauge needle. A single T-fastener (Cope Anchor Set; Cook Medical) was deployed, and a 0.035-inch stiff guidewire was advanced through the same needle. The tract was dilated, and a 12-Fr balloon-retained catheter (Entuit; Cook Medical) was inserted via a 16-Fr peel-away introducer (Enteral Access Dilation System; AVANOS Medical). The balloon was inflated with 7 mL of sterile distilled water. The catheter was secured with an external bolster. The anchor was removed after 7 days, and the patient was discharged. No routine follow-up was scheduled after discharge. However, four days prior to the current visit, the catheter was accidentally dislodged and replaced with a 16-Fr balloon-retained catheter.

[b00009] CASE REPORTS / Case 2
Physical examination revealed erythema and a noticeable odor around the gastrostomy site. In the interventional radiology suite, lateral fluoroscopic imaging and cone-beam CT confirmed that the balloon was located within the gastrostomy tract in the abdominal wall (Fig. 2). To prevent peristomal leakage from the widened tract, the gastrostomy catheter was replaced with a 24-Fr balloon-retained catheter. The enlarged skin opening was approximated with a suture. Antibiotic therapy was administered for one month following the procedure. After three months, his oral intake had improved sufficiently to allow for removal of the gastrostomy catheter.

[b00011] DISCUSSION
Different gastrostomy catheter designs are associated with varying risks of dislodgement and related complications. Pigtail-retained catheters are prone to dislodgement because the pigtail loop can be easily released by external pulling (). However, they have the advantage of causing minimal injury to the tract tissues during dislodgement. In contrast, bumper-retained tubes provide greater resistance to dislodgement due to their intragastric fixation mechanism. Although the internal bumper offers strong anchorage against external pulling forces, repeated episodes can result in significant trauma to the tract tissues. Balloon-retained catheters serve as a middle ground between pigtail- and bumper-retained designs, offering a balance between reduced dislodgement risk and minimal tissue injury ().

[b00012] DISCUSSION
Buried bumper syndrome typically occurs as a late complication of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement. Its reported prevalence ranges from 0.3% to 2.4% (). Rigid internal bumpers can exert excessive pressure on the gastric wall if the external bumper is positioned too tightly. This persistent pressure predisposes gastric tissues to ischemia and eventual embedding of the internal bumper into the gastric wall, which is considered the primary etiological factor (). In contrast, buried balloon syndrome is a rare condition in which the balloon-retained gastrostomy catheter becomes embedded within the gastric or abdominal wall, potentially resulting in catheter dysfunction, leakage, local infection, or even peritonitis. Unlike buried bumper syndrome, buried balloon syndrome has been described only in a few anecdotal case reports, and its true prevalence remains unknown ().

[b00013] DISCUSSION
There are several possible explanations for the discrepancy in prevalence between buried bumper syndrome and buried balloon syndrome. First, balloons are typically softer than bumpers and exert pressure more diffusely across the gastric wall, thereby reducing the risk of ischemia and tissue necrosis. Second, bumper-retained catheters are generally preferred for initial PEG tube placements, while balloon-retained catheters are more commonly used for replacement procedures (). Patients undergoing replacement procedures typically have a mature stoma that is more resistant to mechanical trauma. However, in Korea, balloon-retained catheters are currently used for initial PRG, which involves insertion into immature stomas. This practice may increase the risk of early-onset buried balloon syndrome due to the stoma’s structural vulnerability.

[b00014] DISCUSSION
Currently, no standardized definition exists for distinguishing early- and late-onset buried balloon syndrome. In the context of buried bumper syndrome, cases presenting within one month of tube placement are generally considered early-onset, whereas those occurring beyond one month are classified as late-onset (). Our two cases illustrate distinct clinical patterns of both early- and late-onset presentations of buried balloon syndrome. The first case occurred within three weeks of tube placement. In contrast, the second case developed three months after the initial procedure and followed accidental dislodgement and reinsertion, suggesting a late-onset form with a different pathophysiological mechanism. Notably, the early-onset case exhibited a more severe clinical course, with rapid progression of tissue damage and external balloon exposure. This difference in severity may be attributed to the degree of tract maturation at the time of injury.

[b00015] DISCUSSION
A variety of factors may contribute to the development of buried balloon syndrome, reflecting a multifactorial pathogenesis centered on mechanical tension at the gastrostomy site. A key mechanical factor is excessive tension between the internal retention balloon and the external fixator, which can lead to localized ischemia and tissue necrosis. This tension may be exacerbated by several factors, including over-tightened gastropexy, balloon overinflation, or tight external bolstering. Patient-related factors also play an important role. These include impaired wound healing capacity (e.g., malnutrition or steroid use) and sudden weight changes, which may alter tract anatomy and increase mechanical stress (). Accidental dislodgement or excessive manipulation of the tube by the patient or caregiver may further contribute to tissue trauma. These factors highlight the importance of preventive measures.

[b00016] DISCUSSION
To prevent this complication, several strategies have been proposed in the literature. Immediately after gastrostomy catheter placement, the external bolster should be secured with minimal traction and without tension. Once the gastrostomy tract has healed, routine daily tube rotation and gentle inward advancement are recommended. After tube mobilization, a distance of 10–20 mm between the skin and the external fixator should be maintained to reduce pressure on the gastric wall (). In addition, the length of the external catheter should be measured and recorded. This measurement serves as a baseline reference for future comparisons and may help detect early signs of tube migration or internal embedding. Educating caregivers on proper tube maintenance is also an essential component of prevention.

[b00017] DISCUSSION
There is a significant difference in treatment focus between buried bumper syndrome and buried balloon syndrome. The main challenge in treating buried bumper syndrome lies in successfully removing the embedded retention bumper from the abdominal wall. If external traction fails to dislodge the catheter, an endoscopic or surgical approach becomes necessary. In contrast, in cases of buried balloon syndrome, tube removal is relatively straightforward once the retention balloon is deflated. However, treatment for buried balloon syndrome primarily centers on managing the abdominal wall tissue defect caused by prolonged pressure from the inflated balloon, which is considerably larger than a bumper.

[b00018] DISCUSSION
Management options for buried balloon syndrome range from conservative approaches to surgical intervention. In our experience, when patients do not exhibit significant peritoneal signs, non-surgical management is generally preferred as the initial strategy. This involves removing the existing tube and replacing it with a new catheter, ensuring that the retention balloon is correctly positioned within the stomach. Wound closure may be achieved using external suturing or endoscopic clipping. Appropriate pharmacologic therapy is also essential, as the gastrostomy site is frequently associated with inflammation or infection. However, surgical intervention is warranted in cases complicated by peritonitis, abscess formation, or failure of conservative treatment.

[b00019] DISCUSSION
In summary, this report describes two cases of buried balloon syndrome following PRG. Although buried balloon syndrome has been reported in the literature only rarely, it has emerged as a noteworthy complication in Korea, sometimes necessitating surgical management. Relevant healthcare providers, particularly interventional radiologists, should be aware of this underrecognized condition and its potential consequences. Future studies should aim to identify the incidence and risk factors associated with this syndrome and to establish standardized protocols for its prevention and treatment.
