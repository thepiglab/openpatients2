Severe myelomeningocele in the fourth pregnancy of a 29-year-old woman: a case report

[b00001] Abstract
Neural tube defects represent a heterogeneous and complex group of congenital abnormalities affecting the central nervous system. These defects occur during embryogenesis as a result of the failure of the neural tube to close completely. A highly clinically significant form is myelomeningocele, often referred to as open spina bifida or spina bifida aperta. Delayed diagnosis presents considerable challenges, and folate deficiency is an important risk factor. This case reports a severe myelomeningocele located in the lumbosacral region of a female neonate, identified at 28–29 weeks’ gestation during the only prenatal consultation of a 29-year-old woman who had three previous normal pregnancies and did not take folic acid (FA) supplementation. The delayed diagnosis contributed to the progression of degenerative and traumatic lesions in the neural tissue, leading to the development of Chiari malformation type II and hydrocephalus. These conditions had a profound impact on brain development, significantly increasing the severity of the case. Primary prevention through the periconceptional use of FA supplements has been shown in extensive research to significantly reduce the probability of neural tube defects, including myelomeningocele. For this reason, FA fortification programs as a public health measure have been implemented in many countries.

[b00002] Introduction
Neural tube defects represent a heterogeneous and complex set of congenital malformations involving the central nervous system, with a prevalence ranging from 0.5 to more than 10 per 1000 pregnancies []. These defects are divided into two main subgroups (open and closed defects), with prognosis varying on the level and extent of the affected area []. Among these conditions, the most clinically impactful and the most frequently encountered worldwide is myelomeningocele, often referred to as open spina bifida, which occurs in about one per 1000 live births [].

[b00003] Introduction
Myelomeningocele is a severe congenital anomaly characterized by the protrusion of the meninges, cerebrospinal fluid, and functional neural structures, such as nerve roots or the spinal cord, through a bony defect resulting from the failure of primary neurulation, a process that occurs between days 17 and 28 post-fertilization leaving the exposed neural tissue susceptible to damage []. The bony defect may occur in any part of the spine, but it is predominately observed in the lumbosacral region [].

[b00004] Introduction
Myelomeningocele is frequently associated with brain abnormalities. Among the abnormalities observed, hydrocephalus (94.5%) and Chiari malformation type II (89.1%) are the most frequently reported. Chiari malformation type II is characterized by the downward displacement of posterior fossa structures – including the brainstem, cerebellum, and fourth ventricle – through the foramen magnum []. These abnormalities profoundly impact brain development, contributing to cognitive deficits, impaired executive functions, stridor, and apnea, all of which account for the increased mortality associated with myelomeningocele.

[b00005] Introduction
Neural tube formation is a multistep, zipper-like and discontinuous process that involves both genetic factors and gene–nutrient, gene–environment, and gene–gene interactions []. The genetic contribution to myelomeningocele is thought to constitute 60–70%, although only a limited number of specific genes have been identified. Fewer than 10% of cases are syndromic, often involving trisomy 18, with the majority being non-syndromic and sporadic []. The recurrence risk for siblings is 2–5%, which represents a 20- to 50-fold increase compared with the general population prevalence of approximately one per 1000 [].

[b00006] Introduction
Non-genetic contributors to the development of myelomeningocele include insufficient folate intake – the most well-known risk factor [], multiple gestations [], maternal obesity [], maternal pregestational diabetes mellitus [], low socioeconomic status [], and certain medications such as antiepileptic drugs [].

[b00007] Introduction
Myelomeningocele can be diagnosed by determining maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) during 16 to 18 weeks of pregnancy. This method serves as an initial screening tool, with levels exceeding 2.5 times the median observed in up to 79% of pregnancies affected by open spina bifida []. When AFP levels in maternal serum are elevated, amniocentesis can serve as a diagnostic confirmation [].

[b00008] Introduction
Modern high-resolution ultrasound (US) can diagnose neural tube defects with 97% sensitivity and 100% specificity, minimizing the need for invasive tests such as amniocentesis []. In cases where US fails to establish a clear diagnosis additional imaging techniques including fetal karyotyping, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging may be adjunctive imaging options []. According to some studies, neonatal outcomes are generally unaffected when Caesarean (C) delivery is performed due to neural tube defects, which remain the most frequent indication. Other studies report that C delivery may benefit children with myelomeningocele by protecting the sac and reducing exposure of neural tissue to bacteria. As a result, many centers have established C delivery as a common approach in pregnancies affected by neural tube defects []. According to other studies, no differences were detected between the immediate and long-term outcomes in infants with meningomyelocele alone, analyzed according to the mode of delivery []. Current guidelines from numerous professional bodies do not consider myelomeningocele a mandatory reason for C delivery [, ]. Depending on the timing of diagnosis, gestational age and the severity of the myelomeningocele there are three management options: natural delivery with immediate layered closure after birth; prenatal closure at 26–27 weeks of gestation or C delivery []. Performing myelomeningocele surgery prenatally can enhance motor development and decrease shunt use by 30 months, although it carries potential risks for mother and fetus [].

[b00009] Introduction
The prognosis for myelomeningocele remains highly dependent on the timing of diagnosis and the extent of neural tissue damage in the uterus.

[b00010] Introduction
The primary approach to managing myelomeningocele is prevention. The most common environmental cause is folate deficiency, with most cases classified as “folic acid (FA)-sensitive” [, ]. FA enhances embryonic cell proliferation and supports deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation, which influences gene expression critical for early neural development []. FA supplementation, started at least one month before conception and continued through the first trimester, helps prevent neural tube defect by 60–70% [, ]. It is recommended that women of childbearing age consume 0.4 mg of FA daily. Women with a history of a pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect should take 4 mg of FA daily starting three months before conception and continuing through the first trimester []. International recommendations support FA fortification programs, which have reduced the prevalence of anencephaly and spina bifida by 28% []. In cases that are unresponsive to FA, alternative folates, such as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate or nucleotide precursors, may prevent this condition []. Vitamin B12 supplementation, a cofactor in folate metabolism, may also lower the risk of myelomeningocele []. Inositol supplementation has shown promise in high-risk pregnancies that are unresponsive to FA [, ].

[b00011] Introduction / Aim
This article highlights that myelomeningocele can arise from both genetic and environmental factors and may occur even when the previous three pregnancies were unaffected. It also emphasizes the major role of consistent and thorough prenatal care, which can improve outcomes by detecting the condition early, before it becomes more severe or irreversible. Additionally, the article underscores the significance of FA in prevention – either through fortification of common dietary staples that are regularly consumed or, when fortification is impractical or only partially implemented, through programs providing daily supplementation before and during pregnancy.

[b00012] Case presentation
We report a 29-year-old woman with three previous normal pregnancies and a currently unmonitored pregnancy. She first presented at 28–29 weeks of gestation with altered general condition, lower abdominal cramps, and transient lumbar pain. US examination revealed a female fetus in pelvic presentation with lumbosacral myelomeningocele. In this case, where the pregnancy was complicated by myelomeningocele, the decision was made to proceed with a C delivery to ensure the best possible outcome for the child.

[b00013] Case presentation
Medical history revealed a nonconsanguineous marriage, three previous normal pregnancies, and no concurrent diseases or familial congenital anomalies. She had not received FA supplementation either prior to conception or throughout the first trimester of pregnancy. Additionally, she did not attend prenatal check-ups, and maternal AFP concentrations in the serum were not measured from 16 to 18 weeks into the pregnancy.

[b00014] Case presentation
At birth, the newborn’s weight was 1100 g, length was 36 cm, head circumference was 27 cm, chest circumference was 23.5 cm, and the anterior fontanelle measured 2×2.5 cm. The newborn presented with a severely altered general condition, generalized cyanosis, intermittent apnea, absent bilateral vesicular breath sounds, bradycardia (36 bpm), prolonged capillary refill time, and an Apgar score of 1 at one minute. These signs indicated the severity of the condition, which was further complicated by cardiorespiratory arrest. In the lumbosacral region of the spine, a bony defect consistent with myelomeningocele was identified (Figure 1A). The defect measured approximately 8 cm by 8 cm, with a depth of 1 cm (Figure 1B). The spinal cord and meninges were exposed and herniated through the bony defect, without being covered by integument or meningeal tissue (Figure 1C, 1D). The upper limbs exhibited active motor movements, while the lower limbs were paralyzed with absent tendon reflexes. A detailed examination revealed no additional malformations.

[b00015] Case presentation
Transfontanelle US revealed a severe myelomeningocele accompanied by Chiari malformation type II (posterior fossa engorgement and displacement of brain structures into the occipital region) and pronounced hydrocephalus, indicating a severe prognosis. No additional malformations were identified.

[b00016] Case presentation
In accordance with neonatal life support protocols, endotracheal intubation and chest compressions were performed. Following resuscitation, mechanical ventilation with synchronized intermittent positive pressure ventilation was initiated. Drug therapy, including epinephrine and a 10 mL/kg bolus of saline, was administered after umbilical venous catheterization. Blood gas analysis revealed severe mixed and metabolic acidosis (pH 6.659, pCO2 181.3 mmHg, pO2 20 mmHg, base excess – 16.9 mmol/L, bicarbonate 19.9 mmol/L), along with elevated lactate levels (12.43 mmol/L). Hyponatremia (Na+ 124.3 mmol/L), hyperkalemia (K+ 4.43 mmol/L), and hypocalcemia (Ca2+ 1.39 mmol/L) were also noted. Despite all efforts, the clinical condition remained critical, with an Apgar score of 1, unresponsive to resuscitation, and death occurring shortly after birth.

[b00017] Case presentation
Macroscopic anatomopathological findings included prematurity, generalized cyanosis, moderate internal hydrocephalus, leukomalacia, thymic atrophy, bilateral pulmonary atelectasis, intracardiac thrombosis, and stasis in the liver, spleen, and kidneys. A detailed examination of fetal anatomy revealed no additional malformations. Histopathological examination conducted using Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE)-stained slides provided further insights: placenta (Figure 2A), thrombosed vessels and signs of hemorrhagic necrosis; umbilical cord (Figure 2B), edema in the arterial muscle layers; brain (Figure 2C), cortical histodifferentiation and interstitial edema; lung (Figure 2D), unexpanded lung tissue, vascular stasis, and thrombosis; liver (Figure 2E), hemorrhagic areas with diffuse lymphocytic infiltration and stasis; kidney (Figure 2F), medullary hematic extravasation, ongoing histodifferentiation, and vascular thrombosis.

[b00018] Case presentation
Fetal karyotyping could not be performed. No abnormalities were identified, and no risk factors suggestive of a chromosomal abnormality were observed.

[b00019] Discussions
In the case presented, a severe myelomeningocele was identified in the lumbosacral region of a female fetus (Figure 1A, 1B). This condition resulted from an unmonitored pregnancy in a 29-year-old woman. She first presented at 28–29 weeks’ gestation, with altered general condition, lower abdominal cramps, and transient lumbar pain. Because she did not attend prenatal consultations, AFP testing was not conducted at 16–18 weeks of gestation, and an US examination was not performed until 28–29 weeks’ gestation. Consequently, the decision was made to proceed with a C section to ensure the best possible outcome for the child.

[b00020] Discussions
A study reported that neural tube defects were slightly more common in females than in males [], while another study found an equal male-to-female ratio []. This difference could be attributed to ethnic, racial, and environmental factors that affect the frequency of neural tube defects [].

[b00021] Discussions
This represented the most severe form of myelomeningocele, as the spinal cord and meninges were exposed and herniated through the bony defect, without being covered by integument or meningeal tissue (Figure 1C, 1D). Most cases of myelomeningocele result from embryological defects during the process of primary neurulation, and in 91–95% of cases, there is a diminution or complete absence of epidermal appendages, which is a hallmark of the condition []. Such severe cases are associated with numerous complications and a poor prognosis [].

[b00022] Discussions
In this case, transfontanelle US revealed hydrocephalus and Chiari malformation type II, which are frequently associated with myelomeningocele [] and can worsen a patient’s prognosis reducing survival rates and contributes to early mortality in cases of myelomeningocele []. High morbidity and mortality rates are often associated with myelomeningocele, particularly in cases of delayed diagnosis and inadequate prenatal care []. Mortality rates are further increased in the presence of additional respiratory, cardiac, or renal complications []. In this case, no pulmonary, hepatic, or renal abnormalities were identified on histopathological examination (Figure 2D, 2E, 2F).

[b00023] Discussions
A detailed examination of the fetus was conducted to identify any associated chromosomal or genetic abnormalities. Karyotyping is recommended when additional abnormalities or risk factors suggest a chromosomal defect []. In this case, fetal karyotyping was not performed, but no abnormalities were observed and no risk factors indicating chromosomal abnormalities were identified.

[b00024] Discussions
Myelomeningocele results from a teratogenic process that disrupts the normal closure and differentiation of the embryonic neural tube. Early prenatal diagnosis and regular antenatal care are crucial for improving outcomes in such cases. In this instance, the 29-year-old pregnant woman had no history of miscarriages and had previously experienced three normal pregnancies. She also had no other associated conditions, such as obesity or diabetes, and did not take any medication during her pregnancy. Her family’s socioeconomic status was lower. She did not take FA supplements either before conception or during the first trimester. In this case, the absence of supplementation with FA prior to conception and throughout the first trimester was identified as a potential contributing factor.

[b00025] Discussions
Siblings of a child with the condition have a 2–5% chance of being affected themselves, a risk approximately 20 to 50 times higher than that of the general population []. Considerable progress has been made in preventing congenital malformations, particularly through the use of maternal FA supplements, which clinical studies have shown to substantially reduce the risk of neural tube defects, including myelomeningocele. In a randomized, double-blind prevention trial conducted at 33 centers across seven countries, researchers investigated whether supplementation with FA, or a multivitamin regimen containing seven other vitamins (A, D, B1, B2, B6, C, and nicotinamide), during conception could reduce the risk of neural tube defects, including anencephaly, spina bifida, and encephalocele [. The trial found that FA supplementation was significantly more effective in preventing neural tube defects compared to other vitamins, which showed no meaningful protective effect. Consequently, it is recommended that women with a history of pregnancies affected by neural tube defects receive FA supplementation. Public health programs should also ensure adequate FA intake in the diets of all women of childbearing potential [].

[b00026] Discussions
These findings prompted the recommendation that all women planning pregnancy should supplement with 0.4 mg of FA daily, while those at high risk should take 4 mg daily []. Population-wide food fortification programs to increase FA intake were initiated in the United States in 1999, followed by other nations. These programs have led to a marked decrease in the incidence of neural tube defects [].

[b00027] Conclusions
In this particular case, with three previous unaffected pregnancies, a severe myelomeningocele developed in the fourth pregnancy. Consistent and adequate prenatal care could have improved outcomes by detecting the condition early, while FA supplementation might have prevented it. In this case, following a pregnancy affected by myelomeningocele, the recommended dosage is 4 mg of FA per day to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. Supplementation should begin three months prior to conception and continue throughout the first trimester.
