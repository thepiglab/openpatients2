Vertebral artery dissection and homonymous hemianopsia after a cervical spine fracture in a polytrauma patient with hypoplastic contralateral vertebral artery: a case report

[b00001] Abstract
Despite the increasing incidence of vertebral artery injury (VAI), it can often be overlooked during the management of polytrauma patients. Due to its specific anatomical location, the VA is particularly susceptible to both traumatic and spontaneous injuries. Traumatic VAI can result from blunt or penetrating trauma and is frequently associated with cervical spine injuries. An 18-year-old male patient was brought to the emergency department after being struck by a motor vehicle. The patient exhibited altered mental status while remaining normotensive and tachypneic. Notably, there were no visible injuries to the head or neck, though multiple contusions were present on the chest and abdomen. Radiographic imaging revealed a fracture of the transverse process of the seventh cervical vertebra, which caused dissection, thrombosis, and occlusion of the V1 segment of the left VA. Additionally, hypoplasia of the contralateral VA was observed. Given the presence of ultrasonographically confirmed free intra-abdominal fluid and the patient’s newly developed hemodynamic instability, he was urgently transferred to the operating theatre. A grade 3 liver laceration was discovered, and hemostasis was successfully achieved using direct sutures. After regaining consciousness, the patient reported right-sided homonymous hemianopsia accompanied by signs of cerebral ischemia. Following stabilization, he was started on anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy. Upon discharge and during follow-up, the visual deficit persisted. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing and managing VAI in polytraumatized patients. Furthermore, the rare combination of contralateral VA hypoplasia and VAI significantly influenced the development of neurological deficits.

[b00002] INTRODUCTION
Following cervical spine trauma, the incidence of vertebral artery injury (VAI) in the general population averages 0.9%. Less than 10% of these injuries result in symptoms associated with central nervous system ischemia []. When symptoms occur, they most commonly manifest as impairments related to posterior cerebral circulation, including disturbances in speech and/or vision. Although various treatment options, such as fibrinolysis and endovascular interventions, may be employed, a multidisciplinary and individualized approach is often necessary, especially when patients sustain multiple concurrent injuries []. In this report, we describe a case involving a polytraumatized patient who suffered a cervical spine fracture leading to dissection and thrombosis of a VA, resulting in homonymous hemianopsia. Notably, the patient also exhibited hypoplasia of the contralateral VA, a condition that could predispose individuals to neurological symptoms.

[b00003] CASE REPORT
An 18-year-old male patient with no significant medical history was transported to the emergency department after being struck by a motor vehicle while walking. Upon initial assessment by paramedics, the patient had a Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) of 3, with no verbal or motor responses. He was immobilized using a long spine board and cervical collar, and his airway was secured with a supraglottic device. Upon hospital arrival, however, the patient opened his eyes in response to painful stimuli, produced incoherent sounds, and exhibited withdrawal responses to pain, indicating a revised GCS of 8. Information regarding alcohol or substance use was not available from the paramedics, but subsequent blood tests revealed a blood alcohol concentration of 0.21%. The patient was tachycardic (pulse rate of 132 beats per minute), tachypneic (respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute), normotensive (blood pressure of 138/81 mmHg), and had dilated pupils with delayed response to light. A rapid trauma assessment and physical examination indicated no external bleeding or deformities; however, multiple hematomas, bruises, and abrasions were noted on the upper abdomen and chest wall. No significant head or neck injuries were visible. An extended focused assessment with sonography for trauma revealed intra-abdominal free fluid and a pelvic hematoma measuring 9×5 cm. Whole-body computed tomography (CT) revealed contusion and laceration of the upper lobe of the right lung, bilateral pneumothorax (36 mm on the left and 5 mm on the right), bilateral fractures of the first ribs, and a fracture of the transverse process of the seventh cervical vertebra (C7). The cervical injury resulted in dissection and occlusion of the V1 segment of the left VA (Figs. 1, 2). No abnormalities were observed in intracranial structures. A chest tube was placed for drainage of the left-sided pneumothorax. Due to signs of hemodynamic instability, notably hypotension (blood pressure of 87/55 mmHg), a declining hemoglobin level (from 120 to 91 g/L), and evidence of active intra-abdominal bleeding, he was urgently taken to the operating theatre for exploratory laparotomy. A grade 3 laceration of the eighth liver segment with active bleeding was discovered, and hemostasis was achieved using direct sutures and a hemostatic sponge. Postoperatively, a Philadelphia cervical collar was applied due to the fracture of the C7 transverse process. After regaining consciousness, the patient reported a visual deficit in the right visual field. A follow-up CT scan of the head revealed signs of acute ischemia affecting the left occipital lobe and right cerebellar hemisphere. Because the contralateral VA was hypoplastic, the dissection and thrombosis led to right-sided homonymous hemianopsia. Once the risk of bleeding was considered acceptably low by a pediatric hematologist and neurologist, therapeutic enoxaparin was initiated. The patient recovered well without significant complications, and the chest tube was removed a few days later. The subsequent course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged with 300 mg of acetylsalicylic acid prescribed as chronic therapy, which was later reduced to 100 mg at the last follow-up. At the final follow-up examination 6 months after discharge, the homonymous hemianopsia persisted. Time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography showed resolution and healing of the vascular injury, with only slight irregularity remaining in the arterial wall (Figs. 3, 4), as well as stationary encephalomalacia in the ischemia-affected regions of the brain (Fig. 5).

[b00005] DISCUSSION
Traumatic VAI encompasses conditions ranging from arterial occlusion, dissection, and intimal tear to stenosis and pseudoaneurysm formation. These injuries typically result from high-energy trauma, either blunt or penetrating. Such trauma can potentially lead to hyperextension of the neck and fracture of the cervical spine, causing arterial stretching and subsequent vascular injury []. Although clinical presentations of VAI almost universally include neck pain and headache, the reported incidence of VAI varies widely, mainly due to differences in sample sizes and the evolution of imaging modalities. Most patients with symptomatic injuries exhibit neurological deficits within the first 24 hours, and these cases carry a mortality rate ranging from 4% to 8% [].

[b00006] DISCUSSION
In contrast to isolated VAI, where patients are often conscious and able to communicate their symptoms, polytraumatized patients frequently present differently, often unconscious and experiencing severe associated injuries requiring immediate medical intervention. According to the Denver criteria [], which are used to identify patients at high risk for blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI), our patient had multiple associated injuries placing him at significant risk for such an injury. Similar to the findings of Weber et al. [], our patient also exhibited a higher Injury Severity Score, and worse neurological status compared to patients without BCVI, confirming a significantly elevated stroke rate in individuals suffering from BCVI. On the other hand, our patient experienced significant abdominal trauma, a finding not typically emphasized in prior research involving patients with BCVI [].

[b00007] DISCUSSION
Although fewer than 2% of patients with cervical spine fractures suffer concomitant VAI, nearly half of these patients have fractures of the transverse process extending into the foramen transversarium [], as seen in our case. This relationship is explained by the close anatomical association of the VA passing through multiple cervical foramina. Unlike our patient, who sustained a fracture of the lower cervical spine, fractures involving the middle and upper cervical vertebrae more commonly cause VAI [].

[b00008] DISCUSSION
The VA is anatomically divided into four segments (V1–V4) [], with the V3 segment most frequently involved in injuries [], a discrepancy with our patient who suffered an injury to the V1/V2 segment of the VA. Similar to our patient, most individuals have one dominant VA, typically on the left side, with fewer than 10% exhibiting contralateral hypoplasia []. VA hypoplasia (VAH) not only constitutes a significant risk factor for ipsilateral VA dissection [], but also represents an important determinant of stroke occurrence in patients younger than 65 years [].

[b00009] DISCUSSION
Similar to our case, most patients with VAI are managed conservatively using anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, whereas a smaller proportion undergoes surgical or endovascular interventions []. Our patient belongs to the subgroup described by Temperley et al. [], who initially did not receive anticoagulation due to contraindications related to acute hemorrhage []. Both surgical and endovascular treatments would have presented high risks in our patient due to the presence of contralateral VAH, potentially compromising adequate collateral blood flow. Among widely varied outcomes reported in the literature, our patient falls within the 12% of patients who exhibited persistent neurological deficits at follow-up [].

[b00010] DISCUSSION
Although cervical spine trauma is frequently reported, the triad of polytrauma, VAI, and contralateral VAH occurring simultaneously is exceedingly rare. We report a polytraumatized young male who sustained a VAI leading to neurological deficits, complicated by contralateral VAH. Firstly, this case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary and individualized approach when managing such complex patients. Additionally, the impact of contralateral VAH in this scenario should not be overlooked, as VAH alone significantly increases stroke risk in young adults.
