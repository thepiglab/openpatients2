Drug-Induced Sclerosing Cholangitis Leading to Discontinuation of Treatment in a Patient with Metastatic Breast Cancer: A Case Report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Drug-induced sclerosing cholangitis (SC) is uncommon and has been only rarely reported with paclitaxel, bevacizumab, or trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd). We report a suspected SC following these therapies for metastatic breast cancer, underscoring a possible association with taxane-based chemotherapy and antibody or antibody-drug conjugates.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
A 58-year-old woman with metastatic hormone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-low breast cancer developed cholangitis 17 days after receiving the first cycle of T-DXd, following prior treatment with paclitaxel and bevacizumab. The symptoms improved with antibiotic therapy. However, fever and jaundice recurred on day 49. Imaging studies revealed intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile duct dilation and stricture. SC was suspected based on imaging and clinical course. Histopathological examination showed fibrosis without evidence of malignancy or infiltration by IgG4-positive cells, raising suspicion of drug-induced SC. Due to repeated episodes of cholangitis, the patient’s performance status deteriorated, making further chemotherapy unfeasible. The patient opted for best supportive care and died on day 99.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion
This case highlights the possibility of SC as a delayed adverse event of anticancer chemotherapy. Clinicians should consider drug-induced SC when unexplained biliary strictures arise during or after chemotherapy, as early recognition may impact prognosis and treatment planning.

[b00004] Introduction
The aim of this report was to present a suspected case of drug-induced sclerosing cholangitis (SC) associated with anticancer agents that have been infrequently reported, thereby providing clinical guidance to appropriately consider a drug-related etiology when similar cases are encountered. SC is a chronic, progressive cholestatic disease characterized by inflammation, fibrosis, and bile duct stricture []. It is broadly classified into primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and secondary sclerosing cholangitis (SSC), depending on its etiology. SSC may arise from various conditions, including malignant diseases, autoimmune disorders, infections, common bile duct stones, traumatic or ischemic bile duct injury, ampullary stenosis, chronic pancreatitis, congenital biliary anomalies, and drug-induced injury []. Several drugs have been implicated in causing drug-induced cholangitis. Severe cases can interfere with chemotherapy continuation, making it a clinically significant adverse event. Herein, we present a case of suspected drug-induced cholangitis and provide a brief literature review.

[b00005] Case Presentation
In 2014, a 48-year-old woman was diagnosed with right breast cancer with thoracic vertebral metastasis (clinical T4bN1M1) []. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed an invasive ductal carcinoma that was estrogen receptor-positive (Allred score 5 + 3 = 8), progesterone receptor-positive (Allred score 5 + 3 = 8), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) 2+; however, fluorescence in situ hybridization results were negative. Initial treatment involved luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonist and tamoxifen. As bone metastases progressed, treatment was switched to fulvestrant, palbociclib, and luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonist in 2020. In 2023, further disease progression with pelvic bone metastases led to the initiation of paclitaxel (80 mg/m^(2) on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28-day cycle) combined with bevacizumab (10 mg/kg on days 1 and 15). After four cycles, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy worsened, prompting a switch to trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) in 2024. Previous chemotherapy regimens had not been associated with elevated biliary enzyme levels. There was no notable family history, and the patient’s performance status was 1.

[b00006] Case Presentation
Seventeen days after the first cycle of T-DXd (day 0 was defined as the first administration of T-DXd), the patient reported fatigue. The abdomen was flat and soft, with no tenderness, including in the right hypochondrium. Blood tests revealed elevated γ-glutamyl transferase (γGTP) at 373 U/L (reference range: 13–64 U/L) and C-reactive protein (CRP) at 19 mg/dL (reference range: 0.00–0.14 mg/dL), though liver enzyme levels remained normal. Blood cultures were positive for Escherichia coli. Computed tomography (CT) revealed intrahepatic bile duct dilation (shown in Fig. 1a); however, no definite obstruction was identified. Biliary sludge accumulation associated with anorexia was considered a potential cause of biliary obstruction. Inflammatory markers improved following antibacterial treatment; however, γGTP levels continued to rise. Since the gallbladder was not distended, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography was considered too invasive and was not performed. Instead, conservative management was pursued, leading to symptomatic improvement, and the patient was discharged on day 32.

[b00007] Case Presentation
During a hospital visit on day 49, the patient reported jaundice. The abdomen was flat and soft without tenderness, and the liver edge was palpable one fingerbreadth below the right costal margin. Blood tests showed elevated biliary enzymes, including total bilirubin at 5.0 mg/dL (reference range: 0.4–1.5 mg/dL), γGTP at 839 U/L, and CRP at 15 mg/dL. The patient tested negative for human immunodeficiency virus antibodies/antigen test. Additional parameters, including IgG4 (8 mg/dL; reference range: 11–121 mg/dL), IgG (1,139 mg/dL; reference range: 861.0–1,747.0 mg/dL), IgM (69 mg/dL; reference range: 50.0–269.0 mg/dL), antinuclear antibody (<40; reference range: 0–39), were within normal limits, while antimitochondrial M2 antibody levels were slightly elevated (8.4 U/mL, reference range: <7.0 U/dL). Blood cultures again tested positive for E. coli. CT imaging revealed stenosis and dilation of the bile ducts in both lobes (shown in Fig. 1b). Antibiotic therapy was initiated, followed by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, which showed that the bile duct was intact until confluence; however, the intrahepatic bile ducts could not be visualized. An endoscopic retrograde biliary drainage tube was inserted. Bile culture yielded a low burden of E. coli. Despite the decreasing CRP levels, γGTP and total bilirubin levels remained unchanged. Bile duct biopsy revealed fibrosis, moderate lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, and superficial neutrophils (shown in Fig. 2a). Obliterative phlebitis was not seen. No neoplastic lesion was identified. Immunohistochemically, IgG4+ cells were scarce and there was no CD8+ cell predominance (shown in Fig. 2b). Given the exclusion of other differential diagnoses, drug-induced cholangitis was diagnosed. Considering the patient’s poor general condition, further chemotherapy was considered unfeasible. The patient opted for best supportive care, was discharged on day 71, and died on day 99 (Fig. 3).

[b00008] Discussion
SC is a chronic cholestatic disease characterized by bile duct strictures, beading, and fibrosis. It is classified as PSC when no underlying cause is identified and as SSC when associated with a specific condition []. SSC can result from various underlying conditions, including malignancies (e.g., cholangiocarcinoma, ampullary carcinoma, and pancreatic cancer), autoimmune disorders (e.g., sarcoidosis, IgG4-related cholangitis), infections (e.g., acquired immunodeficiency syndrome-related cholangitis, parasitic infections), bile duct stones, traumatic or ischemic bile duct injury, ampullary stenosis, chronic pancreatitis, congenital biliary anomalies, and drug-induced injury [, ]. PSC was considered unlikely in this case due to the absence of inflammatory bowel disease and the epidemiological background of the patient. Furthermore, the absence of hepatic metastases and lack of malignant features on bile duct biopsy made metastatic disease less probable. Autoimmune causes, including IgG4-related SC, were ruled out based on clinical history and laboratory findings. The patient also tested negative for human immunodeficiency virus.

[b00009] Discussion
Characteristic CT findings of drug-induced cholangitis include extrahepatic and multifocal intrahepatic bile duct strictures []. Given the absence of other likely causes and the characteristic CT findings, chemotherapy-induced SC was suspected. Although recurrent pyogenic cholangitis can cause SSC, it is typically associated with bile stasis from intraductal stones, which predispose patients to bacterial infections []. No bile duct stones were identified in this patient, and recurrent cholangitis without an evident cause was considered unlikely. We hypothesize that drug-induced SC led to intrahepatic bile duct strictures, resulting in bile stasis and secondary bacterial cholangitis. This could explain the isolation of E. coli from blood and bile cultures. However, diagnostic evaluation was limited due to the patient’s phobia of magnetic resonance imaging.

[b00010] Discussion
Various drugs have been associated with drug-induced SC, including ketamine, celecoxib, sevoflurane, amoxicillin-clavulanate, amiodarone, infliximab, venlafaxine, atorvastatin, green tea extract, and anticancer agents []. Although intravenously administered antineoplastic agents have been sporadically reported as causes, immune-related adverse events have become more common with the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors. In this case, paclitaxel combined with bevacizumab and T-DXd are the most recently introduced therapeutic agents. To investigate potential associations, a systematic literature search was conducted using the PubMed database with the following search terms: “([English] AND [“paclitaxel” OR “nab-paclitaxel” OR “protein-bound paclitaxel” OR “docetaxel” OR “cabazitaxel” OR “taxane” OR “trastuzumab deruxtecan” OR “T-Dxd” OR “ado-trastuzumab emtansine” OR “T-DM1” OR “antibody drug conjugate” OR “ADC”] AND [“sclerosing cholangitis” OR “secondary sclerosing cholangitis” OR “drug-induced sclerosing cholangitis” OR “drug-induced cholangitis” OR “cholangitis”])”. Only a few cases of SC associated with taxane-based chemotherapy, including paclitaxel, were identified [–]. In addition to the two previously reported cases involving combination therapy with paclitaxel, only one additional case has been reported using the search terms (“bevacizumab” OR “Avastin” OR “anti-VEGF antibody” OR “VEGF inhibitor” OR “VEGF-blocking agent”) instead [–]. No cases associated with T-DXd or other antibody-drug conjugates were reported. In the DESTINY-BREAST trial, no bile duct-related adverse events were reported in patients with breast cancer [–].

[b00011] Discussion
Drug-induced cholangitis may present with a delayed onset, occurring days to weeks after initiating or discontinuing treatment. Importantly, the development, duration, and severity of cholestasis do not always correlate with the timing of drug cessation []. Proposed mechanisms include immune-mediated injury, direct toxicity from high local drug concentrations, and ischemic changes due to drug effects on the peribiliary vascular plexus [, , ]. A key limitation of this case is the inability to definitively identify a single causative agent among paclitaxel, bevacizumab, and T-DXd. In this case, SC developed 2 months after completing four cycles of paclitaxel combined with bevacizumab and 2 weeks after the first dose of T-DXd. The temporal window for drug-induced cholangitis is compatible with each exposure, so definitive attribution to one agent was not possible.

[b00012] Discussion
Management of drug-induced SC typically involves discontinuation of the offending drug. Additional treatments may include immunosuppressive therapy, ursodeoxycholic acid, and endoscopic retrograde biliary drainage [, ]. However, in this case, immunosuppressive therapy was not administered due to concurrent E. coli infection. Based on the patient’s underlying disease prognosis, best supportive care was selected. Ursodeoxycholic acid was not administered due to its limited immediate efficacy and the burden of additional oral medications.

[b00013] Discussion
In conclusion, we report a case of suspected drug-induced SC following anticancer chemotherapy. Although a definitive diagnosis could not be established due to limitations in diagnostic testing, this case offers valuable insights into the possibility of drug-induced cholangitis attributable to paclitaxel, bevacizumab, or T-DXd. The performance status of the patient declined due to SC, and repeated infection ultimately led to discontinuation of chemotherapy. Drug-induced SC should be considered when characteristic imaging findings are present. [b00014] Patient Perspective
After receiving treatment for cholangitis, I felt much better once the fever subsided. Since I hope to spend my final days at home, I am grateful that I was able to be discharged and continue antibiotic treatment there. My husband has prepared a bed in the middle of our living room and is waiting for me to come home, so returning home means a great deal to me. I truly appreciate the care I received at this hospital.

Fig. 3.
Trends in laboratory findings. At the time of the first administration of T-DXd, inflammatory markers and hepatobiliary enzymes were within normal ranges. On day 17, elevated CRP levels prompted antibiotic therapy initiation. Although CRP levels decreased, γGTP levels continued to rise.
