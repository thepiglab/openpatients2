Left Ventricular Assist Device Support in Duchenne Dilated Cardiomyopathy: Case Series of the First Three Patients in South Korea

[b00001] INTRODUCTION
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked recessive neuromuscular disorder resulting from a mutation in the dystrophin gene. Cardiac involvement in DMD usually begins at 6 years of age. It most often develops in the form of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), leading to advanced heart failure (AdvHF), and associated supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias around the age of 20, resulting in death. There are no specific guidelines for AdvHF in patients with DMD, owing to their limited life expectancy. Herein, we report 3 cases of end-stage DMD-DCM treated with a left ventricular assist device (LVAD). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the long-term LVAD outcomes of DMD-DCM in the Asia-Pacific region.

[b00002] CASE / Case 1
A 19-year-old male with genetically confirmed DMD was transferred to the Adult Cardiology Department. The patient was non-ambulatory and intermittently dependent on noninvasive ventilation (NIV) at night. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 42% without heart failure (HF) symptoms. Three months later, he visited the emergency department (ED) with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and concomitant atrial fibrillation. Thrombectomy was successfully performed, and the ST elevation subsided with sinus conversion. Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF was continued for a year. On November 5, 2020, the patient visited the ED with severe dyspnea. The patient was discharged after a short-term dobutamine treatment. However, he became dependent on dobutamine after 2 more HF hospitalizations. Heart transplantation or LVAD were considered; however, there was no preceding case that is similar reported in the Asia-Pacific region. A multi-disciplinary team (MDT) discussed technical issues associated with severe scoliosis (Figure 1A) and potential ethical problems. Due to the possible increased risk of infection from immunosuppression, the patient underwent LVAD implantation (HeartMate 3, HM3), and surgery followed by postoperative extubation was successful (Figure 1B). After LVAD implantation, the patient's NIV-dependent time was reduced to less than one-tenth of the previous duration. The patient was transferred to the general ward on postoperative day (POD) 7 and was discharged on hospital day 108. De novo aortic regurgitation with low cardiac output symptoms developed approximately 2 years after implantation; however, adjusting the RPM from 4,800 to 5,400 relieved the symptoms (Figure 1C and D).

[b00003] CASE / Case 2
A 29-year-old male was transferred to our hospital due to severe dyspnea. He was wheelchair-bound and required continuous nasal NIV support for 24 hours. Despite optimal GDMT, he was hospitalized three times within 6 months in 2023 for worsening HF. The N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level was 2,902 pg/mL, and chest X-ray revealed right-sided pleural effusion and increased vascular markings.

[b00004] CASE / Case 2
On hospital day 15, the scheduled date for LVAD implantation, the patient experienced cardiac arrest and underwent 20 minutes of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (E-CPR). Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was initiated following endotracheal intubation. A right-sided pneumothorax was identified post-ECMO insertion (Figure 2A), and a chest tube was placed accordingly. The patient regained an alert mental status, and after an extensive MDT discussion, LVAD implantation was performed the following day (Figure 2B).

[b00005] CASE / Case 2
Postoperatively, the patient exhibited a fluctuating right ventricular (RV) dysfunction. He was hospitalized with a tracheostomy and home-ventilator for four months. Amelioration of lung function due to left upper pneumothorax (Figure 2C) made complete weaning from the inotropes difficult. Owing to recurrent closed thoracostomy and tubing-site bleeding, the average international normalized ratio remained between 1.1 and 1.2 for approximately one month from POD 90. On POD 122, the patient experienced a sudden decrease in mental status. A non-contrast brain computed tomography scan revealed a right intracerebral hemorrhage (Figure 2D). After discussion with the patient’s family, they opted not to proceed with further surgical intervention, and the patient died the following day.

[b00006] CASE / Case 3
A 23-year-old male with DMD was referred from the Pediatric Department to the Adult Cardiology Clinic. He was wheelchair-bound and did not use NIV. At the time of referral, the NT-proBNP level was 2,414 pg/mL, and his LVEF was reduced to 28%. GDMT was uptitrated, and the patient was discharged with a plan to evaluate implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation depending on his response to medical therapy. Three months later, he was admitted to the intensive care unit via the ED on August 31, 2023, due to severe dyspnea. Chest X-ray revealed right pleural effusion and pulmonary edema. Dobutamine was initiated at 3 mcg/kg/min. The following day, the patient developed signs of severe hypoperfusion and shock (systolic blood pressure, <70 mmHg). A prompt decision to initiate ECMO was made; however, cannulation was difficult because of the small, shrunken femoral arteries. E-CPR was performed 30 minutes before cannulation for recurrent cardiac arrest. Following intubation and ECMO initiation (Figure 3A), the patient was alert and his vital signs stabilized. An application for LVAD implantation was submitted to the National Health Insurance Authority and approved after eight days. On the day after approval, the patient underwent successful LVAD implantation (Figure 3B). The patient was extubated 2 days postoperatively and experienced major bleeding from the duodenal ulcer on POD 21 (Figure 3C). During the healing process of ulcers, warfarin was skipped. The patient was discharged on POD 174 without any symptoms or complications (Figure 3D).

[b00038] DISCUSSION
A characteristic feature of cardiomyopathy in DMD is the near absence of symptoms until the patient reaches complete decompensation. In both Cases 1 and 3, the patients exhibited minimal or no symptoms prior to full decompensation, which contributed to a lack of urgency or concern among caregivers. This is largely due to the patients’ non-ambulant status and the limited motor function of the upper limbs. The only situations in which these patients expend noticeable energy are during meals or conversations. Therefore, clinical deterioration should be suspected if there is a noticeable prolongation of mealtimes or the emergence of fragmented or broken syllables during speech.

[b00039] DISCUSSION
LVAD implantation in patients with DMD cardiomyopathy is extremely rare worldwide. This rarity stems from the fact that most patients with DMD do not survive beyond the second decade of life due to progressive cardiac or respiratory failure. However, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that while patients born before the 1970s had a median life expectancy around 20 years, those born after the 1990s now live to approximately 28.1 years (95% confidence interval, 25.1–30.3). This improvement in survival has been largely attributed to the widespread use of systemic corticosteroids, which reduce inflammation and respiratory muscle decline, and the implementation of NIV support systems. More recently, advances in genetic testing and molecular therapies, including adeno-associated virus vectors and groundbreaking genome-editing approaches using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) technology, have led to multiple clinical trials on systemic microdystrophin gene therapy. Although it remains uncertain whether these novel therapies will significantly extend the life expectancy of patients with DMD, their potential impact is under active investigation. Importantly, although therapies such as exon skipping with antisense oligonucleotides have shown promise in improving skeletal muscle strength, their effects on cardiac muscles remain limited. Consequently, discordance in disease severity may emerge between improved peripheral motor function and persistent or worsening cardiomyopathies. It is clear that cardiologists, especially HF specialists, will increasingly encounter a new phenotype of DMD: patients who live longer and present with AdvHF requiring complex decision-making.

[b00040] DISCUSSION
As observed in other forms of genetic DCM, patients with DMD-related DCM may develop late RV failure after LVAD implantation. Therefore, accurate prediction and thorough preoperative assessment of the right-sided heart function are critical in this population. Interestingly, however, an autopsy case of a 29-year-old patient who died from sudden cardiac arrest revealed that fibrofatty replacement was confined to the LV wall and interventricular septum, while the RV wall, both atria, and conduction system (including the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes) remained structurally preserved. This suggests that different pathological mechanisms may be involved in LV and RV myocardial involvement in DMD, with the RV potentially less affected. These findings may justify LVAD therapy in selected patients with DMD. In the second case, the patient experienced a highly vulnerable clinical course and ultimately died, highlighting the importance of identifying the optimal timing for LVAD implantation. It appears that the most favorable window may be before the patient becomes fully dependent on ventilator support.

[b00041] DISCUSSION
When LVAD implantation was initiated in patients with DMD, the greatest challenges were the lack of precedents and ethical considerations, similar to those encountered in other muscular diseases. However, as our MDT gained experience, we became increasingly confident that offering proactive treatment to appropriately selected patients could provide a meaningful opportunity for life prolongation, not only for the patients but also for their families.

[b00042] DISCUSSION
In conclusion, our experience suggests that LVAD implantation is a feasible option for meticulously selected patients with both DMD and AdvHF. Because survival may improve in this population, early recognition and timely intervention may enhance outcomes. Multi-disciplinary evaluation is essential to guide the optimal management of these complex cases.
