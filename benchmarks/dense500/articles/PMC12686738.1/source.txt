Lemborexant overdose presenting with mild sedation despite high plasma levels: A case report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Lemborexant is a dual orexin receptor antagonist (DORA) with a favorable safety profile at therapeutic doses. However, clinical data regarding its toxic effects in overdose scenarios are limited. This is the first published case report of serially measured plasma concentrations of lemborexant in a patient who survived following acute overdose.

[b00002] Abstract / Case report
A 33-year-old woman presented to the emergency department four hours after ingesting an estimated 720 mg of lemborexant and 78 mg of eszopiclone. She exhibited mild central nervous system depression (Glasgow Coma Scale score 11) but was hemodynamically stable. With supportive care alone, the patient’s consciousness spontaneously improved. Retrospective analysis revealed that her plasma lemborexant concentration increased from 0.144 µg/mL at four hours to a peak of 1.410 µg/mL at nine hours post-ingestion, despite undergoing clinical recovery.

[b00003] Abstract / Discussions
This case suggests that massive lemborexant overdoses may not cause life-threatening toxicity even when plasma levels approach those reported in fatal cases. This case provides preliminary but clinically important data and highlights the need for further research to better characterize the clinical implications and management of lemborexant overdoses.

[b00004] Highlights
• First case report of serial plasma levels in a surviving lemborexant overdose patient. • Patient showed only mild sedation despite ingesting 720 mg of lemborexant. • Plasma concentration peaked at 1.410 µg/mL, a level comparable to fatal cases. • A dissociation was seen between rising plasma levels and clinical improvement. • Supportive care alone was sufficient for a favorable outcome in this case.

[b00005] Introduction
Lemborexant (Dayvigo®) is a novel dual orexin receptor antagonist (DORA) approved for the treatment of adult insomnia, which is characterized by sleep onset and/or maintenance difficulty , . Unlike traditional sedative-hypnotics that enhance GABAergic neurotransmission, DORAs competitively inhibit the wake-promoting orexin signaling pathway by binding to orexin-1 (OX1R) and orexin-2 (OX2R) receptors , , . This distinct mechanism likely offers a favorable safety profile with minimal residual daytime effects .

[b00006] Introduction
Although DORAs are generally well-tolerated at therapeutic doses, information on their toxicity in overdose settings is limited. The primary toxic effect of DORA overdose is central nervous system (CNS) depression, which presents as somnolence, dizziness, and headaches . Unlike the classic sedative-hypnotic toxicity, DORAs alone do not typically cause significant respiratory depression or cardiovascular instability. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published case of serially measured plasma concentrations of lemborexant in a patient surviving an episode of acute overdose. This study had three objectives. First, to describe the clinical course of the patient following a massive overdose of lemborexant. Second, to report serial plasma concentrations of the drug in a patient surviving the overdose for the first time. Third, to discuss the observed dissociation between the patient's toxicokinetic profile and clinical recovery.

[b00007] Case presentation
A 33-year-old woman was brought to the emergency department (ED) with impaired consciousness, having no specific chief complaint. Approximately eight hours prior to presentation, she had reportedly consumed an unknown quantity of alcohol. It is estimated that she ingested the medications approximately four hours before her arrival. One hour before arrival, she contacted her mother, implying an intentional drug overdose. Her mother subsequently called emergency medical services (EMS). Upon EMS arrival, numerous empty blister packs were found at the scene, including ones for 146 tablets of lemborexant (5 mg) and 26 tablets of eszopiclone (3 mg), suggesting an overdose of these medications. On arrival to the ED, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate of 15 breaths/min, heart rate of 86 beats/min, blood pressure of 99/59 mmHg, body temperature of 36.2 ℃, and oxygen saturation of 97 % on room air. She exhibited impaired consciousness, with a Japan Coma Scale (JCS) score of 10 points and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 11 points (E3V3M5). Her pupils were equal and reactive (3 mm / 3 mm), and no signs of tremor, diaphoresis, or toxidrome-specific physical findings were observed. A rapid urine toxicology screen (SIGNIFY-ER®) tested positive for benzodiazepines. Other laboratory findings, including complete blood count, metabolic panel, and liver function tests, were unremarkable. The osmolar gap was calculated to be 20.1 mOsm/L, and the estimated initial blood alcohol concentration was 92.7 mg/dL. The patient was managed with supportive care alone. Her level of consciousness gradually improved without specific intervention, and she was able to ambulate by nine hours of post-ingestion. She was discharged on hospital day two, in good condition. Initially, it was presumed that her recovery paralleled a decline in drug plasma concentrations. However, retrospective measurement of lemborexant concentrations in stored blood samples revealed an unexpected trend: the plasma level increased over time. Specifically, the concentration was 0.144 µg/mL at four hours post-ingestion (the time of ED arrival) and had risen to 1.410 µg/mL at nine hours post-ingestion. These findings indicated a delayed peak in plasma concentration, prompting further investigation into lemborexant’s pharmacokinetics. Venous blood samples were collected at two critical time points: 4 h post-ingestion (upon ED arrival) and 9 h post-ingestion. Samples were immediately centrifuged, and the resulting plasma was stored at below −20°C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of lemborexant were measured using fully validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method, as previously described by Mano et al . The method was initially developed to quantitate only the parent lemborexant by protein precipitation using methanol from 100 µL of human sodium heparin plasma before LC-MS/MS analysis , . The lower limit of quantitation was 0.000100 μg/mL in plasma. This assay has demonstrated high accuracy and precision, thereby ensuring the reliability of the reported values , .

[b00008] Discussion
This case report describes the serially measured plasma concentrations of lemborexant in a patient who survived an episode of acute drug overdose. The patient ingested 720 mg lemborexant along with 78 mg eszopiclone and had moderate central nervous system (CNS) depression (GCS 11 on arrival), with spontaneous improvement and full recovery on supportive care. This case highlights observations regarding toxicokinetics, although the implications for clinical severity remain uncertain from a single case. Notably, the patient’s plasma lemborexant concentration increased over time, from 0.144 µg /mL at four hours post-ingestion (time of ED arrival) to 1.410 µg/mL at nine hours, despite a continuous improvement in her level of consciousness. Although only two levels were obtained, this trend suggests a possible delayed rise in the plasma concentration. The coingestion of eszopiclone and alcohol is likely to have pharmacodynamic consequences. Pharmacodynamically, both eszopiclone (a GABAA receptor agonist) and an estimated initial blood alcohol concentration of 92.7 mg/dL are potent CNS depressants. Their combined sedative effects undoubtedly contributed to the patient's initial presentation (GCS 11), potentially masking the true isolated effects of the lemborexant. While the patient's initial CNS depression (GCS score 11) was undoubtedly exacerbated by the co-ingestant, the rapid and spontaneous recovery was particularly noteworthy. This improvement occurred as the effects of the more rapidly clearing substances alcohol and eszopiclone (half-life of approximately 6 h), subsided . Therefore, this clinical course suggests that lemborexant alone, despite its increase in plasma concentration, may exert limited sedative effects, even at high doses. Lemborexant has been approved for the treatment of insomnia and is generally regarded as having a favorable safety profile. In clinical trials, tolerability of doses as high as 200 mg has been demonstrated, with no serious outcomes reported; the most common adverse side effect reported is somnolence , . Despite its safe use minimal clinical data regarding toxic doses, overdose management, and outcomes in humans remain limited . No specific antidote to overdose exists and supportive care is the only form of treatment currently available , . Pharmacologic studies have found plasma concentrations as high as 0.431 µg/mL after a dose of 200 mg, but the post-overdose toxicity and lethality mechanisms remain poorly understood . Respiratory arrest due to excess sedation is speculated to play a role, especially given the potent OX2R antagonism of lemborexant, which is crucial for maintaining a wakeful state . Unlike GABAergic agents, DORAs modulate wakefulness by selectively antagonizing orexin receptors without broadly depressing the CNS activity. Moreover, the pharmacokinetic characteristics of lemborexant, particularly its large distribution volume (1970 L) and high plasma protein binding rate (94 %), suggest extensive tissue distribution and reduced free drug availability within the plasma . Even at a high total concentration, the free fraction may not reach a level sufficient to cause severe toxicity. Moreover, CNS receptor saturation may have occurred, whereby further increases in plasma concentrations did not lead to a proportionally greater sedative effect. Finally, individual variability in receptor sensitivity may play a role. In addition, the large number of tablets ingested may have prolonged gastric emptying or slowed absorption, possibly contributing to a delayed increase in plasma concentration . In combination, these factors may explain the dissociation between a delayed plasma peak and early clinical recovery. Notably, the peak plasma concentration in this case (1.410 µg/mL) came near to that of a fatal case that has previously been reported (1.651 µg/mL) . However, significant differences are observed between the two cases. The patient in this study received prompt medical attention and supportive care in a controlled hospital environment. In contrast, the fatal case involved a patient discovered outdoors who also suffered traumatic blood loss, introducing critical confounding factors such as hypothermia and hemorrhagic shock . Furthermore, the toxicological analysis was based on postmortem blood, and the patient had suffered traumatic blood loss, which may have altered the drug distribution and clearance . These findings do not confirm low toxicity but suggest that other physiological or situational factors may play a crucial role in determining the outcome. This study has several important limitations. First, as a single case study, our findings cannot be generalized to all patients receiving lemborexant overdoses. The patient response may have been influenced by unique metabolic or pharmacodynamic factors. Second, the presence of co-ingestants (eszopiclone and alcohol) made it difficult to isolate the toxic effects solely attributable to lemborexant. Third, plasma concentrations were measured at only two time points, which provided a snapshot rather than a complete pharmacokinetic curve. A more detailed time series analysis is required to fully characterize the absorption and elimination phases.

[b00009] Conclusions
This case, the first report of serial plasma concentrations in a patient who survived lemborexant overdose, demonstrates a significant dissociation between toxicokinetics and clinical effects. Despite ingesting a massive dose and achieving plasma levels comparable to those in a previously reported fatal case, the patient experienced only mild CNS depression and recovered fully with supportive care alone. This suggests that delayed absorption may occur in large ingestions, and that high plasma levels of lemborexant are not necessarily indicative of severe toxicity. Further research is essential to establish a clear risk profile and optimal management strategy for lemborexant overdose.
