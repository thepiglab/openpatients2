Urinary Tract Infection Masquerading as a Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder Exacerbation: A Case Report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a relapsing autoimmune demyelinating disease, characterized by severe attacks of optic neuritis and transverse myelitis. Distinguishing true relapses from infection-related pseudo-exacerbations is essential, particularly in immunosuppressed patients where clinical presentations may be atypical.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
An 83-year-old woman with AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD and Parkinson’s disease presented with acute visual loss and altered mental status, initially suspected to represent a relapse. Empirical intravenous corticosteroids were administered, but her condition worsened with new hallucinations. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed no new demyelinating lesions. Urinalysis and culture confirmed a urinary tract infection due to Klebsiella oxytoca. Corticosteroids were discontinued, and antibiotic therapy initiated, leading to resolution of mental status changes and a return to baseline vision. Follow-up neuro-ophthalmic examination confirmed stability without new inflammatory activity.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion
Urinary tract infection can masquerade as an NMOSD exacerbation, complicating diagnosis in elderly immunosuppressed patients.

[b00004] Introduction
Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a relapsing, inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system that is distinct from multiple sclerosis []. NMOSD presents with severe attacks of optic neuritis and transverse myelitis. The pathogenesis is autoimmune, and serum aquaporin-4 immunoglobulin G (AQP4-IgG) antibodies are highly specific for the disease [].

[b00005] Introduction
The diagnosis of NMOSD in a patient with a positive AQP4-IgG test requires at least one core clinical characteristic, including: optic neuritis, acute myelitis, area postrema syndrome, acute brainstem syndrome, symptomatic narcolepsy or acute diencephalic syndrome with NMOSD-typical diencephalic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesions, or symptomatic cerebral syndrome with NMOSD-typical brain lesions [].

[b00006] Introduction
MRI typically reveals multifocal T2-hyperintense lesions in AQP4-rich regions. Optic neuritis in NMOSD often affects the posterior optic nerve in a longitudinally extensive pattern (particularly with extension into the optic chiasm), and spinal cord lesions also tend to be longitudinally extensive, involving three or more vertebral segments.

[b00007] Introduction
Acute attacks are managed with high-dose intravenous corticosteroids, followed by plasmapheresis or intravenous immunoglobulin when needed. Steroid response is often poor. Currently, approved agents include eculizumab [], inebilizumab [], and satralizumab, with off-label options such as azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and tocilizumab. Despite treatment, NMOSD is associated with a high risk of incomplete recovery, particularly after optic neuritis, with visual outcomes significantly worse than in MS.

[b00008] Case Report
An 83-year-old white female presented to the emergency department with acute unilateral painful loss of vision in the right eye (OD), described as a “lavender haze.” She had a history of seropositive NMOSD, diagnosed in 2014, and was maintained on mycophenolate mofetil (CellCept) 1,000 mg daily. At her initial presentation in 2014, she had reported similar symptoms, describing a lavender-colored, feather-shaped figure that moved within her visual field. Cranial MRI with contrast confirmed left optic nerve enhancement consistent with optic neuritis. There was a longitudinally extensive cervical spinal cord lesion involving more than three vertebral segments. Serum AQP4-IgG were detected at a titer of 1:10 by cell-based indirect immunofluorescence assay.

[b00009] Case Report
She denied any previous visual disturbance in the OD. Her past medical history included Parkinson disease and hypertension. Her current medications include carbidopa-levodopa, gabapentin, and losartan.

[b00010] Case Report
At admission, the patient had altered mental status. She denied hiccups, nausea, vomiting, hypersomnolence, headaches, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, or weight loss. Visual acuity at the time of presentation to the emergency department was 20/40 OD and counting fingers at 3 feet in the left eye (OS). A relative afferent pupillary defect was present OS. Ocular motility was full. Ishihara color plates were 13/14 OD and 0/14 OS. Anterior segment biomicroscopy was unremarkable. Fundus examination showed optic disc pallor OS. The patient was stable until readmission with blurred vision and worsened mental status.

[b00011] Case Report
At admission, her temperature was 97.6°F. Laboratory studies showed a hemoglobin of 13.3 g/dL, a leukocyte count of 8.0 × 10^(3)/µL, and a platelet count of 272 × 10^(3)/µL. C-reactive protein was elevated at 1.50 mg/dL (reference <0.50 mg/dL), and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 22 mm/h. A cell-based assay using flow cytometry (FACS) for AQP4-IgG was positive at 1:100 (normal <1:5), while MOG-IgG by FACS was negative.

[b00012] Case Report
These symptoms raised concern about either an NMOSD relapse or an infection-related pseudo-flare. The patient received empirical intravenous methylprednisolone, but her mental status worsened, and she developed hallucinations. An MRI of the brain, orbits and spine with contrast showed no new lesions or optic nerve enhancement; however, it demonstrated left optic nerve atrophy, without evidence of acute demyelination (Fig. 1). A temporal artery ultrasound was negative. Urinalysis was positive for leukocyte esterase, with 24 white blood cells per high-power field and bacteriuria. A urine culture grew Klebsiella oxytoca at 10^(5) cfu/mL, confirming a urinary tract infection.

[b00013] Case Report
Given the MRI showing no new enhancement, the evidence of infection, and her clinical deterioration, steroids were discontinued, and she was started on doxycycline and amoxicillin-clavulanate. Her mental status then improved, and hallucinations resolved.

[b00014] Case Report
On neuro-ophthalmic examination 3 weeks later, the patient returned to her baseline status. Her visual acuity was 20/50 OD and count fingers OS. Ishihara color plates were 13/14 OD and 0/14 OS. Humphrey visual field-testing (mean deviation 24-2) revealed a nonspecific scatter defect with a mean deviation of −0.78 decibels OD and a dense central scotoma with a superior altitudinal field defect OS. Optical coherence tomography measurements of the retinal nerve fiber layer of the optic nerve measured 103 µm globally OD and 57 µm OS, consistent with sectoral atrophy inferiorly OS corresponding to the superior altitudinal field defect OS.

[b00033] Discussion
According to the American Academy of Family Physicians, UTIs are the most common bacterial infections and the leading source of bacteremia in older adults. The diagnosis was challenging because she did not present with typical signs of infection, unsurprising given her immunosuppressive therapy. She was initially treated with steroids for a presumed NMOSD flare, but symptoms worsened. After MRI showed no new demyelinating lesions and urinalysis suggested infection, steroids were stopped, and antibiotic therapy was initiated.

[b00034] Discussion
In the N-MOmentum study of inebilizumab in NMOSD [], MRI correlates were found in 37 of 41 adjudicated attacks (90%), highlighting MRI’s role in adjudicating flares. In this case, the absence of new enhancing lesions and the presence of infection led to adjudication as a pseudo-relapse. During the N-Momentum randomized controlled period, attacks were adjudicated using predefined attack diagnosis criteria that were developed specifically for this study. The primary endpoint was time to adjudicated attack; secondary endpoints included worsening from baseline in EDSS score at the last visit, cumulative number of active MRI lesions (new gadolinium-enhancing T1 or new/enlarging T2), hospitalizations during the randomized controlled period, and change from baseline in low-contrast visual acuity binocular score. Our patient would not have met adjudicated attack criteria clinically or radiographically for NMOSD relapse.

[b00035] Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first report exploring the distinction between an NMOSD relapse and an infectious pseudo-flare manifesting with visual loss. Clinicians should recognize that NMOSD patients presenting with a presumed relapse may not exhibit classic signs of urinary tract infection due to their immunosuppressed status. Neuroimaging (e.g., cranial and orbital MRI with contrast) without radiographically active disease or optic nerve enhancement in presumed optic neuritis should prompt evaluation for both NMOSD activity and infection-related complications.
