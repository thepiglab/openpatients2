Brimonidine Allergy Presenting as Corneal Subepithelial Infiltrates: A Case Report

[b00002] None
Brimonidine tartrate is an α2-adrenoceptor agonist widely used as an antiglaucoma agent [–]. Nevertheless, the drug is not infrequently associated with ocular allergic reactions, including papillary or follicular conjunctivitis, conjunctival injection, blepharoconjunctivitis, and, rarely, anterior uveitis [,].

[b00003] None
We herein present a case of bilateral corneal subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs) that presumably presenting as brimonidine-induced allergic reaction. Written informed consent for publication of the research details and clinical images was obtained from the patient.

[b00004] None
A 55-year-old Asian male patient had been using tafluprost 0.0015% and timolol maleate 0.5% fixed combination (Tapcom, Santen) for the management of normal tension glaucoma. The intraocular pressure (IOP) was 14 mmHg in the right eye and 16 mmHg in the left eye, as measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry. The medication was switched to brimonidine tartrate 0.2% and timolol maleate 0.5% fixed combination (Bridin plus, Hanlim Pharmaceutical) due to insufficient IOP control. Three months later, the IOP was 11 mmHg in the right eye and 10 mmHg in the left eye. However, mild conjunctival injection and follicular conjunctivitis were noted bilaterally. No conjunctival discharge or membranous formation was observed. Although olopatadine 0.7% ophthalmic solution (Pazeo, Alcon) was prescribed based on a clinical impression of ocular allergic reaction, there was no improvement in the conjunctival injection or conjunctivitis after 2 weeks. Multiple corneal SEIs were also observed bilaterally (Fig. 1A, 1B). There was no corneal epithelial defect or neovascularization. Brimonidine was discontinued, and fluorometholone 0.1% (Samil Pharmaceutical) and cyclosporine 0.05% (Restasis, Allergan) ophthalmic solutions were prescribed. Four days later, improvement of the SEIs was observed bilaterally (Fig. 1C, 1D). At 1 week after the discontinuation of brimonidine, the SEIs had completely resolved (Fig. 1E, 1F). Fluorometholone and cyclosporine were tapered.

[b00005] None
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of corneal SEIs associated with brimonidine-induced ocular allergy in South Korea. Similar cases of corneal SEIs associated with brimonidine use have been reported in other countries in 2020 and 2024 [,,]. Similar to our case, these cases were also accompanied by signs of ocular allergic reaction, including conjunctival injection and follicles, and showed no evidence of infection such as corneal epithelial defect, discharge, membranous formation, respiratory symptoms, or lymphadenopathy [,,]. Most importantly, in these cases, improvement in the corneal infiltrates was also observed within 1 week to 2 months following the discontinuation of brimonidine [,,]. Purgert et al. [] reported that no additional treatment was needed, whereas others added topical fluorometholone to manage the SEIs. We used topical fluorometholone and cyclosporine to promote rapid resolution of the infiltrates and prevent visual sequelae.

[b00006] None
Although the mechanism underlying the formation of corneal SEIs associated with brimonidine-induced ocular allergy remains unclear, it is presumed to involve delayed type hypersensitivity reaction [,]. In this T cell–mediated immune response, prolonged exposure to antigens, possibly components of brimonidine or antigens induced by its use, may activate sensitized Th1 cells. This triggers an inflammatory cascade characterized by the release of proinflammatory cytokines, macrophage activation, and infiltration of inflammatory cells into the corneal stroma [,–]. The resulting immune response leads to the formation of SEIs, as observed in our case [,,]. Studies have also suggested that brimonidine decreases conjunctival epithelial cell volume and expands intercellular spaces, thereby facilitating the entry of potential allergens into the subepithelial tissue and triggering an immune cascade [,]. In an animal experimental study, Shin et al. [] demonstrated that topical application of brimonidine resulted in elevated levels of intracameral inflammatory cytokines and lymphocytic infiltration in conjunctival tissue, supporting these proposed mechanisms. Preservatives, such as, benzalkonium chloride, have also been suggested to trigger immune responses [,]. However, this may not be applicable to our case, as a preservative-free formulation was used.

[b00007] None
In conclusion, corneal SEIs can occur as a manifestation of an allergic reaction to brimonidine. Thorough review of the patient’s medication history, including the use of brimonidine, is essential in cases of SEIs without clinical signs of infection, such as epidemic keratoconjunctivitis. In suspected cases, discontinuation of brimonidine is needed for the resolution of infiltrates. Topical application of anti-inflammatory medications, such as corticosteroids or cyclosporine, may also be beneficial.
