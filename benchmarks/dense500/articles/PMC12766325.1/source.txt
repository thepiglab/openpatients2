Endovascular Management of Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysms in Patients with Nickel Allergy: Three Cases

[b00001] Abstract / Objective
Nickel allergy is one of the most common forms of metal hypersensitivity, yet its relevance to neuroendovascular therapy has rarely been reported. We describe 3 cases of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients with confirmed nickel allergy, and review the literature to highlight safe endovascular management strategies.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
Three female patients (2 in their 70s and 1 in her 60s) were incidentally diagnosed with unruptured intracranial aneurysms located at the internal carotid artery (n = 2) and the vertebral artery (n = 1). All patients tested positive on patch testing for nickel. To minimize allergic risk, devices with low nickel content were chosen, including bare platinum coils (i-ED coils; Kaneka Medix, Osaka, Japan) and a flow diverter (Surpass Evolve; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Endovascular treatment was completed without perioperative complications, and no allergic reactions were observed during a mean follow-up of 12.7 months. Corticosteroids were not administered in any of the cases.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusion
Endovascular treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients with nickel allergy can be performed safely by avoiding devices with high nickel content and by performing preoperative allergy testing. Awareness of device materials is critical for preventing adverse reactions and ensuring favorable outcomes.

[b00004] Introduction
Nickel allergy is one of the most common types of metal hypersensitivity, affecting approximately 8%–19% of the adult population in Europe.^()) While occasionally encountered in dermatologic practice, it has also become relevant in neuroendovascular therapy due to the widespread use of nickel-containing devices such as coils, neck-bridge stents, and flow diverters. Allergic reactions to these devices have been linked to complications including stroke, in-stent stenosis, and delayed white matter lesions. In this report, we describe 3 cases of unruptured cerebral aneurysms treated endovascularly in patients with confirmed nickel allergy, all of whom experienced favorable outcomes without any apparent allergic reactions.

[b00005] Case Presentation / Case 1
A woman in her 60s was referred to our hospital for further evaluation and treatment of an unruptured aneurysm measuring over 5 mm at the right internal carotid–posterior communicating artery junction, which had been identified at another hospital. Cerebral angiography confirmed the presence of the aneurysm (Fig. 1A), and preventive treatment was planned. Following the angiographic evaluation, the patient reported a history of metal allergy. Patch testing conducted by our dermatology department revealed positive reactions to both nickel and gold (Fig. 1B). Although surgical clipping with titanium clips was considered, the patient preferred an endovascular approach; therefore, coil embolization was selected. Because certain embolization coils are known to contain nickel, often as a component of stainless steel or nitinol, there was concern that implantation could trigger an allergic reaction.^()) Accordingly, we planned coil embolization using only i-ED coils (Kaneka Medix, Osaka, Japan), which are reported to be nickel-free. Dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated 10 days before the procedure. Under general anesthesia, a 9-Fr balloon-guiding catheter was advanced into the right internal carotid artery via the right femoral artery, followed by the insertion of a 6-Fr distal support catheter up to the petrous segment. Using the double-catheter technique, a total of 6 i-ED coils were deployed, and the endovascular procedure was completed with a small neck remnant (Fig. 1C). Postoperative diffusion-weighted MRI performed the following day showed a punctate high-intensity lesion in the right basal ganglia, which was asymptomatic. During more than 1 year of follow-up, no allergic symptoms were observed. Dual antiplatelet therapy was reduced to a single agent after the procedure and continued for 1 month.

[b00006] Case Presentation / Case 2
A woman in her 70s was referred to our department for further evaluation and treatment after a saccular aneurysm with a maximum diameter of 11 mm in the left vertebral artery was identified at a local clinic (Fig. 2A). During her admission for diagnostic workup, she reported a history of metal allergy. Patch testing performed by our dermatology department revealed a positive reaction to nickel (Fig. 2B). Stent assistance was deemed essential for treatment; however, all available neck-bridge stents—including the Enterprise (Codman Neuro, Raynham, MA, USA), LVIS (Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA), and Neuroform (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA)—were found to contain nickel and were therefore excluded. As an alternative, the use of a flow diverter was considered. However, both Pipeline Flex (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and FRED (Terumo Neuro), which are generally approved for this indication, also contain nickel. Therefore, we opted for the Surpass Evolve (Stryker), which is reported to have a lower nickel content based on its composition. Under general anesthesia, a 6-Fr guiding sheath was introduced via the right femoral artery and advanced to the left subclavian artery. A distal support catheter was then navigated to the V2 segment of the left vertebral artery, and a microcatheter was subsequently advanced to the V4 segment distal to the aneurysm. A Surpass Evolve (4.0 × 20 mm) device was deployed across the aneurysm neck to ensure complete coverage (Fig. 2C). No apparent allergic symptoms were observed during more than 6 months of follow-up. Dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated 10 days prior to the procedure, with a plan to transition to single antiplatelet therapy 1 year after endovascular treatment.

[b00007] Case Presentation / Case 3
A woman in her 70s had been under follow-up at a previous hospital for a known aneurysm located medially at the C2 segment of the right internal carotid artery (Fig. 3A). Due to gradual enlargement over several years, she was referred to our department for treatment. The aneurysm exhibited a “snowman-like” morphology, with a maximum diameter exceeding 5 mm (Fig. 3B). Given the risk of rupture, endovascular intervention was considered. The patient reported a history of metal allergy, and a patch test performed at another hospital revealed a positive result for nickel. Preoperative imaging suggested that navigating a microcatheter into the aneurysm would be technically difficult. Even if catheterization were successful, the likelihood of achieving sufficient coil packing was considered low. Therefore, the use of a flow diverter was considered, and the Surpass Evolve, which is reported to have a relatively low nickel content, was selected. Under general anesthesia, a 6-Fr guiding sheath was inserted via the right femoral artery, and a 5-Fr distal support catheter was positioned just proximal to the carotid siphon. A microcatheter was then advanced into the right middle cerebral artery. A Surpass Evolve (4.0 × 15 mm) was successfully deployed from just proximal to the posterior communicating artery to the C4 segment of the internal carotid artery, fully covering the aneurysm neck (Fig. 3C). No allergic symptoms were observed during a follow-up period of more than 6 months. Antiplatelet therapy was the same as that used in Case 2.

[b00008] Discussion
Nickel is the most common cause of metal allergy, yet it is widely used due to its low cost and ease of processing. In daily life, nickel-containing items such as earrings, necklaces, eyeglasses, and cooking utensils are frequently encountered. Moreover, nickel is also present in various medical devices, including orthopedic implants, orthodontic appliances, and vascular grafts in the cardiovascular field, leading to frequent direct contact. Nickel allergy is a condition caused by direct skin contact with or implantation of nickel-containing metal products, resulting in local or systemic dermatitis. It is suggested to be mediated by a type IV hypersensitivity reaction.^()) In neuroendovascular therapy devices commonly used in our practice, nickel is found in coils, neck-bridge stents, and flow diverters. Generally, these devices contain some amount of nickel, either as stainless steel—an alloy primarily composed of iron with chromium and nickel—or as nitinol, a synthetic metal alloy of nickel and titanium mixed in a fixed ratio. Implantation of these devices carries a potential risk of inducing allergic reactions. The use of coils, neck-bridge stents, and flow diverters is contraindicated by all manufacturers for patients with metal allergies when treating intracranial aneurysms. Therefore, when considering neuroendovascular treatment for patients with metal allergies, it is essential that clinicians possess accurate knowledge of the risks and benefits associated with device use and thoroughly evaluate the appropriateness of the treatment prior to the procedure.

[b00009] Discussion / Causes of nickel allergy
As mentioned above, type IV hypersensitivity is suggested to play a role in nickel allergy; however, many aspects of the pathogenesis following device implantation remain unclear.^()) Jensen et al. proposed that the onset of symptoms depends on the concentration of nickel in the bloodstream.^()) According to Narayana et al., blood nickel levels peak at 1 month postimplantation and reach a steady state by 6 months.^()) Therefore, even if no issues arise immediately after the procedure, careful monitoring for at least 6 months is crucial. In our institution, all 3 cases have been followed for more than 6 months postoperatively, with no allergic symptoms observed.

[b00010] Discussion / Causes of nickel allergy
Regarding the question of whether symptoms develop only after blood nickel levels exceed a certain threshold, Vanent et al. investigated this using intracranial stents.^()) They quantitatively measured nickel elution from 7 different intracranial stents: 5 neck-bridge stents (Neuroform [Stryker], Wingspan [Stryker], Enterprise [Codman Neuro], LVIS Jr [Terumo Neuro], and Zilver [Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA]) and 2 flow diverters (Surpass Evolve [Stryker] and Pipeline Flex [Medtronic]). Each stent was cultured in a human plasma-like medium for 30 days, and nickel release was measured on days 0 and 30. Their results showed no detectable nickel release from any of the stents, suggesting that stents might be safely used even in patients with nickel allergy. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the onset of nickel allergy after device implantation remain largely unknown.

[b00011] Discussion / Clinical manifestations of nickel allergy
Reports of nickel allergy in the field of endovascular therapy are most commonly found in cardiovascular medicine. Adverse symptoms such as skin rashes, fatigue, and chest discomfort have been observed following the implantation of coronary stents and atrial septal occluder devices.^(–)) In some cases, local allergic reactions within the heart have been associated with in-stent restenosis.^()) Furthermore, although based on in vitro studies, there are reports indicating that the release of metal ions from coronary stents can induce contact dermatitis.^()) On the other hand, some studies have found no clear association between coronary stent implantation and restenosis in patients with documented metal allergies.^()) Overall, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying nickel allergy following device implantation in the cardiovascular field remain largely uncertain.

[b00012] Discussion / Clinical manifestations of nickel allergy
In the field of neurosurgery, Tsang et al. reviewed 10 cases (reported in 9 articles) of nickel allergy between 1960 and 2018.^()) Among them, 5 cases involved craniotomy with the use of nickel-containing aneurysm clips, while the other 5 were associated with endovascular devices—4 with intracranial stents or flow diverters and 1 with a guidewire. Two patients developed dermatological symptoms, while the remaining 8 exhibited neurological manifestations, including paralysis, encephalitis, or seizures due to cerebral edema. Recently, there have also been scattered reports of delayed white matter lesions postoperatively. While these lesions may be attributable to nickel allergy, another possibility involves foreign body embolization caused by shedding of hydrophilic coatings—such as polyvinylpyrrolidone—from microcatheters and other devices, leading to granulomatous inflammatory reactions.^()) In addition, various systemic symptoms have been reported in nickel-allergic patients, including respiratory, gastrointestinal, and psychiatric symptoms. A study focusing on device-specific reactions found that 13.9% (5 of 36) of nickel-allergic patients who underwent endovascular procedures—including neurovascular and cardiovascular interventions—developed in-stent restenosis or occlusion.^()) Moreover, a significant association has been reported between the “fish mouth” phenomenon at the ends of flow diverters and a history of allergy to metal jewelry, particularly those containing nickel.^())

[b00013] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
The diagnosis of metal allergy typically involves a combination of clinical history, patch testing, the lymphocyte stimulation test, and skin biopsy, among which patch testing is considered the most convenient and effective screening method. However, because the sensitivity and specificity of patch testing are reported to be approximately 70%–80%, clinicians should be aware of the possibility of both false-positive and false-negative results. A meta-analysis by Guéroult et al. demonstrated a significant association between a positive nickel patch test and an increased risk of adverse events following endovascular procedures (odds ratio 2.61, 95% confidence interval 1.41–4.85). Interestingly, this correlation was not observed in patients who had self-reported a history of metal allergy prior to the procedure.^())

[b00014] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
Therefore, all patients with a history of metal allergy at our institution routinely undergo preoperative patch testing. However, there have been occasional cases suggesting that test results do not necessarily correlate with adverse reactions to nickel-containing endovascular devices. Although patch testing is more reliable than self-reporting, it cannot fully predict such adverse events.

[b00015] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
One management strategy involves the use of devices with low or no nickel content, which has been shown to significantly reduce the incidence of allergic reactions in the cardiovascular field.^()) According to Tsang et al., commonly used neck-bridge stents such as Enterprise, LVIS, and Neuroform contain approximately 55% nickel.^()) While embolization coils are reported to have 0% nickel content, there have nonetheless been case reports of allergic reactions to coils,^()) suggesting a possible discrepancy in our current understanding. As for flow diverters, the nickel content varies by device: FRED contains approximately 55%, Pipeline 25%–28%, and Surpass 14%–16%. The Woven EndoBridge (Terumo Neuro) also has a reported nickel content of 55%.^())

[b00016] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
Although the implantation of nickel-containing devices in patients with a known metal allergy is generally contraindicated, it may be considered when no suitable alternatives exist or when other treatment options are infeasible. In our cases, we used i-ED coils for Case 1, and Surpass Evolve for Cases 2 and 3. Many coils, such as Target (Stryker) and Axium (Medtronic), may contain nickel as a component of their alloy composition. In contrast, i-ED coils are composed of a platinum–tungsten alloy and, according to the manufacturer’s documentation, are nickel-free, potentially reducing the risk of allergic reactions.

[b00017] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
Among currently available flow diverters, Surpass Evolve has one of the lowest reported nickel content levels—approximately 15%.^()) Moreover, recent in vitro studies have shown that it does not release nickel ions,^()) and it was therefore chosen for its presumed lower risk of allergic complications after implantation. However, since its approved indication in Japan is limited to anterior circulation aneurysms with a diameter of at least 10 mm, its use in these cases was off-label. Accordingly, approval was obtained from our institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient and their family prior to treatment. If, as suggested by some reports, allergic reactions are dose-dependent with respect to blood nickel concentrations, then the absence of postoperative symptoms for more than 6 months in all 3 cases suggests a minimal likelihood of future allergic responses. Based on these findings, we believe that the careful selection and use of low-nickel-content devices in patients with nickel allergy undergoing endovascular treatment for cerebral aneurysms may lead to favorable outcomes.

[b00018] Discussion / Diagnosis and management of nickel allergy
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published report to describe the successful use of the aforementioned devices specifically in patients with nickel allergy. However, we would like to emphasize that this report is not intended to promote or recommend the routine use of such devices in allergic individuals. On the other hand, there are prior reports of patients with metal allergy treated using Pipeline embolization devices. One study tracked 26 aneurysms in 20 patients with metal allergies and found no evidence of allergic reactions postoperatively, concluding that the treatment was both safe and effective.^()) Another report documented 2 patients with nickel allergy treated with Pipeline, who remained free of allergic symptoms for more than 2 years after treatment.^()) Interestingly, one study reported implanting a nickel-containing peripheral arterial stent in the forearm of a patient with confirmed nickel allergy to evaluate tolerance, and subsequently using nickel-containing devices for treatment when no reaction occurred^())—a strategy that may raise ethical considerations. Table 1 summarizes previously reported cases and the current cases of endovascular treatment for cerebral aneurysms in patients with nickel allergy.^(–)) Collectively, these findings highlight the need for continued accumulation and analysis of cases to better understand the relationship between nickel allergy and neuroendovascular therapy.

[b00051] Conclusion
This report highlights the critical importance of device selection in the endovascular treatment of cerebral aneurysms in patients with nickel allergy. While most currently available neuroendovascular devices contain nickel, the use of devices with lower nickel content allowed for safe and effective treatment without triggering allergic reactions. These findings underscore the importance of thorough preoperative evaluation—including patch testing—and careful planning of treatment strategies to minimize the risk of complications in this patient population.
