MGRS or MGUS? Diagnostic Dilemma of ANCA-Associated Pauci-Immune Crescentic Glomerulonephritis with Monoclonal Gammopathy: A Case Report

[b00001] Abstract / Introduction
Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is the most common plasma cell dyscrasia, defined by clonal monoclonal immunoglobulin (MIg) in serum and/or urine in the absence of end-organ damage caused by plasma cell proliferation. By contrast, monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) encompasses a spectrum of kidney disorders directly or indirectly driven by MIg, with clinical phenotypes ranging from insidious proteinuria to rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. The diagnostic ambiguity arising when MGUS coexists with renal dysfunction remains a major clinical challenge. Here, we report a rare case of a patient initially suspected to have MGRS complicated by pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis (PICGN). However, this diagnosis was ultimately excluded by the absence of MIg deposits in renal tissue on histopathological examination.

[b00002] Abstract / Case Presentation
We describe a female with concurrent PICGN and MGUS, presented with acute kidney injury, proteinuria, and hematuria, with renal biopsy revealing type III crescentic glomerulonephritis and immunofluorescence showing weak positivity for κ and λ MIg deposits. Despite initial suspicion of MGRS, immuno-electron microscopy did not confirm monoclonal light chain deposition, leading to a final diagnosis of PICGN and MGUS.

[b00003] Abstract / Conclusions
This case underscores the importance of integrating serological, histopathological, and advanced imaging techniques to distinguish between autoimmune and plasma cell dyscrasias in renal pathology. It also emphasizes the limitations of immunofluorescence alone in diagnosing MGRS and the necessity of immuno-electron microscopy for definitive exclusion. This report calls for further research into the pathophysiological interactions between ANCA-associated vasculitis and monoclonal gammopathies, particularly in cases with overlapping renal injury features.

[b00004] Introduction
Pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis (PICGN) is a form of crescentic glomerulonephritis mediated by ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV). The pathological features of PICGN include the formation of crescents of varying ages and fibrinoid necrosis, with immunofluorescence microscopy showing little to no deposition of immunoglobulins or complements []. Clinically, it presents with microscopic hematuria, red blood cell casts, proteinuria, and may even manifest as rapidly worsening renal function, progressing swiftly to end-stage renal disease [].

[b00005] Introduction
Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is a common type of plasma cell proliferative disorder characterized by three key features: (1) the presence of abnormal monoclonal immunoglobulin (Mlg) or immunoglobulin free light chains in blood and/or urine []; (2) serum monoclonal protein <3 g/dL and clonal bone marrow plasma cells <10%; (3) absence of end-organ damage attributable to plasma cell proliferative disorders, such as hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia, or bone lesions []. However, when renal disease coexists with MGUS, it is essential to differentiate it from another condition known as monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS). MGRS is a renal disorder caused by nephrotoxic Mlg secreted by B cells or plasma cells, in the absence of symptoms of multiple myeloma (MM), waldenström macroglobulinemia, or chronic lymphocytic leukemia []. These Mlg can directly or indirectly induce kidney injury [].

[b00006] Introduction
Studies have shown that, compared to patients with non-MGRS monoclonal gammopathy and renal disease, MGRS patients are typically older, have fewer comorbidities, exhibit higher levels of proteinuria, demonstrate greater abnormalities in free light chain ratios, and more frequently present with abnormal bone marrow findings []. Here, we report a case of PICGN coexisting with MGUS. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report, including associated images and tables.

[b00007] Case Report
A 55-year-old female patient with no significant past medical history was admitted to our hospital due to rapidly declining renal function. Upon admission, she presented with symptoms including cough, vomiting, fatigue, oliguria, and foamy urine, as well as a recent weight loss of 5 kg. Physical examination revealed the following: body temperature 36.4°C, heart rate 63 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure 128/69 mm Hg. There was no costovertebral angle tenderness, and no edema was observed in the lower extremities. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Upon further detailed history-taking, the patient reported that her serum creatinine (SCr) level was within the normal range during a routine health check-up 1 month prior to admission.

[b00008] Case Report
Laboratory tests upon admission revealed the following: hemoglobin (HGB) 79 g/L (normal range: 115–150 g/L), SCr 1106 μmol/L (normal range: 41–73 μmol/L), Serum albumin 35 g/L (normal range: 40–55 g/L), and hypersensitive C-reactive protein 84.45 mg/L (normal range: 0–10 mg/L). Neutrophil percentage was 83% (normal range: 40–75%). Urinalysis showed 2+ proteinuria and 3+ hematuria. These findings indicated that the patient was suffering from acute kidney injury and likely had an inflammatory renal disease. Chest CT revealed nodules, cavities, and exudative lesions, and the renal ultrasonography indicated altered parenchymal echogenicity in both kidneys, while the remaining examination results showed no significant abnormalities.

[b00009] Case Report
To establish a definitive diagnosis, we performed additional tests, including antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) test, an autoimmune antibody panel (shown in Table 1), immunoglobulin assays, complement testing, serum-free light chain analysis, immunofixation electrophoresis (the presence of an IgGλ MIg in serum immunofixation electrophoresis), and serum protein electrophoresis (shown in Table 2). A renal biopsy was also conducted (shown in Fig. 1, 2), which revealed findings consistent with PICGN.

[b00059] Case Report
Based on the patient’s autoimmune profile, which demonstrated the anti-PM-Scl antibody weak positive (reference range: negative) and anti-PR3 antibody strong positive (reference range: negative) in the ANCA test, in conjunction with the renal biopsy findings, a provisional diagnosis of ANCA-associated PICGN was considered. Given the results of serum protein electrophoresis and serum-free light chain analysis, the possibility of MGRS or MM could not be excluded. Consequently, a bone marrow biopsy and flow cytometric analysis were performed, supplemented by immuno-electron microscopy of the renal biopsy (shown in Fig. 3). The bone marrow biopsy revealed scattered plasma cells, accounting for approximately 2% of the cellularity. Flow cytometric analysis identified approximately 0.12% monoclonal plasma cells with an immunophenotype positive for CD38, CD138, and CD56, and exhibiting intracellular immunoglobulin lambda light chain restriction. However, immuno-electron microscopy of the renal biopsy did not demonstrate definitive evidence of monoclonal light chain deposition. Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with PICGN and MGUS.

[b00060] Case Report
The patient received treatment including hemodialysis, intravenous methylprednisolone sodium succinate pulse therapy (500 mg/day on the first day, and 250 mg/day for 4 days, followed by 40 mg/day, ivgtt), and cyclophosphamide therapy (600 mg, ivgtt, monthly). Upon discharge, the corticosteroid regimen was transitioned to oral prednisone acetate (50 mg/day, p.o. tapering off). During the 3-month follow-up period, the patient’s Scr level stabilized at approximately 450 μmol/L, with the anti-PR3 antibody gradually changing from strong positive to negative (The patient reported during a telephone follow-up). Serum levels of κ- and λ-free light chains also demonstrated a gradual reduction, and the κ/λ FLC ratio returned to near-normal levels. Regretly, due to the irreversible nature of the renal impairment, the patient remains dependent on maintenance hemodialysis (shown in Fig. 4).

[b00062] Discussion
This case report delineates a rare co-occurrence of PICGN and MGUS. The diagnosis of PICGN was substantiated by the presence of strong positive anti-PR3 antibodies in the serum ANCA panel, corroborated by renal histopathology demonstrating extensive fibrinoid necrosis with scant immunoglobulin and complement deposition. Concurrently, MGUS was confirmed through comprehensive diagnostic modalities, including serum immunofixation electrophoresis, bone marrow biopsy, and immuno-electron microscopic analysis of renal tissue.

[b00063] Discussion
Initial diagnostic considerations included MGRS, prompted by the detection of weakly positive κ and λ Mlg deposits on renal biopsy immunofluorescence. However, the diagnostic criteria for MGRS, which necessitate the identification of MIg deposits in renal tissue via biopsy and immunofluorescence, were not fully met []. The immuno-electron microscopy revealed no definitive evidence of such deposits, thereby excluding MGRS from the final diagnosis.

[b00064] Discussion
The acute kidney injury in this case was predominantly attributed to PICGN, as evidenced by renal biopsy findings consistent with type III crescentic glomerulonephritis. Additionally, the patient’s chest CT findings of nodular, cavitary, and exudative lesions were interpreted as extrarenal manifestations of AAV []. The presence of MGUS, however, introduced a layer of diagnostic complexity, as MIgs are known to exert direct nephrotoxic effects or indirectly contribute to renal injury through mechanisms such as complement activation or amyloid deposition.

[b00065] Discussion
Certain subtypes of Monoclonal Gammopathy of Renal Significance (MGRS) can manifest as rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN). These include proliferative glomerulonephritis with MIg deposits (PGNMID) [], C3 glomerulopathy [], cryoglobulinemic vasculitis []. A hallmark of nearly all MGRS subtypes is the deposition of immune complexes and/or MIg light chains within the kidney, as evidenced by renal biopsy. For instance, PGNMID is characterized by immunofluorescence revealing granular deposits along the glomerular capillary loops, typically composed of a single immunoglobulin isotype (most commonly IgG3) and a single light chain (κ or λ). In C3 glomerulopathy, immunofluorescence demonstrates dominant C3 deposition []. Cryoglobulinemic vasculitis can be identified ultrastructurally by electron microscopy, which shows organized mesangial and subendothelial deposits with frequently curved, tubular, or annular substructures, accompanied by significant infiltration of monocytes and macrophages []. These distinct pathological features stand in sharp contrast to the renal biopsy findings in our case, which showed only weak, nondominant staining for both light chains and no definitive deposits on immuno-electron microscopy. Therefore, a diagnosis of MGRS was ruled out.

[b00066] Discussion
The co-occurrence of ANCA-associated PICGN and MGUS presents significant diagnostic challenges, particularly in distinguishing whether acute renal failure stems from AAV or Mlg-related injury. Both conditions can manifest with overlapping features such as acute kidney injury, hematuria, and proteinuria [, ]. Hematological tests alone are insufficient for differentiation, especially given the well-documented occurrence of ANCA-negative PICGN since its systematic reporting in 2009 [] and subsequent recognition in later studies [–]. While bone marrow biopsy helps exclude B-cell proliferative disorders like MM and WM, it cannot definitively diagnose MGRS or MGUS in the context of renal injury.

[b00067] Discussion
Renal biopsy remains the cornerstone for accurate diagnosis, enabling the identification of key features such as crescents, fibrinoid necrosis, and MIg deposits through histopathology, immunofluorescence, and immuno-electron microscopy. This approach is critical for differentiating ANCA-mediated injury from Mlg-related nephropathy, particularly when serological and hematological results are inconclusive.

[b00068] Discussion
The coexistence of ANCA and monoclonal gammopathy has long been a focus of interest in such cases, with many scholars suggesting that this association is not coincidental. The relationship between monoclonal gammopathy and autoimmunity has been explored since 1990. Esnault et al. [] detected ANCA positivity in 16 out of 150 samples from patients with monoclonal gammopathy, proposing that ANCA represents an additional autoreactive component in the sera of certain monoclonal gammopathies. Similarly, Wang et al. [] identified Mlg positivity in 16 out of 91 AAV patients and suggested that the presence of Mlg may increase mortality in AAV. McShane et al. [], through a systematic review, reported that individuals with autoimmune diseases have a 1.42-fold higher relative risk of developing MGUS compared to those without autoimmune conditions. Hishida et al. [] hypothesized that AAV and monoclonal gammopathy may be linked through immune-mediated mechanisms. Other researchers have proposed that the concurrence of AAV with hematological disorders, including MM or MGUS, may be driven by inflammation-induced stimulation []. Regrettably, many scholars believe that there is a connection between ANCA and monoclonal gammopathy, but conclusive evidence is still lacking.

[b00069] Discussion
In this case, the patient was treated with a standard therapeutic regimen for AAV, comprising glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide. Although initial treatment led to some improvement in renal function, the severity and irreversibility of the renal damage ultimately necessitated maintenance hemodialysis. Notably, following the administration of glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide, the Mlg previously detected in hematological tests became undetectable. However, the follow-up period in this case was insufficient to determine whether Mlg would reappear upon discontinuation of glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide.

[b00070] Discussion
In summary, we report a case of concurrent PICGN and MGUS, presenting with acute kidney injury, proteinuria, and hematuria. This case underscores the critical importance of integrating serological, histopathological, and advanced imaging techniques to delineate the contributions of coexisting autoimmune and plasma cell dyscrasias to renal pathology. It also highlights the limitations of relying solely on immunofluorescence for diagnosing MGRS and emphasizes the necessity of immuno-electron microscopy for definitive exclusion. Furthermore, this case calls for further research to elucidate the pathophysiological interactions between AAV and monoclonal gammopathies, particularly in scenarios where renal injury exhibits overlapping features. In conclusion, we believe this is a clinical case of diagnostic and therapeutic implications.
