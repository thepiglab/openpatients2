Severe allergic reaction to ustekinumab reinitiation in a pediatric patient with Crohn's disease: A case report

[b00001] Abstract
Ustekinumab (UST) is a monoclonal antibody used in the management of moderate to severe inflammatory bowel disease. While generally well‐tolerated, serious adverse reactions, including hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis, have been documented, specifical following the initial intravenous (IV) dose. There is limited literature on hypersensitivity reactions in pediatric patients, particularly after re‐induction with IV therapy. This case highlights a novel, previously unreported scenario of a severe allergic reaction in a 10‐year‐old female with Crohn's disease (CD) during her second, re‐induction IV dose of UST, despite prior tolerance and no detectable UST antibodies. This case emphasizes the need for continued monitoring during IV UST administration, even in patients with prior tolerability.

[b00002] INTRODUCTION
Ustekinumab (UST) is a monoclonal antibody employed in the treatment of moderate to severe Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis. This IgG1 antibody works through its action as an interleukin‐12 (IL‐12) and interleukin‐23 (IL‐23) antagonist, inhibiting their interaction with the IL‐12 receptor beta‐1 on natural killer and T cells to reduce inflammation. In patients with IBD, UST treatment is typically initiated as an intravenous (IV) dose, followed by subcutaneous (SQ) maintenance doses every 8 weeks. While this medication is typically well tolerated, serious side effects have been reported, including various infections, malignancies, and allergic reactions.

[b00003] INTRODUCTION
Unfortunately, there is limited literature on hypersensitivity and anaphylactic reactions in pediatric patients receiving UST, and there are no reports of these allergic reactions following a second IV re‐induction in a child that previously tolerated the initial infusion., , , , This case highlights this unusual, severe allergic reaction during UST IV reinitiation, despite previous tolerance to initial exposures and an absence of anti‐drug antibodies.

[b00004] CASE REPORT
A 10‐year‐old female presented to the clinic with hematochezia, tenesmus and abdominal pain. Complete workup revealed an elevated fecal calprotectin of 2030 μg/g (normal: <50 μg/g) and biopsy findings of mild chronic duodenitis, chronic inactive gastritis with granulomatous inflammation, chronic active gastritis, and mild chronic active colitis. Computed tomography enterography demonstrated a 6‐cm segment of terminal ileal inflammation with mild rectal involvement (Figure 1). She was diagnosed with Crohn's disease, classified as A1b, L3, L4a, B1, G0 per the Paris classification, and started on Adalimumab (ADA).

[b00005] CASE REPORT
ADA was initiated with an induction dose of 80 mg then 40 mg 2 weeks later, followed by maintenance of 20 mg every 2 weeks, based on her weight of 24.7 kg. Persistent inflammation (with calprotectin of 1030 μg/g) and suboptimal ADA level (7.6 μg/mL; normal: ≥7–10 μg/mL) led to dose escalation to 40 mg every 2 weeks. Despite transient improvement with an ADA level of 11.3 μg/mL one year later, inflammation persisted, prompting repeat esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy, confirming chronic active gastritis, duodenitis, and ileitis with focal lamina propria granulomata. Given inadequate response, she transitioned to UST.

[b00006] CASE REPORT
Following the first IV loading dose of 260 mg UST the patient developed a transient, erythematous rash at the infusion site. She subsequently tolerated one SQ injection without complications 8 weeks later.

[b00007] CASE REPORT
Due to insurance gaps and coverage, the patient was without UST treatment for 4 months, so the clinical decision was to re‐induce UST 260 mg as a one‐time IV dose administration. Within 30 s of beginning the infusion at an outside hospital, the patient was reported to develop clinical acute respiratory distress, characterized by facial cyanosis and dyspnea. The infusion was immediately discontinued, and intramuscular epinephrine, oxygen via nasal cannula delivery, and fluids were administered. She was placed under observation, with complete resolution of symptoms.

[b00008] CASE REPORT
Post‐reaction laboratory evaluation 2 months later revealed a mild iron deficiency anemia, with a hemoglobin of 11.4 g/dL (normal: 11.5–15.5 g/dL), and elevated inflammatory markers with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 59 (normal: <20 mm/h) and C‐reactive protein of 6.8 (< 1 mg/L). Notably, antibody testing for UST was negative, with no detectable drug levels present. Notably, α‐gal testing was also negative. Based on these findings and the conclusions drawn from prior case reports, the decision was made with the family to proceed with UST SQ treatment.

[b00009] CASE REPORT
SQ administration of UST was given in office for monitoring. It was well‐tolerated without adverse reactions. She was monitored for 30 min and did not receive any pre‐medication.

[b00010] DISCUSSION
The monoclonal antibody UST has been effective in the management of moderate to severe CD due to its action against the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐12 and IL‐23., Although common side effects include headache, gastrointestinal symptoms, and injection site irritation, serious adverse reactions have been noted, such as infection, malignancy, and allergic responses. Prior reports have highlighted anaphylaxis and hypersensitivity reactions to IV UST, with one case of an adult patient with CD experiencing a hypersensitivity reaction upon restarting IV UST with no mention of antibody testing., However, no literature has specifically showcased a severe allergic response in a pediatric patient following a second IV initiation without antibodies, despite tolerating the induction. This case underscores the importance of careful monitoring of patients receiving IV initiation of UST for hypersensitivity reactions, even when prior administration has been uneventful.

[b00011] DISCUSSION
Various hypotheses have been proposed as to the etiology of the reactions to IV infusions of UST. One hypothesis suggests the reaction may be due to an ingredient in the infusion rather than the drug itself., , Most attention has been given to the additive ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) only present in the infusion formulation, as patients who experienced reactions to the infusion were able to tolerate the SQ formulation of the drug., , In a case series involving six pediatric and young adult patients, those who reacted to the first infusion, ranging from mild allergic reactions to full anaphylaxis, had no detectable antibodies and safely received the SQ injection without complications. This phenomenon has been observed in multiple other reports, suggesting these reactions are likely due to an ingredient in the infusion formulation as opposed to the drug., , Consistent with these findings, the patient described herein experienced an acute reaction to the IV infusion but subsequently tolerated the re‐initiation of the SQ injection without incident, further supporting this hypothesis.

[b00012] DISCUSSION
In another case series of six patients with allergic UST infusion reactions, it was found that those affected had immunoglobulin E specific for galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose (α‐gal) in their serum. Notably, these individuals also tolerated SQ administration of UST. However, a direct relationship between the anti‐α‐gal antibody and these infusion reactions remain uncertain. In the case of our patient, she did not test positive for α‐gal.

[b00013] DISCUSSION
Since the reaction described herein is extremely rare and has not yet been reported in other patients undergoing UST re‐induction, additional monitoring beyond the standard protocol is likely not necessary. That said, it remains important to be aware that these reactions can occur and to educate patients and their families about their possibility. In this case, the patient endorsed a transient rash during her initial infusion, consistent with a mild infusion site reaction rather than a sign of hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis. As UST induction typically only involves a single IV dose and a hypersensitivity reaction on re‐induction had not yet been reported, this mild reaction did not alter the subsequent treatment approach. Nonetheless, clinicians should remain aware of minor infusion site reactions, but routine changes to monitoring or dosing are likely unnecessary unless more significant symptoms occur.

[b00014] CONCLUSION
This case emphasizes the need for monitoring of patients receiving the IV formulation of UST, and continued vigilance if re‐induction is required. This case further highlights that when anti‐drug antibodies are absent, especially after prior tolerance, SQ administration may be considered, but it requires careful clinical judgment, family discussion, and close monitoring. Moreover, further investigation into the exact mechanism and the role of possible infusion‐related components, like EDTA, and influence of α‐gal is still needed.
