Two Cases of Psychiatric Symptoms Associated with Zonisamide Antiepileptic Treatment

[b00001] Abstract
To report two cases of psychiatric symptoms associated with zonisamide, an antiepileptic drug, and raise clinical awareness of this potential adverse effect. Two male patients with epilepsy treated with zonisamide were retrospectively analyzed. Case 1 (25 years old) developed acute emotional and behavioral abnormalities (e.g., insomnia, aggression, incoherent speech) after switching from sodium valproate to zonisamide (200 mg/day). Case 2 (48 years old) had long-term zonisamide use (≥5 years) with persistent treatment-resistant psychotic symptoms (e.g., delusions, command hallucinations). Clinical courses, medication adjustments, and symptom responses were documented. In Case 1, psychiatric symptoms resolved after discontinuing zonisamide and switching to sodium valproate, with improved mood stability and reduced impulsivity. In Case 2, despite escalating antipsychotic medications (risperidone, clozapine), psychotic symptoms persisted, likely due to ongoing zonisamide use. Both cases highlighted zonisamide’s potential to exacerbate or induce psychiatric manifestations, possibly via mechanisms involving sodium/calcium channel inhibition and neurotransmitter dysregulation (e.g., dopamine, serotonin). Zonisamide can cause or worsen psychiatric symptoms, particularly in vulnerable individuals. Clinicians should monitor for mental health changes during zonisamide treatment and consider drug discontinuation or substitution with alternative antiepileptics (e.g., sodium valproate) if psychiatric adverse effects emerge. Awareness of this association is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis and optimize epilepsy management.

[b00002] INTRODUCTION
Zonisamide, as a broad-spectrum antiepileptic drug, is widely used in clinical practice. However, the occurrence of psychiatric symptoms during treatment is not uncommon and is often overlooked. This report presents two cases of psychiatric symptoms caused by zonisamide treatment for epilepsy, with the aim of drawing the attention of clinicians.

[b00005] CASE / Case 1
A 25-year-old male patient presented to the outpatient department on March 21, 2024, with a history of “recurrent epileptic seizures for 20 years, abnormal behavior for 2 years, and recurrence for 8 days.” At 3 months of age, he developed hydrocephalus after trauma, followed by epileptic seizures characterized by generalized convulsions, jaw clenching, loss of consciousness, and post-ictal amnesia, with frequencies increasing to monthly episodes in adulthood. Diagnosed with “focal epilepsy, history of head trauma and hydrocephalus,” he was treated with sodium valproate sustained-release tablets and oxcarbazepine, reducing seizure frequency to every 2−3 months and allowing normal work (e.g., real estate sales).

[b00006] CASE / Case 1
First-onset psychiatric symptoms 2 years ago included food refusal, nocturnal insomnia, and wandering, managed with olanzapine 20 mg/day. Ten days prior to admission, his sodium valproate was switched to zonisamide at Peking Union Medical College Hospital’s epilepsy clinic. Eight days later, acute emotional-behavioral abnormalities emerged: refusal to work, chaotic lifestyle, food refusal, staring, mutism, lethargy, incoherent speech, self-talk, insomnia, irritability, aimless wandering, and violent resistance to family intervention. Admission diagnosis: “Epileptic psychosis.”

[b00007] CASE / Case 1
Mental examination revealed clear consciousness, angry affect, uncooperativeness, emotional incongruence, infantile behavior (incessant crying “Dad, save me”), delusional speech (“I am an atomic bomb; the world is peaceful”), and impulsive aggression without insight. Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score 94, Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) score 87. Key auxiliaries: head computed tomography (CT) showed brain atrophy; other labs (electrolytes, toxicology screen) were unremarkable.

[b00008] CASE / Case 1
Treatment course: Initial therapy included zonisamide 200 mg/day, oxcarbazepine 0.6 g twice daily (BID), and olanzapine 10 mg BID. On day 2, suspecting zonisamide-related psychosis, it was discontinued and replaced with sodium valproate sustained-release 1,500 mg/day for seizure control and mood stabilization, with diazepam added for agitation. Post-discontinuation, the patient gradually calmed: by day 4, he could answer simple questions; by day 6, olanzapine was reduced to 15 mg/day (due to somnolence) and later discharged after 1 month with resolved symptoms. PANSS = 7, BPRS = 5. Time table in Case 1 is shown in Figure 1.

[b00009] CASE / Case 2
A 48-year-old male police officer has suffered from epilepsy due to trauma 20 years ago, leading to delirium and excessive suspicion for 15 years. He has been treated with zonisamide for at least 5 years at the outpatient department of Southwest Hospital. The patient stopped taking zonisamide on his own for one week during the discharge period. His rambling talk decreased compared to before, but he had an epileptic seizure, with generalized convulsions at home. He then went to Southwest Hospital again to get zonisamide, and after treatment, there were no more convulsions, but he rambled all day long, with severe hallucinations and delusions, and his clothes were dirty and messy.

[b00010] CASE / Case 2
His psychiatric symptoms have never significantly improved, with delirium, prominent command hallucinations, persecutory delusions, erotic delusions, delusions of noble descent, and poor sleep. PANSS = 100, BPRS = 90. He has been repeatedly hospitalized at the Chongqing Mental Health Center, diagnosed with “1. Psychotic disorder due to trauma; 2. Epilepsy.”

[b00011] CASE / Case 2
He began treatment with risperidone oral solution 4 ml/day for psychosis, while continuing to take zonisamide as prescribed by Southwest Hospital for epilepsy. However, there was no significant improvement in his psychiatric symptoms. The dosage of risperidone was increased to 6 ml/day, and later combined with clozapine 150 mg/day for psychosis treatment, but there was still no significant improvement. After six months of hospitalization, the patient developed weakness in the lower limbs and general paralysis, which forced his discharge and transfer to Southwest Hospital for further treatment. [b00012] DISCUSSION
Zonisamide, full name is 1,2-benzisothiazole-3-methanesulfonamide, which is an antiepileptic drug mainly used as an adjunctive therapy for partial seizures in adults. It is currently the only antiepileptic drug on the market that can improve both early and late symptoms of Parkinson’s disease. It was first approved for clinical use in Japan in 1989, and subsequently approved in countries such as South Korea, the United States, and Europe for the adjunctive treatment of partial seizures in adults. The mechanism of action of zonisamide in treating epilepsy is not fully understood, but it may function by acting on sodium and calcium ion channels and has a weak carbonic anhydrase inhibitor effect []. In addition to adjunctive treatment for epilepsy, studies have also shown that zonisamide can be used for off-label indications such as migraine, neuropathic pain, obesity, and Parkinson’s disease [].

[b00013] DISCUSSION
Its primary mechanism of action is related to the inhibition of sustained high-frequency repetitive firing of action potentials dependent on Na⁺ and the reduction of T-type calcium channels. It has been established that antiepileptic drugs that act on sodium channels and T-type calcium channels are effective against simple, partial, generalized tonic-clonic, and absence seizures [,]. Therefore, the pharmacological activity of zonisamide at these sites can predict a similar broad spectrum of activity, which is also supported clinically. Zonisamide also alters the metabolism of dopamine, serotonin, and acetylcholine, shown as Figure 3. Compared to other antiepileptic drugs, zonisamide has no significant effect on GABA function []. Administration of zonisamide is associated with the release of glutamate, and these effects may be indirect, mediated by its actions on voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels.

[b00014] DISCUSSION
Zonisamide may activate the DA pathway by activating the striatal serotoninergic system and inhibiting the overactive direct pathway medium spiny neurons through serotonin 5-HT_(1B) receptors. The effects of zonisamide may also involve the inhibition of voltage-dependent sodium channels and T-type calcium channels, reducing depolarization-induced glutamate release, enhancing γ-aminobutyric acid and monoaminergic transmission [-]. These mechanisms of action can induce or even exacerbate psychiatric symptoms. Studies have confirmed that zonisamide at doses exceeding 100 mg/day has significant side effects, mainly including movement disorders, decreased appetite, somnolence, hallucinations, etc. [].

[b00015] DISCUSSION
The first patient experienced abnormal mental behaviors such as not eating, not sleeping at night, and running outside two years ago. Symptoms were alleviated after treatment with 20 mg/day of olanzapine. However, after switching to zonisamide 10+ days before admission, the patient developed significant mental symptoms and behavioral abnormalities, including emotional instability, impulsiveness, irritability, and unpredictable crying and laughing. On the second day of hospitalization, zonisamide was replaced with sodium valproate sustained-release tablets for antiepileptic treatment, after which the patient’s mental symptoms disappeared and there were no further epileptic seizures. The mental symptoms were likely caused by zonisamide. Differential diagnosis: (1) mental disorders caused by psychoactive substances: the onset of mental symptoms caused by psychoactive substances is closely related to the use of drugs in time. Before taking the medication, the patient’s mental state was normal. Both the patient and family members denied any history of psychoactive substance use, and there were no corresponding psychological or physical dependence symptoms in the clinical setting. Drug abuse screening can be performed to rule out this disease. (2) Schizophrenia: The patient exhibited bizarre behaviors such as disorganized thinking, muttering to oneself, and wandering around, which require consideration of this disease. However, given the patient’s history of epilepsy and the history of antiepileptic medication adjustment before this episode, this diagnosis is not considered. In addition, the patient’s syphilis/human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), thyroid function, lipid profile, cardiac injury markers, glucose, liver function, tumor markers, and substance abuse screening were all within normal limits. Head CT showed no significant abnormalities. This excludes infectious (e.g., syphilis/HIV-induced mental disorders), metabolic (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, diabetes-induced mental disorders), and structural (e.g., space-occupying lesions) causes of mental disorders.

[b00016] DISCUSSION
The second patient developed epilepsy due to trauma and had been taking zonisamide for years as an antiepileptic treatment. Despite the fact that zonisamide can cause hallucinations, delusions, and other psychiatric symptoms, it was almost never discontinued. Differential diagnosis: (1) epileptic psychosis: the patient’s psychiatric symptoms are secondary to trauma. Although epilepsy and psychiatric symptoms coexist, the psychiatric symptoms are not secondary to epilepsy, so this diagnosis is excluded. (2) Primary psychosis: the patient’s psychiatric symptoms are clearly secondary to trauma, which does not conform to the characteristics of primary psychosis (i.e., having no clear etiology or not being secondary to physical diseases/trauma), so this diagnosis is excluded. Even after increasing the dosage of risperidone to 6 ml/day and combining it with 150 mg/day of clozapine for antipsychotic treatment, the patient still experienced auditory hallucinations and delirious speech. Due to physical symptoms, the patient was reluctantly discharged from the hospital, and all of these issues were related to the psychiatric symptoms caused by zonisamide as an antiepileptic medication.

[b00017] DISCUSSION
It is evident that individuals with abnormal mental behavior should use zonisamide for epilepsy treatment with caution. It has been reported in the literature that approximately 2% of patients develop psychotic symptoms (such as hallucinations and delusions) after taking zonisamide []. The truly novel aspects of these two cases lie in the rapid onset of Case 1 and the drug resistance observed in Case 2. If it is found that someone is already on zonisamide treatment for epilepsy and exhibits mental symptoms, it is advisable to discontinue zonisamide and switch to an alternative antiepileptic drug for the patient’s benefit. In summary, zonisamide can cause adverse drug reactions that lead to mental symptoms, and it is hoped that clinicians will pay attention to this.
